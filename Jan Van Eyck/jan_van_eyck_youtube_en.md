## Video Positioning

- **Suggested length:** 17–19 minutes
- **Central story:** Jan van Eyck did not invent oil painting, but he made oil paint preserve light, texture, power, faith, and the human gaze with unprecedented force.
- **Audience:** Intelligent general viewers with little or no art-history background.
- **Editorial tone:** Clear, immersive, and curious; documented facts, plausible interpretations, and popular legends remain visibly distinct.
- **Channel:** Our Famous Artists

## Full Video Structure, Narration, and Visual Direction

### 1. Opening: The Eye That Never Blinks (00:00–00:55)

**Purpose:** Create curiosity through details and unanswered questions.  
**Key message:** Van Eyck’s details are not decoration; they position us inside the painted world.  
**Visuals:** Cut between the convex mirror, the Lamb’s frontal eyes, the red-turbaned sitter, rosary shadows, polished armor, and tiny figures on a distant bridge.

**Narration:**

A convex mirror only a few centimeters wide contains an entire room—and two more people standing in a doorway. A Lamb faces us from an altar while its blood flows into a chalice. Beneath a red headdress, a man refuses to look away, as if he is waiting for us to inspect every wrinkle, every hair, and every layer of light.

These images were painted by Jan van Eyck in the fifteenth century. Later viewers have described him as possessing an “eye that never blinked.” It is a metaphor for his astonishing powers of observation, not a phrase recorded from the artist himself.

Yet van Eyck’s achievement was not the mechanical copying of everything he saw. He selected, moved, resized, and transformed reality until an invented world became completely persuasive. Through twelve essential works, we will see how he made oil paint hold light—and why his perfect details still refuse to give us only one answer.

### 2. When History First Finds Him, He Is Already a Master (00:55–01:55)

**Purpose:** Establish the documented life and its gaps.  
**Key message:** Van Eyck’s origins and training remain uncertain; dependable professional records begin in 1422.  
**Visuals:** Map of the Low Countries; illuminate The Hague, Lille, Bruges, and Ghent; layer archival handwriting over the map.

**Narration:**

We do not possess a secure record of van Eyck’s birth year, birthplace, parents, or apprenticeship. Tradition connects him with Maaseik in present-day Belgium and a birth sometime around 1380 to 1390, but that is not the same thing as a surviving birth certificate.

His name first appears reliably in court records in The Hague in 1422. By then, he was already directing assistants on palace decorations. The first time history clearly sees him, he is not a student but an established professional.

In 1425, he entered the service of Philip the Good, Duke of Burgundy, as both court painter and *valet de chambre*. That position brought him into the center of diplomacy, political display, and international culture.

### 3. The Painter as Diplomatic Agent (01:55–02:55)

**Purpose:** Explain the political function of a realistic portrait.  
**Key message:** The Portuguese mission of 1428–29 turned portraiture into a medium of dynastic evaluation.  
**Visuals:** Route from Bruges to Lisbon; later copies associated with Isabella of Portugal; Burgundian textiles and heraldry.

**Narration:**

In 1428, van Eyck traveled with a Burgundian embassy to Portugal. He was probably asked to paint Isabella of Portugal, whom Philip was considering as his future bride. The original portraits are lost, so we should not pretend to know exactly what they looked like.

Nor should we reduce the episode to the romantic claim that a single painting caused a royal marriage. Dynasty, diplomacy, and political advantage all mattered. The portrait’s role was to transform a distant candidate into a credible image that could be viewed and assessed. Here, realism was not merely beautiful. It carried political trust.

The Burgundian court surrounded itself with velvet, jewels, metalwork, fur, illuminated manuscripts, and ceremony. Van Eyck would later make each of those surfaces catch light differently, turning material splendor into both religious and political language.

### 4. He Did Not Invent Oil Painting (02:55–04:05)

**Purpose:** Correct the most famous myth about the artist.  
**Key message:** Oil-based media already existed; van Eyck transformed their use through glazing, reflection, and precise control.  
**Visuals:** Simple diagram of translucent paint layers; compare skin, gems, fur, glass, and brass.

**Narration:**

For centuries, Jan van Eyck was celebrated as the inventor of oil painting. But European artists had used oil-based media before him. A more accurate claim is that he reorganized existing techniques and revealed an unprecedented range of optical possibilities.

Transparent and semi-transparent layers could be built one over another. Light entered those layers and reflected from a brighter ground beneath. Red appeared to glow from within; skin gained depth; metal, glass, fur, and jewels no longer shared a single generic shine.

Technique, however, is not a secret recipe. Van Eyck’s judgment mattered most: where to use transparency, where to become opaque, where a single point of white would create a reflection, and where a shadow should allow an underlayer to remain visible. He did not simply accumulate detail. He controlled the speed of our attention.

### 5. The Ghent Altarpiece: A Universe That Opens (04:05–05:35)

**Purpose:** Introduce the artist’s most monumental project and its moving structure.  
**Key message:** Liturgical time transformed the restrained exterior into a dazzling vision of salvation.  
**Visuals:** Show the closed altarpiece, then animate the wings opening; move through the Annunciation, musical angels, Adam and Eve, and the central Lamb.

**Narration:**

Completed in 1432, the *Ghent Altarpiece* is not one static picture. It is a visual machine activated by the church calendar. When closed, it presents a restrained Annunciation, prophets, donors, and saints painted to resemble gray stone sculpture. On feast days, the wings opened and released color, brocade, jewels, naked bodies, singing angels, and an immense landscape.

At the center of the lower register, the Mystic Lamb stands on an altar as blood flows into a chalice. Apostles, prophets, martyrs, hermits, and pilgrims converge from every direction. Distant cities, hills, and fields do not behave like passive scenery; they organize the created world into a theater of redemption.

Here, detail becomes theology. Each plant, gem, curl of hair, and reflection proposes that matter is not opposed to the sacred. The visible world can become the medium through which sacred meaning appears.

### 6. Two Brothers, a Missing Panel, and an Ongoing Restoration (05:35–06:50)

**Purpose:** Explain authorship, theft, and the material history of the altarpiece.  
**Key message:** “Hubert began and Jan completed” is the safest summary; today’s object includes original paint, overpaint, a replacement panel, and conservation history.  
**Visuals:** Frame inscription; names of Hubert and Jan; archival image of the *Just Judges* and its replacement; before-and-after view of the Lamb’s face.

**Narration:**

An inscription on the frame says that Hubert van Eyck began the work and Jan completed it. But the inscription cannot assign every brushstroke. Recent technical study supports Hubert’s involvement in major planning and parts of the painting, while Jan completed and substantially revised the ensemble after Hubert died in 1426. Calling it Jan’s solitary miracle—or dividing every panel neatly between the brothers—goes beyond the evidence.

In 1934, the *Just Judges* panel was stolen. The original has never been recovered, and a replacement occupies its position today. During the Second World War, the altarpiece was looted by the Nazis and recovered from an Austrian salt mine in 1945.

A phased conservation campaign begun in 2012 has also revealed extensive later overpaint. As of September 2026, the final phase remains in progress. We are not looking at a surface sealed in 1432, but at a historical object shaped by devotion, war, crime, aging, and conservation decisions.

### 7. Portraits That Make the Frame Speak (06:50–08:10)

**Purpose:** Introduce gaze, commemoration, and artistic identity through three portraits.  
**Key message:** Van Eyck combined faces, writing, simulated stone, and signatures to manufacture presence.  
**Visuals:** *Léal Souvenir*, *Portrait of a Man (Self Portrait?)*, and *The Goldsmith Jan de Leeuw*; enlarge inscriptions, ring, and eyes.

**Narration:**

In the 1432 portrait known as *Léal Souvenir*, a man stands behind a simulated stone parapet. The word “Tymotheos” may be a name, but its function remains uncertain. The carved-looking letters are paint, and the wooden panel performs simultaneously as portrait, monument, and document—as if it could prove that this person once existed.

The following year, the sitter in *Portrait of a Man* confronts us even more directly. His red headdress becomes an architecture of folded light. On the original frame, van Eyck painted **ALS ICH KAN**, approximately “as well as I can,” a phrase that also plays on his name. Most scholars consider the sitter very likely to be the artist, but the museum retains a question mark because no inscription identifies him directly.

In the 1436 *Portrait of Jan de Leeuw*, the goldsmith holds a ring while the frame speaks in his voice, giving his name, birth date, and the year of the painting. The frame is no longer merely an edge. It is where sitter and artist speak together.

### 8. The Arnolfini Portrait: Resist the Rush to Decode (08:10–09:40)

**Purpose:** Demonstrate how to handle competing interpretations.  
**Key message:** This is not a proven wedding document; its mirror and inscription place us inside an uncertain event.  
**Visuals:** Full painting; dress folds, joined hands, chandelier, oranges, dog, shoes, rosary, convex mirror, and inscription.

**Narration:**

The 1434 *Arnolfini Portrait* was long described as a wedding and even as a visual marriage contract. A more careful account says that it probably portrays a couple associated with Giovanni Arnolfini, while the painting’s exact function remains disputed.

The woman’s rounded silhouette is not evidence of pregnancy. It is created by the fashionable mass of cloth she gathers in front of her. The dog may suggest fidelity, and the oranges may evoke wealth, luxury, or Eden. Yet a dog can also be a household animal, while oranges were costly imported goods. Religious, social, and material meanings can coexist without every object becoming a code with only one solution.

The convex mirror changes everything. It reflects the couple from behind, the rear of the room, and two figures in the doorway. Above it appears the statement, “Jan van Eyck was here, 1434.” We cannot prove that the red figure in the mirror is the painter. But van Eyck gives us an irresistible sensation: someone is standing outside the depicted couple—and that is exactly where we, the viewers, have been placed.

### 9. The Madonna of Chancellor Rolin: Power Sits Across from Heaven (09:40–10:45)

**Purpose:** Examine the overlap of private devotion and political self-presentation.  
**Key message:** Rolin and the Virgin appear at nearly equal scale while the miniature city expands private prayer into a world order.  
**Visuals:** Facing figures; three arches; small garden figures; bridge, river, city, and mountains; conservation comparison.

**Narration:**

Around 1430, van Eyck painted the Burgundian chancellor Nicolas Rolin kneeling before the Virgin and Child. Their bodies are nearly equal in scale, almost as if temporal power and heavenly authority were seated across the same table. The work may express private devotion, but it is also the self-presentation of a political figure who wishes to enter sacred order.

Beyond the arcade lies a miniature world of rivers, bridges, churches, vineyards, and mountains. Scholars do not agree that it represents one identifiable city. The two tiny figures in the garden are sometimes called van Eyck and an assistant, but the evidence cannot confirm that claim.

Research and conservation from 2021 to 2024 removed oxidized yellow varnish and returned clarity to the colors and distant landscape. Conservation does not simply make an old picture “new.” It shapes what twenty-first-century viewers are able to see of a fifteenth-century surface.

### 10. The Impossible Church: Natural and Symbolic Light (10:45–12:00)

**Purpose:** Compare *The Madonna in the Church* and *The Annunciation*.  
**Key message:** Credible architecture carries impossible scale, direction, and sacred time.  
**Visuals:** Place the works side by side; mark the Virgin’s scale, light direction, Romanesque and Gothic zones, and pavement imagery.

**Narration:**

*The Madonna in the Church* measures only about thirty-one by fourteen centimeters, yet Mary rises like a giant inside a Gothic nave. According to the building’s proportions, she could not possibly be that tall. This is not a simple perspective mistake. Mary stands within the church while also embodying the Church, so she is symbolically greater than the architecture around her.

In *The Annunciation*, painted around 1434–36, Gabriel and Mary also meet inside a church. Older Romanesque architecture and newer Gothic forms coexist. They have often been interpreted as a visual passage from Old Testament to New, from law toward grace.

The work was originally painted on a wooden panel and later transferred to canvas. Its medium today must therefore be described as oil on canvas transferred from panel. Both paintings show van Eyck using believable buildings to organize an invisible structure of faith.

### 11. The Madonna of Canon van der Paele: Old Age Enters Sacred Space (12:00–13:10)

**Purpose:** Show material description alongside an unidealized human body.  
**Key message:** Wrinkles, eyeglasses, fatigue, and physical vulnerability make the donor’s body part of the prayer.  
**Visuals:** Full painting; the canon’s face and spectacles; Saint George’s armor; vestments, carpet, gems, and fur.

**Narration:**

Painted from 1434 to 1436, *The Virgin and Child with Canon Joris van der Paele* is among van Eyck’s largest surviving single panels. The elderly canon is introduced to the Virgin through Saint George and Saint Donatian.

Van Eyck does not make him young. Loose skin, wrinkles, spectacles, and fatigue remain visible. This is not cruel observation. It gives the commission its religious urgency: age, illness, and the awareness of death are precisely why someone might seek intercession and salvation.

Now compare Saint George’s armor, gold-thread vestments, an imported carpet, gems, and fur. They do not all receive the same blanket highlight; each material responds to light differently. Claims that the painter can be identified in the tiny reflections of the armor go beyond what those marks can securely prove. What we can say is that van Eyck made unlike substances share one sacred presence.

### 12. A Universe Small Enough for Private Prayer (13:10–14:25)

**Purpose:** Introduce the *Dresden Triptych*, *Lucca Madonna*, and *Madonna at the Fountain*.  
**Key message:** As the object becomes smaller, the viewer’s relationship with it becomes more intimate.  
**Visuals:** Open each object; use a hand or book for scale; focus on nursing, flowing water, angel-held brocade, and the grisaille exterior.

**Narration:**

The 1437 *Dresden Triptych* compresses the opening and closing experience of a monumental altarpiece into a portable object. Closed, its wings present a gray Annunciation. Opened, it reveals the colored Virgin and Child, saints, and donor. Its viewing distance is closer to reading an illuminated manuscript than surveying a church altar.

In the *Lucca Madonna*, painted around 1437, Mary nurses the Christ Child inside a narrow room. She is Queen of Heaven and a physical mother at once. The throne resembles an altar, while brass, carpet, and window light return the sacred scene to the world of touch.

The 1439 *Madonna at the Fountain* is smaller still. Two angels hold brocade behind Mary in an enclosed garden while water moves through the foreground. Its original frame, signature, and date survive. These works are not reduced versions of larger masterpieces; they were designed for repeated, intimate, close-range attention.

### 13. The Most Intimate Face: Margareta van Eyck (14:25–15:20)

**Purpose:** Connect the private household to artistic identity.  
**Key message:** A specific face and an inscribed frame preserve personal presence without resolving emotion.  
**Visuals:** Full portrait; close views of the eyes, mouth, white headdress, and frame inscription.

**Narration:**

In 1439, van Eyck painted his wife, Margareta. There is no mirror, city, jewel, or elaborate room to distract us. There is only an asymmetrical face, a closed mouth, a white headdress, and a reserved gaze.

The frame records her name, age, date, and relationship to the painter. Later viewers sometimes turn her expression into a witty response to her husband, but emotion cannot be proven from a face alone. What matters is that van Eyck gives someone from his own household the same exacting presence usually reserved for courtiers, merchants, and sacred figures.

It is one of his major late signed works. Van Eyck died two years later, in 1441.

### 14. Realism Is Not a Photograph—It Is Designed Trust (15:20–16:35)

**Purpose:** Synthesize the artist’s style and offer a viewing method.  
**Key message:** Apparently natural worlds are built from multiple viewpoints, altered proportions, and revisions made during painting.  
**Visuals:** Compare underdrawing and final details in *The Arnolfini Portrait*; show multiple scales in the *Ghent Altarpiece* and proportion guides in *The Madonna in the Church*.

**Narration:**

Van Eyck’s worlds are so convincing that we may assume he simply copied what stood before him. Technical imaging tells a different story. In *The Arnolfini Portrait*, famous elements such as the dog, chandelier, rosary, and shoes were not all fixed in the earliest design. The room developed through revision. It is not a passive photograph but a carefully staged fiction.

The *Ghent Altarpiece* does not obey one consistent viewpoint. *The Madonna in the Church* uses impossible scale. The city behind Chancellor Rolin may combine different visual memories. Van Eyck’s realism is not perfect geometric consistency. It is the accumulation of enough persuasive local evidence that we trust the whole.

The best way to look is to step back and find the order, then move closer and compare materials. Look for mirrors, doors, windows, inscriptions, and frames to discover where the viewer has been placed. Finally, preserve what the picture does not answer.

### 15. Conclusion: Detail Is Not the Same as an Answer (16:35–17:35)

**Purpose:** Leave the viewer with a memorable way to look.  
**Key message:** Van Eyck thickens the visible world while protecting its mysteries.  
**Visuals:** Slow montage of the twelve works; end inside the mirror’s doorway, then dissolve to the eyes of the red-turbaned man.

**Narration:**

We live in a world where images pass across a screen in seconds. Van Eyck asks for the opposite speed: slow down, move closer, and then step back.

His details do not guarantee a single truth. Every object in a room may look credible, yet we may still not know what happened there. Every wrinkle can be preserved, yet we cannot know what a sitter truly thought. The Virgin can enter a specific church, yet the sacred does not become fully explainable.

Van Eyck did not seal the world onto wood exactly as he found it. He selected the light, texture, scale, and gaze that should remain, making reality denser than ordinary sight. When we approach the convex mirror, the picture gains more than two figures in a doorway. It gains us—viewers still trying to understand it six centuries later.

This is **Our Famous Artists**. Thank you for watching, and join us next time as we step closer to another work of art.

## Introductions to 12 Essential Artworks

> Dimensions follow commonly published image or object measurements. Polyptychs, works retaining original frames, and paintings transferred to a new support may be measured in different ways by different institutions.

### 1. *The Ghent Altarpiece / The Adoration of the Mystic Lamb*

- **Artist:** Hubert and Jan van Eyck; Hubert began and Jan completed the work, although their precise division of labor remains under study.
- **Date:** c. 1425–1432; completed in 1432.
- **Medium:** Oil on oak panels; multi-panel altarpiece.
- **Dimensions:** Approximately 340 × 460 cm when open; measurements vary with configuration.
- **Current location:** Saint Bavo’s Cathedral, Ghent, Belgium.
- **Introduction:** When closed, the altarpiece presents a restrained Annunciation and donor portraits. When opened, it becomes a universe of salvation centered on the Mystic Lamb. The original *Just Judges* panel, stolen in 1934, remains missing and is represented by a replacement.
- **What to notice:** The change in color between closed and open states, the Lamb’s frontal gaze, differentiated plants and materials, and the way multiple viewpoints construct theological order.

### 2. *Léal Souvenir (Portrait of a Man)*

- **Artist:** Jan van Eyck.
- **Date:** 1432.
- **Medium:** Oil on wood, probably oak.
- **Dimensions:** 33.3 × 18.9 cm.
- **Current location:** The National Gallery, London, United Kingdom.
- **Introduction:** The sitter appears behind a simulated stone parapet. “Tymotheos” may be a name, but it is not securely identified; the French phrase *Léal Souvenir* gives the image a commemorative character.
- **What to notice:** Painted imitation carving, the contrast between inscription and signature, and the way a portrait becomes both memorial and document.

### 3. *The Madonna in the Church*

- **Artist:** Jan van Eyck.
- **Date:** c. 1437–1440; earlier dates have appeared in older scholarship.
- **Medium:** Oil on oak panel.
- **Dimensions:** Approximately 31 × 14 cm.
- **Current location:** Gemäldegalerie, Berlin, Germany.
- **Introduction:** Mary appears at an impossible, monumental scale inside a Gothic church. She occupies the building while also embodying the Church itself.
- **What to notice:** The scale of figure to architecture, the direction of light, colored reflections, and the union of credible space with supernatural meaning.

### 4. *Portrait of a Man (Self Portrait?)*

- **Artist:** Jan van Eyck.
- **Date:** 1433.
- **Medium:** Oil on wood, probably oak.
- **Dimensions:** 26 × 19 cm.
- **Current location:** The National Gallery, London, United Kingdom.
- **Introduction:** A man confronts the viewer beneath a brilliantly folded red headdress. The original frame carries the motto **ALS ICH KAN**. He is very likely van Eyck, but no inscription directly confirms the sitter’s identity.
- **What to notice:** Eyes, stubble, asymmetrical skin, folds of red fabric, and the frame as a declaration of authorship.

### 5. *The Arnolfini Portrait*

- **Artist:** Jan van Eyck.
- **Date:** 1434.
- **Medium:** Oil on wood, probably oak.
- **Dimensions:** 82.2 × 60 cm.
- **Current location:** The National Gallery, London, United Kingdom.
- **Introduction:** A richly dressed couple stands in a Bruges interior, probably connected with Giovanni(?) Arnolfini. The work is not a proven wedding document, and the woman’s dress does not establish pregnancy.
- **What to notice:** The convex mirror and its ten Passion medallions, the unusual inscription, imported oranges, the dog, chandelier, and details added during the painting process.

### 6. *The Annunciation*

- **Artist:** Jan van Eyck.
- **Date:** c. 1434–1436.
- **Medium:** Oil on canvas transferred from panel.
- **Dimensions:** Painted surface 90.2 × 34.1 cm; support 92.7 × 36.7 cm.
- **Current location:** National Gallery of Art, Washington, D.C., United States.
- **Introduction:** Gabriel and Mary meet inside a church. The painting may once have belonged to a larger ensemble, but its original format is not securely known; it should not be presented as the confirmed wing of a specific surviving triptych.
- **What to notice:** Romanesque and Gothic architecture, pavement imagery, rays carrying the Holy Spirit, and Mary’s golden response written upside down toward God.

### 7. *The Madonna of Chancellor Rolin*

- **Artist:** Jan van Eyck.
- **Date:** c. 1430; some scholarship uses c. 1430–1435.
- **Medium:** Oil on oak panel.
- **Dimensions:** Approximately 66 × 62 cm.
- **Current location:** Musée du Louvre, Paris, France.
- **Introduction:** Burgundian chancellor Nicolas Rolin faces the Virgin and Child at nearly equal scale while a miniature city unfolds beyond the arcade. The work underwent major research and conservation from 2021 to 2024.
- **What to notice:** Scale between donor and sacred figures, the three arches, small garden figures, river city and distant mountains, and the overlap of political power with private devotion.

### 8. *The Virgin and Child with Canon Joris van der Paele*

- **Artist:** Jan van Eyck.
- **Date:** 1434–1436; the original frame records the foundation and completion years.
- **Medium:** Oil on oak panel.
- **Dimensions:** Panel approximately 122 × 157 cm; approximately 141 × 176.5 cm with the original frame. Published measurements vary according to the object measured.
- **Current location:** Groeningemuseum, Bruges, Belgium.
- **Introduction:** The elderly canon is presented to the Virgin through Saint George and Saint Donatian. His aging body remains specific rather than idealized.
- **What to notice:** Spectacles and skin, armor reflections, gold-thread vestments, fur, carpet, and stone—each responding differently to light.

### 9. *The Dresden Triptych*

- **Artist:** Jan van Eyck.
- **Date:** 1437.
- **Medium:** Oil on oak panels; portable triptych.
- **Dimensions:** Approximately 33 × 27.5 × 5 cm when closed; opened width depends on measurement convention.
- **Current location:** Gemäldegalerie Alte Meister, Dresden, Germany.
- **Introduction:** The Virgin and Child occupy the center, with Saint Michael, Saint Catherine, and a donor on the wings. When closed, the exterior presents a grisaille Annunciation.
- **What to notice:** Portable scale, opening ritual, the shift from gray exterior to colored interior, and deep architecture within a tiny object.

### 10. *The Lucca Madonna*

- **Artist:** Jan van Eyck.
- **Date:** c. 1437.
- **Medium:** Oil and tempera layers on oak panel; the museum also describes it broadly as mixed technique on oak.
- **Dimensions:** Approximately 65.7 × 49.6 cm.
- **Current location:** Städel Museum, Frankfurt, Germany.
- **Introduction:** Mary nurses the Christ Child in a narrow room. Her throne evokes an altar while household objects make the sacred scene physically intimate.
- **What to notice:** Nursing gesture, lion-shaped armrests, imported carpet, brass vessel, window light, and the coexistence of motherhood and theological symbolism.

### 11. *Madonna at the Fountain*

- **Artist:** Jan van Eyck.
- **Date:** 1439.
- **Medium:** Oil on oak panel with original frame.
- **Dimensions:** Approximately 19 × 12 cm.
- **Current location:** Royal Museum of Fine Arts Antwerp, Belgium.
- **Introduction:** Two angels hold brocade behind the Virgin in an enclosed garden while a fountain flows in the foreground, evoking life, purity, and paradise.
- **What to notice:** Miniature scale, water, garden, brocade pattern, and the signature and date on the original frame.

### 12. *Portrait of Margareta van Eyck*

- **Artist:** Jan van Eyck.
- **Date:** 1439.
- **Medium:** Oil on panel.
- **Dimensions:** Panel approximately 32.6 × 25.8 cm; framed dimensions are larger.
- **Current location:** Groeningemuseum, Bruges, Belgium.
- **Introduction:** The artist’s wife turns toward the viewer in a rare and highly specific fifteenth-century private portrait. The original frame records her name, age, date, and relationship to the painter.
- **What to notice:** Facial asymmetry, white headdress, restrained expression, frame inscription, and the entry of private relationship into the artist’s system of authorship.

## Bonus Artwork: *The Goldsmith Jan de Leeuw*

- **Artist:** Jan van Eyck.
- **Date:** 1436.
- **Medium and dimensions:** Oil on panel, approximately 24.6 × 19.2 cm.
- **Current location:** Kunsthistorisches Museum, Vienna, Austria.
- **Introduction:** The sitter holds a ring while the original frame speaks in the first person, giving his name, birth date, and the year of the portrait. It is a key example of van Eyck joining portraiture, inscription, and artistic self-awareness.

## YouTube Description

Did Jan van Eyck really invent oil painting? Is *The Arnolfini Portrait* a wedding document? And is the man in the red headdress truly the artist himself?

In this episode of **Our Famous Artists**, a convex mirror, the Mystic Lamb, a red headdress, and a nearly invisible ray of light lead us into van Eyck’s world. We explore his court and diplomatic career, his transformative use of oil paint, the opening structure and conservation history of the *Ghent Altarpiece*, and twelve essential works.

The video also separates documented history from interpretation and legend. Van Eyck did not invent oil painting; the wedding and pregnancy readings of the *Arnolfini Portrait* are not established facts; and the *Ghent Altarpiece* belongs to the intertwined history of Hubert, Jan, and their workshop.

## Historical and Production Notes

- Do not call van Eyck the “inventor of oil painting.” Explain that he transformed the optical and material potential of an existing medium.
- Do not present *The Arnolfini Portrait* as a proven wedding scene or the woman’s silhouette as proof of pregnancy.
- Treat *Portrait of a Man (Self Portrait?)* as very likely, but not conclusively identified, as a self-portrait.
- “Hubert began, Jan completed” is the safest summary of the *Ghent Altarpiece*; precise authorship remains debated.
- *The Annunciation* is now oil on canvas transferred from panel; its original ensemble is uncertain.
- The *Just Judges* currently seen is a replacement. The original panel stolen in 1934 remains missing.
- As of September 2026, the third phase of the *Ghent Altarpiece* conservation campaign remains in progress. Recheck its status if the video is released later.
- Confirm the usage license and photographic rights on every museum or Wikimedia download page before production. The ability to view a high-resolution image does not automatically grant commercial reuse.
