# Hieronymus Bosch, The Garden of Earthly Delights — YouTube Artwork Contents

Purpose: A source-grounded planning document for an AI- or NotebookLM-assisted art-education video centered on one artwork. This is not a finished voice-over, subtitle file, or slide deck.
Research updated: September 3, 2026

## A. Video Core Narrative

Opening hook

Close The Garden of Earthly Delights and all we see is a quiet, gray-green world before humankind. Open its wings and a surface nearly four meters wide erupts with naked bodies, oversized fruit, impossible animals, musical instruments, and punishments. Bosch's deepest puzzle is not simply what each monster means. It is this: if the triptych warns against sensual excess, why must temptation look so beautiful?

Central question

Is the center panel an innocent world untouched by the Fall, a sensual feast on the road to Hell, or a visual trap designed to make courtly viewers debate where one becomes the other?

Story promise

The video follows the triptych as an unfolding experience: from the still Creation on the closed wings to the first charged gaze in Eden, the multiplying desires of the center, and the “musical Hell” in which the tools of pleasure turn against their users. Underdrawing, provenance records, and early commentary will help separate documented fact from scholarly interpretation, contested hypothesis, and visual observation. The final segment offers a practical way to look again: rather than decoding every object from a symbol dictionary, follow how the same kinds of things move from beauty and pleasure to control and punishment.

## E. Detailed Segment Plan

## Segment 1 — A Visual Explosion Behind Two Doors

**Narrative Purpose**

Begin with the viewer's original experience of closing and opening the object, not with the artist's birth. The sudden changes in color, implied sound, density, and scale establish the central question: why is temptation so beautiful?

**Viewer Question**

If the center is a warning, why did Bosch first make it resemble a dream of paradise?

**Content**

Do not begin with the famous center. Hold on the closed wings. A gray-green world sits inside a transparent sphere. Land, water, and vegetation exist, but human beings, the sun, and the moon do not. God is remarkably small in the upper left. Across the wings runs the Latin Ipse dixit, et facta sunt; ipse mandavit, et creata sunt, echoing Psalm 33:9. The exterior is generally understood as the third day of Creation, but this is an art-historical reading, not a caption written by Bosch.

Now simulate the opening. Gray-green stillness gives way to pink, blue, fresh green, flesh, and fire. The left wing shows God, Adam, and Eve. The center contains gigantic fruit, birds, riders, glass-like spheres, and bodily play. The right wing ends in night, flame, ice, and punishment. Opening the object is itself a narrative event; a flat digital image of all three interior panels cannot reproduce it.

The triptych borrows a format associated with sacred art, yet its interior lacks the familiar holy focus that would normally stabilize the viewer. Its largest field is an ambiguous spectacle of pleasure. We do not stand safely outside temptation. The object first draws us in.

**What to Show**

- The complete closed exterior, moving toward the transparent world, tiny Creator, and Latin inscription.
- An opening-wing animation followed by the full interior and its color contrast.
- A scale graphic relating the panels and tiny figures to the triptych's nearly four-meter open span.
- Authoritative Prado or BRCP imagery, with reproduction terms checked before publication.

**Key Facts and Interpretations**

- Fact: This is a closing triptych painted in oil on oak, with a grisaille exterior. Source: Museo Nacional del Prado — https://www.museodelprado.es/en/the-collection/art-work/the-garden-of-earthly-delights-triptych/02388242-6d6a-4e9e-a992-e1311eab3609
- Fact: The image height is 185.8 cm; the center is 172.5 cm wide and each wing 76.5 cm wide. Source: same Prado record.
- Visual observation: The sparse exterior and transparent sphere sharply oppose the crowded, brightly colored interior.
- Interpretation: The exterior is conventionally read as the third day of Creation; the fragile globe can also serve as a formal motif connecting the outside to the transparent enclosures within.
- Disputed / unknown: We do not know when the wings were opened in their original courtly setting and should not impose a church altarpiece's liturgical schedule on the object.

**Transition**

Why did an object with the form of an altarpiece first appear in an aristocratic palace? The answer begins with Bosch's city, religious network, and probable patron.

## Segment 2 — A Sacred Form in a Secular Palace

**Narrative Purpose**

Use only the biography that helps explain the triptych: the family workshop, the Brotherhood of Our Lady, Bosch's courtly reputation, and the Nassau provenance.

**Viewer Question**

Was Bosch an isolated heretic-genius, or a respected professional who knew religious and courtly conventions well enough to unsettle them?

**Content**

Bosch was born Jheronimus van Aken around 1450 in 's-Hertogenbosch in the Duchy of Brabant. His grandfather, father, and other male relatives were connected to painting. No apprenticeship contract survives, but the family workshop is the most reasonable setting for his training in panel painting. By signing with an abbreviated form of his hometown, “Bosch,” he turned a local identity into a personal reputation.

His marriage brought greater property and social stability. He joined the Brotherhood of Our Lady in 1486/87 and entered its smaller circle of sworn members the following year. The records place him within the city's religious and elite networks, not outside society as a hallucinating recluse. In 1504 Philip the Fair commissioned a large Last Judgment from him. That commission cannot be securely identified with a surviving work, but it proves Bosch had reached a courtly level of recognition.

The original title, contract, and first intended setting of The Garden are unknown. Using a 1490s date and the early provenance, the Prado identifies Engelbert II of Nassau as the patron. BRCP's later date of 1501–1505 reminds us that dating and patronage arguments affect one another. What is secure is that Antonio de Beatis saw the triptych in the Nassau Palace in Brussels in 1517. It was already a high-status object in a secular courtly interior.

That audience knew scripture, love literature, proverbs, exotic animals, and visual wit. Guests could have been entertained while debating sex, marriage, generation, sin, and salvation. Entertainment and warning need not be opposites.

**What to Show**

- A historical map of 's-Hertogenbosch, St. John's Cathedral, and Brotherhood records.
- Bosch's signature; do not use later portraits as exact evidence of his appearance.
- Historical imagery of the Nassau or Coudenberg palace, labeling reconstructions as such.
- Visualized excerpts from the 1504 commission and the 1517 travel account.

**Key Facts and Interpretations**

- Fact: Bosch came from a family of painters, joined the Brotherhood of Our Lady, and received a court commission in 1504. Sources: Museo Nacional del Prado, Hieronymus Bosch — https://www.museodelprado.es/en/the-collection/artist/hieronymus-bosch/c9716e4a-4c24-44dd-ac65-44bc4661c8b5 | BoschDoc — https://www.huygens.knaw.nl/en/resources/boschdoc-2/
- Fact: The 1517 travel account is the earliest secure textual witness to the triptych and places it in the Nassau Palace in Brussels. Source: Prado object record — https://www.museodelprado.es/en/the-collection/art-work/the-garden-of-earthly-delights-triptych/02388242-6d6a-4e9e-a992-e1311eab3609
- Interpretation: The triptych may have functioned as a “conversation piece” through which educated court viewers identified, argued, and displayed knowledge.
- Disputed / unknown: The original title, exact year, contract, first setting, and precise function are unknown. The proposed patron is a museum conclusion, not a surviving contractual fact.

**Transition**

The object returns its viewers to the beginning of the human story, but Bosch's Eden is not entirely stable. Its first fissure appears in Adam's gaze at Eve.

## Segment 3 — The First Gaze in Eden

**Narrative Purpose**

Show how the left wing establishes both sacred order and signs of instability, making the center feel like an expansion rather than an unrelated fantasy.

**Viewer Question**

Why are predation, hybrid creatures, and an ambiguous gaze already present before the Fall?

**Content**

In the foreground, God appears young and Christ-like. He holds Eve by the wrist and presents her to the reclining Adam. Hands, eyes, and postures form a chain of relationships. Adam looks up at Eve. That glance might suggest surprise, recognition, or the beginning of desire. The video should not diagnose him; it should show how Bosch makes looking itself an event.

At the center of the landscape, a pink Fountain of Life combines plant, mineral, architecture, and body-like form. An owl sits in an opening at its base. In medieval contexts an owl could suggest darkness, blindness, evil, or silence, but it had no single meaning valid in every image. Nearby are an elephant, giraffe, lion, unicorn-like creature, strange fish, and many birds. Predation is already visible. If this is a perfect Eden, why is death present? Bosch does not resolve the problem.

The point is not to assign one sin to every animal. It is to notice the structure: God anchors the union of man and woman, and the fountain anchors the landscape, yet instability hides at the center and edges of both. The center panel will expand these fissures into an entire human world.

**What to Show**

- The whole left wing, then the linked gestures and gazes of God, Eve, and Adam.
- Close views of the Fountain of Life and the owl.
- Predation, the giraffe, elephant, and imagined animals.
- Only authenticated technical comparisons; never imitate infrared reflectography with a filter.

**Key Facts and Interpretations**

- Fact: The left foreground shows God presenting Eve to Adam. Source: Museo Nacional del Prado — https://www.museodelprado.es/en/the-collection/art-work/the-garden-of-earthly-delights-triptych/02388242-6d6a-4e9e-a992-e1311eab3609
- Visual observation: Adam looks at Eve; an owl occupies the fountain; predation and hybrid life appear in Eden.
- Interpretation: Adam's gaze can function as the narrative origin of the center panel's cycles of desire.
- Disputed / unknown: The owl, fountain, and animals have no single fixed code. Adam's expression cannot be established as lust.

**Transition**

When one couple becomes hundreds of bodies in the center panel, a first gaze becomes a pursuit that never seems to stop.

## Segment 4 — A Garden Beautiful Enough to Lower Our Guard

**Narrative Purpose**

Slow down in the center instead of announcing its meaning. Build the argument through scale, circular motion, fruit, and fragile enclosures.

**Viewer Question**

The people in the center appear free of pain. On what grounds do we call their world sinful rather than innocent?

**Content**

The center continues the left wing's horizon, blue water, and strange rock formations, as if Eden has grown to the right. But God has vanished. Almost everyone is young and naked. There are no obvious elderly people, children, workplaces, illnesses, permanent houses, or stable institutions. Figures pick, eat, carry, or hide inside enlarged fruit; they touch, bathe, ride animals, and insert themselves into glass, shells, and plant husks.

First, look at reversed scale. Berries can contain or overwhelm a human body. Early inventories and José de Sigüenza's later account used madroño, the strawberry tree, in naming the object, so fruit was prominent in early reception. But the claim that a strawberry always and only meant lust is too rigid.

Second, look at cycles. Around a circular pool, men ride horses, camels, cattle, pigs, and imaginary beasts. Riding suggests control, yet the endless circular procession also suggests that instinct carries the riders. Third, look at fragile shelters. Transparent globes, tubes, membranes, skins, and shells seem to protect intimacy, but none could last.

The panel includes people with different skin colors and others whose bodies are covered with hair. These figures may intersect with ideas about foreign peoples, primeval humanity, European “wild men,” and an expanding geographical imagination. They should not be converted into a modern celebration of diversity, nor assigned precise identities without focused scholarship.

End on the man at the lower right who looks out of the painting. He has been proposed as Adam, Bosch, the patron, or another witness, but none is secure. His glance nevertheless reverses the viewing relation: while we inspect the desires of others, someone inside the picture seems to notice us.

**What to Show**

- The full center, with three successive moves toward reversed scale, circular riding, and fragile enclosures.
- Oversized fruit, the couple in a transparent sphere, the pool, and the mounted procession.
- Dark-skinned and hairy figures shown in their full compositional context before close-up.
- The outward-looking man at lower right, held long enough to register his return gaze.

**Key Facts and Interpretations**

- Fact: The 1593 inventory and Sigüenza's account made madroño fruit an important early naming and viewing cue. Source: Museo Nacional del Prado — https://www.museodelprado.es/en/the-collection/art-work/the-garden-of-earthly-delights-triptych/02388242-6d6a-4e9e-a992-e1311eab3609
- Visual observation: Most figures are young and nude; fruit, birds, and containers exceed ordinary scale; riders circle a pool; fragile transparent forms recur.
- Interpretation: Oversized fruit can suggest sensual attraction and transience; circular motion can suggest repetitive desire; thin enclosures can suggest fragile pleasure and intimacy.
- Disputed / unknown: The center may show sinful indulgence, the world before the Flood, humanity without the Fall, or several courtly topics at once. There is no consensus.
- Disputed / unknown: The outward-looking man is unidentified and is not a confirmed Bosch self-portrait.

**Transition**

This world can look like a dream conceived whole. The underdrawing tells a different story: Bosch continued to revise and invent on the panel.

## Segment 5 — How a Monster Was Made

**Narrative Purpose**

Make technical evidence part of the narrative. Technique is not a materials list; it explains why impossible beings feel materially persuasive.

**Viewer Question**

Did Bosch begin with a complete code of monsters, or did he invent while drawing, painting, erasing, and revising?

**Content**

The triptych is painted on oak. A prepared ground received an underdrawing, over which oil paint established color, texture, and light. Netherlandish painters could make metal, glass, feathers, skin, fruit, and water feel tangible. Bosch used that descriptive power to recombine recognizable organs, animals, and objects. His monsters convince us not because they are vague, but because their parts appear real.

Since 2010, BRCP has studied Bosch's works through dendrochronology, infrared reflectography, high-resolution macrophotography, and related methods. The Garden's underdrawing differs from the painted surface in poses, contours, and invented forms. A man in a basket appears in the underdrawing of the Hell wing but not in the final paint; the Prado has also noted elements removed while developing the center. The overall design may have been planned, while local invention remained open. Monsters grew through sketching, covering, altering, and recombining.

Attribution to Bosch is secure, but ordinary workshop practice does not prove that he personally executed every centimeter, nor does it let us assign arbitrary passages to assistants. The cautious formulation is that the object embodies Bosch's recognizable design and painting practice, while the precise extent of assistance remains unresolved.

**What to Show**

- A concise diagram of oak support, ground, underdrawing, and oil layers.
- Visible-light and infrared-reflectography images of the same detail.
- Extreme details of feathers, glass, flesh, metal, and fruit.
- BRCP research imagery with its source identification intact.

**Key Facts and Interpretations**

- Fact: The medium is oil on oak; the project has been examined with dendrochronology, infrared reflectography, and high-resolution imaging. Sources: Museo Nacional del Prado — https://www.museodelprado.es/en/the-collection/art-work/the-garden-of-earthly-delights-triptych/02388242-6d6a-4e9e-a992-e1311eab3609 | BRCP — https://boschproject.org/
- Fact: Underdrawing and final paint differ, showing deletion and revision. Source: Museo Nacional del Prado research news — https://www.museodelprado.es/en/whats-on/new/the-museo-del-prado-and-fundacion-bbva-are/33d168da-53b2-40eb-bbb6-1a187106c5ac
- Visual observation: Hybrid beings combine human anatomy, birds, fish, plants, containers, and tools.
- Interpretation: The revisions suggest that local inventions developed during the working process rather than being mechanically transferred from one fixed design.
- Disputed / unknown: Research cannot map workshop assistance precisely. Dendrochronology gives an earliest possible use date for wood, not the painting date itself.

**Transition**

On the right wing, recombination becomes cruel: music, food, animals, and tools once available for human pleasure begin to control the human body.

## Segment 6 — When Instruments Begin to Play People

**Narrative Purpose**

Present Hell as an inversion of the center, not a catalog of monsters. Follow recurring objects as users become the used.

**Viewer Question**

Why are Hell's instruments of punishment familiar things—music, dice, drink, contracts, and ordinary animals?

**Content**

Color first severs Hell from the garden. Fresh green and daylight become brown-black darkness, icy blue, and a burning city. Yet the foreground is not an abstract sea of flame. It is a torture ground built from the remains of human culture. A sinner is stretched across harp strings; another lies beneath a lute. Drums, pipes, and bagpipes no longer serve dancing or feasting. Bodies become material for instruments. The music written on a man's buttocks has been transcribed and performed online, but within the panel it first operates as an inversion: humiliation replaces harmony.

Dice, cards, gaming boards, and vessels turn leisure into punishment. A pig dressed as a nun pressures a man to sign a document, fusing religious costume, contract, appetite, and bodily violation. A rabbit carries away a human: prey has become hunter. Giant ears pierced by a blade and arrow turn a sensory organ into a violent machine. The M on the blade may be a maker's mark, an initial, or something else; no explanation is secure.

The Tree-Man combines person, dead tree, eggshell, tavern, boats, and landscape. His pale face has often been proposed as Bosch's, without decisive comparative evidence. A bird-headed monster on a commode-like throne swallows people and excretes them, making gluttony, power, and endless consumption into one grotesque machine. “Prince of Hell” is a modern convenience, not Bosch's title.

The shared structure matters more than assigning one sin to each detail. Sensations, goods, and animals pursued in the center acquire agency in Hell. Food ceases to nourish, music ceases to harmonize, contracts cease to protect order, and bodies cease to belong to themselves. Hell looks like pleasure internally transformed after losing all measure.

**What to Show**

- Match cuts from fruit, animals, and sensual play in the center to instruments and monsters on the right.
- The musical Hell, buttocks score, giant ears, and M-marked blade.
- A layered reading of the Tree-Man's face, shell body, tavern interior, and boat-like feet.
- The bird-headed monster and nun-pig in their larger compositional setting.

**Key Facts and Interpretations**

- Visual observation: Punishments incorporate musical and gaming instruments, documents, animals, sense organs, and human bodies.
- Interpretation: The right wing can be read as the center inverted: tools used by people now use people.
- Interpretation: This accords with the Prado's reading of humanity ruled by sensual pleasure and moving toward ruin. Source: Museo Nacional del Prado — https://www.museodelprado.es/en/the-collection/art-work/the-garden-of-earthly-delights-triptych/02388242-6d6a-4e9e-a992-e1311eab3609
- Disputed / unknown: The Tree-Man is not a confirmed self-portrait; the M on the blade remains unexplained; “Prince of Hell” is not a historical title.

**Transition**

If Hell so clearly represents disorder, must the center represent sin? Five centuries of disagreement reopen what looks like an easy answer.

## Segment 7 — A Moral Machine Without One Answer

**Narrative Purpose**

Compare major interpretations fairly. Uncertainty is not failed research; it is part of the work's meaningful viewing condition.

**Viewer Question**

Is there one key capable of unlocking every fruit, body, and monster?

**Content**

The oldest enduring approach is moral warning. The left establishes creation and the union of man and woman; the center expands sex, food, and sensual pleasure into endless play; the right shows disorder's consequences. Around 1605, El Escorial librarian Fray José de Sigüenza defended Bosch's strangeness as an exposure of human sin and folly. Perishable fruit could support the transience of pleasure. This is important early reception, but it came about a century after the painting and cannot simply substitute for Bosch's intention.

A second approach imagines humanity without the Fall, or a possible primeval world. The center lacks obvious violence, aging, disease, and hierarchy; it resembles a condition before history, labor, and pain. Some scholars therefore read it as innocent generation had humankind not fallen, or as a pre-Flood or pre-civilized world. This accounts for the beauty and apparent freedom from pain, but it must still explain Hell and the directional structure of the triptych.

Wilhelm Fraenger's influential 1947 theory connected the work to a secret Adamite sect, sexual ritual, and even a sectarian wedding. No reliable document places Bosch in such a group. His Brotherhood and courtly ties also prevent the “heretical code” from being treated as fact. The theory belongs in the history of interpretation, not as a revelation of proven purpose.

A productive current approach treats the object as a courtly engine for conversation. It combines Creation, marriage, sin, proverbs, love culture, and foreign nature while allowing details to conflict. Moral warning, learned competition, and sensual entertainment could coexist. A useful test is not whether one key sounds exciting, but whether an interpretation accounts for the opening wings, the left-to-right structure, the center's beauty, Hell's inversions, and the early palace setting together.

**What to Show**

- A three-column comparison: moral warning / unfallen or primeval humanity / heretical hypothesis, with evidence and limitations.
- Sigüenza's publication and the madroño title.
- Brotherhood and Nassau records beside Fraenger's 1947 book.
- Return to the complete center; do not use one bizarre detail as proof that the whole work has been solved.

**Key Facts and Interpretations**

- Fact: Early Spanish records and commentary used concepts including madroño, the variety of the world, and lust; Sigüenza offered an important early moral reading. Source: Museo Nacional del Prado — https://www.museodelprado.es/en/the-collection/art-work/the-garden-of-earthly-delights-triptych/02388242-6d6a-4e9e-a992-e1311eab3609
- Fact: Bosch belonged to the Brotherhood of Our Lady; no reliable document proves Adamite membership. Source: BoschDoc — https://www.huygens.knaw.nl/en/resources/boschdoc-2/
- Interpretation: Moral warning, unfallen humanity, and courtly conversation each address part of the evidence; no single consensus exists.
- Disputed / unknown: No statement by Bosch confirms the center's unique meaning. Fraenger's theory is influential reception history, not a documented original function.

**Transition**

Because the work never closed around one answer, it continued to be copied, recast, and renamed. “Bosch” soon became more than one artist's name; it named an imaginative category.

## Segment 8 — From Courtly Marvel to a “Boschian” World

**Narrative Purpose**

Separate Bosch's courtly visibility around 1500, the sixteenth-century market for Boschian imagery, and his twentieth-century reframing through Surrealism and digital culture.

**Viewer Question**

Was Bosch forgotten for four hundred years and rediscovered only by the Surrealists?

**Content**

We should neither project today's fame backward nor say Bosch vanished after death. The 1504 court commission and 1517 Nassau Palace record show elite recognition during his lifetime and soon after it. But contemporary public reactions were not preserved like modern reviews, so claims that the first display “shocked all Europe” are unsupported.

In the sixteenth century, Bosch's Hells, monsters, and human follies circulated through copies, imitations, and prints. The Metropolitan Museum's Christ's Descent into Hell is assigned to a follower of Bosch; the museum notes that Bosch's fiery Hells became highly popular across sixteenth-century Europe and inspired imitators. Pieter Bruegel the Elder inherited the large crowd scene, proverb, hybrid creature, and upside-down human world, then redirected these devices toward sharper social observation. Big Fish Eat Little Fish (1557) offers a traceable print example rather than a claim based only on resemblance.

Surrealism reframed Bosch in the twentieth century. MoMA's 1936–1937 Fantastic Art, Dada, Surrealism included him within a long history of fantastic art. Yet calling him “a Surrealist four centuries early” erases a crucial difference. Surrealism foregrounded the unconscious, dreams, and the modern self; Bosch's art grew within Christianity, proverb culture, courtly discussion, and apocalyptic imagination.

High-resolution digital viewing now turns the buttocks score, Tree-Man, and bird-headed monster into memes, animations, or musical performances. This opens the work to new viewers but can fragment it into a collection of weird details. Zooming reveals; zooming back out restores direction. Both are necessary.

**What to Show**

- Sixteenth-century follower works and prints, clearly labeled as follower, copy, or imitation.
- Bruegel's Big Fish Eat Little Fish beside Bosch's reversals of animal and human roles.
- Archival material from MoMA's 1936–1937 exhibition.
- A digital zoom into the buttocks score, followed by a pullback to the right wing and whole triptych.

**Key Facts and Interpretations**

- Fact: Bosch's fiery Hell imagery was popular in sixteenth-century Europe and inspired many imitators. Source: The Metropolitan Museum of Art, Christ's Descent into Hell — https://www.metmuseum.org/art/collection/search/435725
- Fact: MoMA's exhibition ran December 9, 1936–January 17, 1937 and included Bosch. Source: MoMA — https://www.moma.org/calendar/exhibitions/2823
- Interpretation: Bruegel extended and transformed a Boschian language of proverbs, collective folly, and inversion; a concrete comparison is Big Fish Eat Little Fish. Source: The Met — https://www.metmuseum.org/art/collection/search/373271
- Disputed / unknown: Formal resemblance alone cannot prove that Dalí, Miró, or Ernst responded directly to a specific detail in The Garden. Letters, exhibition history, or specialized scholarship would be needed.

**Transition**

Boschian monsters traveled across Europe, while the original itself passed through courts, a royal collection, a monastery, civil war, and restoration before becoming one of the Prado's defining works.

## Segment 9 — How the Triptych Reached Us

**Narrative Purpose**

Complete the provenance, return to the physical experience of the painting today, and close with the transparent world from the opening.

**Viewer Question**

How did The Garden travel from the Nassau Palace to Madrid, and how can we avoid getting lost in its endless detail?

**Content**

The triptych was in the Nassau Palace by 1517. According to the Prado's provenance, it passed through the line of Engelbert II to Henry III, René of Chalon, and William I of Orange. During the political turmoil of the Netherlands it was confiscated in 1568 by forces associated with the Duke of Alba and then entered his family. Philip II acquired it in 1591, and it went to El Escorial in 1593. A devout Catholic monarch did not automatically regard naked bodies and monsters as heresy; the work could operate as moral and religious reflection.

It was sent from El Escorial to the Prado for restoration in 1933 and placed in the museum's care after the outbreak of the Spanish Civil War in 1936. Documents established its permanent loan in 1943. It remains legally owned by Patrimonio Nacional rather than purchased by the Prado. A major 1999–2000 conservation campaign and technical study improved structural stability and legibility. Conservation did not make the colors “new again”; it negotiated original material, historical change, and present stability.

On the research date, the Prado object page lists Room 056A. In front of the painting, first step back far enough to see all three panels and ask one question: Which things change function from left to right? Follow water, globes, fruit, animals, instruments, and the relation between users and used. Then move close, and finally step back again. This whole-detail-whole rhythm is more productive than decoding objects one at a time.

For the ending, close the wings in imagination. Hundreds of pleasures, sounds, and punishments disappear, leaving an uninhabited globe and the distant Creator. The opening question receives a concrete but open answer: temptation must be beautiful because viewers must recognize that they, too, can be drawn in. Bosch finally asks when a passing pleasure stops being something we use and becomes something that uses us.

**Transition**

Closing question: When you look at the center again, do you first see freedom and innocence—or a desire that has already become impossible to stop?

Source conflicts and treatment

- Date: The Prado label gives c. 1490–1500; the same page lists BRCP's 1501–1505. Both are retained. A dendrochronological terminus is not treated as the painting date.
- Patron: The Prado identifies Engelbert II, but no contract survives; “proposed patron” is used.
- Dimensions: The panel-image dimensions from the Prado are used. Larger overall measurements are not called “framed dimensions” when their basis is unclear.
- Center panel: Moral warning, unfallen humanity, pre-Flood or pre-civilized life, and courtly conversation remain competing approaches.
- Heresy: Fraenger's theory matters to reception history but lacks documentary evidence of Bosch's Adamite membership.
- Early reaction: The 1517 observation, later Spanish inventories, and Sigüenza's 1605 commentary are documentable. A sensational Europe-wide “first reception” is not.
- Modern influence: MoMA's exhibition documents Bosch's inclusion in a Surrealist genealogy. Direct influence on individual artists requires further evidence.

Research gaps

- Before quoting de Beatis or Sigüenza verbatim, verify the original edition, page, language, and translation.
- Detailed pigment, panel-construction, pentimento, or treatment claims require direct consultation of the BRCP technical volumes and Prado conservation report.
- Using a modern performance of the buttocks score requires checking the transcriber, arrangement, and recording rights.
- Recheck Room 056A and display status before publication or travel.
