Around the year 1605, a monk named Fray José de Sigüenza
offered a really crucial historical defense of Bosch.
He said the painting uses its odd imagery to reveal human folly,
where the easily perishable fruits echo the brief and illusory nature of sensual pleasure.
He argued that these grotesque symbols were meant as a sharp, satirical warning about human behavior,
not just the random ravings of a madman.
And honestly, this explains why even pious kings collected Bosch's work.
They saw the deep, moral warning, hidden right beneath the madness.
