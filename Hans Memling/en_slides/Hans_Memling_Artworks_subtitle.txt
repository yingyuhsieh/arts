A banker folds his hands. A young man displays an ancient coin. A saint’s journey unfolds around a miniature chapel. Hans Memling’s art often feels quiet, yet its details keep inviting us closer. Across thirteen works, we will see how he brings everyday experience into sacred images, and how a painting can guide the way we look.
[Page 2]
Memling was born in what is now Germany, although his birth date remains approximate. He is documented in Bruges from 1465. This trading city brought him foreign merchants and local religious patrons, while commissions also connected his workshop with England and Spain. His painted surfaces reward close attention. Skin looks soft, jewels catch light, and heavy fabrics fold with convincing weight. Hands establish relationships between figures. Windows lead our eyes into distant landscapes. Hinged panels can change what a viewer sees when a work opens or closes. Keep these choices in mind as we meet the people and stories in his paintings.
[Page 3]
This Last Judgment was intended for a family chapel near Florence. Privateers seized the ship carrying it in 1473, and the painting eventually remained in Gdańsk. Within the picture, human destinies also separate. Christ sits above on a rainbow. Directly below him, the Archangel Michael weighs souls. On our left, the saved approach heaven. On our right, the condemned descend into hell. Notice how differently the bodies move. Steps guide the eye upward toward salvation, while twisting figures pull it down toward punishment. The central axis holds these opposing movements together. Memling gives an immense and frightening subject a structure that we can follow. His balanced composition accommodates both hope and terror, reminding us that a calm arrangement can contain intensely emotional events.
[Page 4]
The Adoration of the Magi Triptych organizes Christ’s childhood across three connected panels. The Nativity appears on the left, the Adoration in the centre, and the Presentation in the Temple on the right. In the first scene, Mary kneels before the infant while Joseph enters carrying a candle. In the centre, rich garments and carefully painted containers give the visitors’ gifts a tangible presence. Architecture matters just as much. Small gaps reveal blue sky, and a town appears beyond the arches. These openings create depth, while the first two panels share the setting of the manger. Memling draws on compositions associated with Rogier van der Weyden, adapting them to his own sequence. We can follow the events while also noticing how walls and openings connect their spaces.
[Page 5]
Tommaso Portinari managed the Bruges branch of the Medici bank. The Met dates this portrait to about 1470 and connects its commission, tentatively, with his marriage to Maria. His joined hands explain the painting’s devotional purpose. This panel once formed a wing of a triptych with a central image of the Virgin and Child, now missing. Look closely at his chin. Stubble and a scar give his face individual character. Such details preserve a recognizable person within the formal pose of a wealthy patron. The illusionistic frame adds another effect, making his body seem to project toward our space. Viewed alone, the panel records a face. Imagining its original arrangement restores the act of worship that gave the portrait its direction and meaning.
[Page 6]
Maria’s portrait belonged to the same triptych as Tommaso’s. Her hands are joined, and her orientation leads toward the missing central image of the Virgin and Child. This time, begin with the tall headdress and its translucent veil. The veil appears light, while the necklace suggests the weight of jewels and metal. Together, they set off the softly modelled skin. Her dress makes social standing visible and places an Italian sitter within the clothing culture of Burgundian Bruges. Memling gives each material its own response to light. That careful distinction allows us to appreciate the ornaments while still attending to her face and prayerful gesture. The two portraits’ corresponding poses restore a sense of relationship, even though the object that once united their devotion has disappeared.
[Page 7]
At first, Scenes from the Passion of Christ resembles a crowded city view. Follow its roads slowly, and a sequence of events begins to emerge. Christ enters Jerusalem, shares the Last Supper, faces arrest and judgment, and suffers on the cross. These moments occupy different parts of one continuous setting. They belong to different times, yet Memling lets us encounter them within a single space. The same figure can appear again as the story moves forward. Gateways act like entrances to new episodes, while streets help our eyes travel between them. The Portinari donors kneel in the lower corners, bringing contemporary worshippers into relation with biblical events. We can begin with one road and follow a few recognizable figures, then return to see how their episodes connect. Memling organizes the time we spend looking. A single wooden panel becomes a long journey that gradually leads the viewer through suffering and toward the hope of resurrection.
[Page 8]
The man holds an ancient coin where we can examine it. Its portrait of the Roman emperor Nero points toward an interest in classical antiquity. A palm tree appears in the landscape, and laurel leaves enter the foreground. The Antwerp museum links these clues with the emblem of Bernardo Bembo, a Venetian diplomat and collector, supporting his identification as the sitter. We should understand this as a reading built from visual evidence. Now look at the man’s gaze. He meets our eyes while his body turns slightly to the side. Open sky gives his face a clear setting, and dark clothing brings attention to his hand and coin. This small gesture places appearance, learning and collecting within one portrait, as though he were showing us a prized possession.
[Page 9]
The Donne Triptych places a family before the Virgin and Child. Sir John Donne kneels on the left. His wife Elizabeth and a daughter appear on the right, accompanied by Saints Catherine and Barbara. John the Baptist and John the Evangelist occupy the wings. Notice the figures’ scale. Donors and saints share a comprehensible setting, giving prayer the form of an encounter. The tiled floor and open architecture help connect the panels. Rich textiles and distant scenery support the central gathering without dispersing our attention. The National Gallery’s technical research also reveals changes to the couple’s faces during painting. Memling adjusted their appearance toward greater individuality. The work shows how a devotional image can combine ideal figures with the recognizable features of the people who commissioned it.
[Page 10]
Completed in 1479, the St John Altarpiece was commissioned for the church of St John’s Hospital in Bruges. Saints gather around the Virgin and Child. The infant gives Catherine a ring, presenting the traditional subject of her spiritual marriage to Christ. The left wing depicts the Baptist’s martyrdom. On the right, John the Evangelist receives an apocalyptic vision on Patmos. A stable central gathering stands beside dramatic events. The background also contains a connection to hospital life. Between the columns, a wooden crane recalls the equipment used to lift wine barrels in Bruges. Museum research links income from the wine trade with the hospital. This small feature joins the sacred scene to the city’s economy and to the institution that paid for the work.
[Page 11]
Scenes from the Joys of Mary also carries the title Advent and Triumph of Christ. Its traditional reference to seven joys encompasses many narrative episodes. Begin with the Adoration of the Magi near the centre, then follow the visitors’ journeys through the surrounding landscape. Episodes concerning Mary and events after Christ’s resurrection extend across the view. Roads turn into towns and lead toward water, giving our eyes places to pause and change direction. Distance does not always tell us the order of events. We need to recognize figures and settings to reconstruct their time. Pieter Bultinc and his wife donated the painting in 1480 for the tanners’ chapel in the Church of Our Lady in Bruges. It now belongs to the Alte Pinakothek in Munich. Compared with the earlier Passion scene, this picture opens more of its narrative into an expansive landscape. Scenery provides a beautiful place to look and a structure that connects different moments, inviting the viewer to assemble the story.
[Page 12]
The left panel of this diptych presents the Virgin and Child. The right shows the young Maarten van Nieuwenhove with folded hands. Each panel has its own principal figure, yet together they form a shared interior. The crucial clue is a small convex mirror behind Mary. Its reflection helps establish that she and Maarten occupy the same room. Heraldry in the windows and the open prayer book before him connect that room with the donor’s identity and devotion. An inscription on the original frame records the year 1487 and his age of twenty-three. The image therefore belongs to a particular person at a particular moment. Look first at the two panels as a whole, then return to the mirror and windows. A tiny object changes our understanding of the larger composition. Memling makes the relationship between worshipper and sacred figure spatially imaginable, as though prayer could bring them together in a room whose objects we might recognize.
[Page 13]
This portrait, dated 1487, is traditionally known as Benedetto Portinari. Begin with the joined hands and the sideways gaze. His prayer extends beyond the edge of the panel. The picture belonged to a triptych, so its orientation makes sense in relation to other images. Meanwhile, the distant landscape opens space behind the sitter. Dark clothing gives his face and hands a clear presence, allowing the gesture to become our point of entry. The Uffizi describes the presumed sitter as Tommaso Portinari’s nephew. His precise identity remains a matter of interpretation, so that qualification matters. Compared with the earlier Portinari portraits, the devotional gesture continues, while landscape plays a more active role in the setting. Personal likeness and religious use remain closely connected in this form of portraiture.
[Page 14]
The Shrine of Saint Ursula turns painting into an object that asks us to walk around it. Gilded wood forms a miniature Gothic chapel, with delicate pinnacles, openings and a pitched roof. Six narrative scenes along its sides follow Ursula’s legendary pilgrimage and martyrdom. These are episodes from religious tradition, which we should distinguish from verified historical events. Boats, towns and groups of people remain readable within very small painted spaces. Made for St John’s Hospital, the shrine received relics in 1489. Unlike a flat panel, it connects viewing with bodily movement. Turning to another side reveals another episode. Its architectural shell also joins the preservation of relics with the narration of a saint’s life. Memling’s precise detail supports a physical experience of prayer and remembrance.
[Page 15]
This work has often been titled Christ with Singing and Music-Making Angels. The Antwerp museum currently identifies the central figure as God the Father and dates the panels to 1483–1494. He holds a crystal orb and raises his right hand in blessing. Singing angels gather beside him, while both side panels contain instrumental players. Together, they create a vision of heavenly music. Notice how the performers’ hands meet their instruments, and how wood and metal respond differently to light. Such precise details make the panels useful evidence for the study of early instruments. They originally formed the upper tier of an immense altarpiece for the monastery at Nájera in Spain. The rest is lost. Familiar objects and convincing gestures allow these surviving fragments to suggest music within a silent image.
[Page 16]
After these works, the quietness of Memling’s art becomes easier to understand. Folded hands direct a portrait toward prayer. A mirror joins two panels within a shared room. Roads connect moments across a landscape. Small objects can change our reading of an entire image. When you next encounter a painting by Memling, begin with its gestures, then explore the surrounding objects and spaces. Taking time reveals how everyday experience enters sacred imagery, and how the painter gives our attention somewhere to rest.
