# Our Famous Artists | Grant Wood’s *American Gothic*: Why Aren’t They Smiling?

## Video Positioning

- **Subject:** Grant Wood, the creation and visual structure of *American Gothic*, its historical context, related works, and cultural afterlife.
- **Central question:** Is the painting a satire of the American Midwest or a monument to labor and endurance?
- **Suggested length:** 17–19 minutes.
- **Approach:** A mystery-led opening followed by close looking, historical clarification, and the question of who gets to represent America.
- **Channel:** Our Famous Artists.

## Full Narration, Storyboard, and Visual Direction

### 1. Opening: Why Aren’t They Smiling? (00:00–01:05)

**Visuals**

- Begin in darkness with the three tines of the pitchfork.
- Move upward to the man’s hand and glasses, then the woman’s sideways glance, before revealing the entire painting.
- Show the words “Married?”, “Farmers?”, and “Satire?” before replacing them with one question mark.

**Narration**

A man grips a pitchfork and looks directly at us like a guard. The woman beside him refuses our gaze and looks somewhere beyond the frame. Behind them stands an ordinary white wooden house—except for one narrow pointed window that seems to belong to a small church.

This painting has almost become another face of America. Yet nearly everything we think we know about it may be wrong. The figures were not married. They did not live in the house. They never posed together in front of it. Even their identity as farmers was a role constructed by the artist.

So who were they? Is the pitchfork a tool or a warning? Was Grant Wood honoring the Midwest or making fun of it? Today, we will enter *American Gothic* and see how one house and two unsmiling faces became a nation’s image of itself.

### 2. The House Came First (01:05–02:20)

**Visuals**

- Locate Eldon, Iowa, on a map.
- Show the Dibble House, now known as the American Gothic House, and align it with the painted house.
- Move into a close-up of the pointed window.

**Narration**

The story did not begin with the people. It began with a house. In 1930, Wood saw a modest white residence in Eldon, Iowa. Its scale was unremarkable, but its front gable contained an unusually tall, pointed window.

The building belongs to a style known as Carpenter Gothic. Nineteenth-century American builders used wood and relatively simple methods to imitate the pointed arches and vertical lift of European Gothic churches. Wood was fascinated by the mismatch. Why would such an ordinary home wear a window that looked as if it belonged to a chapel?

He drew the house first and only afterward imagined the kind of people who might live inside. The occupants did not generate the architectural story; the architecture generated its fictional occupants.

The real building, known as the Dibble House, dates to 1881–82. Wood simplified the yard and walls and added other rural elements. The painting is not a document of one observed moment. It is a stage assembled from a real house, separately posed models, and the artist’s imagination.

### 3. They Were Not Married (02:20–03:35)

**Visuals**

- Show photographs of Dr. Byron H. McKeeby and Nan Wood Graham.
- Use a three-level card: “Real models,” “Painted roles,” and “Popular assumption.”

**Narration**

The male model was Wood’s dentist, Dr. Byron H. McKeeby. The woman was the artist’s sister, Nan Wood Graham. They posed separately. They were not husband and wife, and neither lived in the house.

We must also distinguish the models from the roles they perform. Wood later described the figures as a farmer and his daughter. Popular culture, however, has persistently treated them as a married couple. The mistake is understandable: they stand together before a home, the man appears older, and the entire image resembles an old family photograph.

There are therefore three separate realities. The models were a sister and a dentist. The painted characters, according to Wood’s later description, were a father and daughter. In popular memory, they became the world’s most famous stern married couple.

Wood drew on family photographs and old-fashioned clothing. The man’s overalls and collarless shirt, the woman’s white collar, apron, and cameo connect them to thrift, duty, domestic order, and inherited values.

### 4. The Pitchfork Is the Painting’s Skeleton (03:35–04:50)

**Visuals**

- Animate lines upward through the pitchfork, suspenders, shirt, nose, siding, and window.
- If rights permit, compare the final work with the early sketch in which the man holds a rake.

**Narration**

We notice the faces first, but the pitchfork truly organizes the painting. It rises from the lower edge as a stable vertical axis. Its three long tines reappear in the man’s suspenders, striped shirt, narrow nose, wooden siding, and pointed window.

In an early drawing, the man held a rake. In the final painting, Wood replaced it with a three-tined pitchfork. No definitive explanation survives, but compositionally the change binds tool, body, and architecture into a much stronger vertical rhythm.

The pitchfork is first an agricultural tool, a sign of labor and land. In the man’s grip, however, it can also become an emblem of identity, a boundary, a shield, or even something faintly threatening. He does not raise it, yet his posture seems to tell us that we have come close enough.

Without the pitchfork, this might be merely an awkward family portrait. The tool connects occupation, character, property, and the viewer’s position outside the home.

### 5. Two Gazes and a Story Without an Ending (04:50–06:00)

**Visuals**

- Move from the man’s eyes to the woman’s sideways glance.
- Draw subtle sight lines.
- Briefly show a clearly labeled visualization of how the mood would change if she looked directly outward.

**Narration**

The man confronts us directly. His closed mouth and round spectacles sharpen his face, while the pitchfork and his position make him appear to guard private territory.

The woman stands slightly behind him and is partly blocked by his body. She looks away from us. Is she worried, irritated, watchful, submissive, or simply following the decorum of an old portrait? The painting does not say.

This difference creates the psychological drama. The man prevents us from entering, but the woman implies that something else exists beyond the frame. Viewers instinctively invent what has happened and what may happen next.

The painting supplies characters without a script. That is one reason it can be restaged endlessly: every generation can insert its own family tension, social anxiety, or political conflict between these two lines of sight.

### 6. The House Is the Third Character (06:00–07:05)

**Visuals**

- Compare the pointed window with a European Gothic church window, then return to the wooden house.
- Overlay the window’s shape on the man’s face and pitchfork.

**Narration**

The word “Gothic” in the title refers first to the house’s pointed window, not to a supernatural story. Carpenter Gothic architecture miniaturized the forms of Old World churches, rebuilt them in wood, and placed them on New World homes.

The house is more than a background. It projects its shapes onto the figures. The man’s long face, nose, suspenders, and pitchfork all answer the window and vertical siding. He appears to have grown from the building’s structure.

The space is unusually shallow. The figures press toward us while the house presses behind them. There is no broad landscape into which the eye can escape, so we feel uncomfortably close—as if we have crossed into private space.

We might even reverse the usual question. Did these people choose this house, or did the house choose the people who best expressed its personality?

### 7. Learning to Paint Iowa from Van Eyck (07:05–08:20)

**Visuals**

- Show details from Jan van Eyck, Hans Memling, and Albrecht Dürer: hands, fabrics, hair, glass, and wooden surfaces.
- Compare Northern Renaissance donor portraits with *American Gothic*.
- Introduce *Woman with Plants* and its cameo and apron details.

**Narration**

*American Gothic* looks unmistakably American, but its technique owes much to Europe. Wood traveled there several times and, in Munich, deepened his engagement with Northern Renaissance painting. He admired the clear contours, precise surfaces, and solemn figures of Van Eyck, Memling, and Dürer.

He carried the authority of those old portraits back to Iowa. Skin, spectacles, hair, cloth, and wood are rendered with meticulous clarity. The two figures stand with something like the solemnity of donors in an altarpiece—except that European nobles and sacred patrons have been replaced by ordinary Midwestern Americans.

Wood’s portrait of his mother, *Woman with Plants*, already uses similar apron trim and a cameo brooch. It is a prelude to *American Gothic*: a family member, old-fashioned clothing, and precise technique transformed into a vessel of local memory.

Regionalism, then, did not simply reject Europe. Wood borrowed an Old World language to create a New World mythology.

### 8. 1930: A National Portrait in a Time of Crisis (08:20–09:35)

**Visuals**

- Historical images of the 1929 crash, Depression-era streets, and Midwestern farms.
- A graphic noting the 1930 Art Institute of Chicago exhibition and award.

**Narration**

Wood completed the painting in 1930 as the United States was descending into the Great Depression. It does not show unemployment, bankruptcy, or ruined crops and should not be treated as a journalistic record of Depression farm life. But the crisis changed what viewers saw in it.

Some early observers interpreted the rigid faces as a mockery of conservative small-town people. Some Iowans felt insulted, believing that Wood had portrayed them as backward and ridiculous.

As the Depression continued, other viewers found a different meaning in the clean clothing, maintained home, firm tool, and unyielding posture. They saw attachment to land, thrift, labor, and endurance. The same picture could therefore signify satire and hope, narrowness and resilience.

At the Art Institute of Chicago’s 1930 annual exhibition, *American Gothic* received the Norman Wait Harris Bronze Medal and a three-hundred-dollar prize. The museum subsequently acquired it. A painting only about thirty-one inches high rapidly became a national image.

### 9. Satire or Tribute? (09:35–10:45)

**Visuals**

- Build two columns: “Satire” and “Tribute.”
- Under satire: elongated faces, disproportionate window, role-playing.
- Under tribute: frontal dignity, work tool, meticulous technique.
- Merge both into the word “Ambiguity.”

**Narration**

If we call the picture only satire, we lose the figures’ dignity and weight. If we call it only praise, we ignore the humor created by exaggeration, rigidity, and mismatch.

The satirical reading points to the elongated faces and poses, the almost pretentious church window on a small wooden house, and two modern models performing an imagined rural type.

The respectful reading points to their central placement and the solemnity of Northern Renaissance portraiture. The pitchfork, work clothes, and frontal posture can signify labor, self-reliance, and the protection of home.

Wood later emphasized a positive intention, but that statement cannot erase the image’s comedy and distance. The most accurate answer may not be a choice between two meanings. Its lasting power comes from allowing admiration and criticism to occupy the same frame.

### 10. How a Small Painting Creates Monumental Force (10:45–11:50)

**Visuals**

- Compare the painting’s 78 by 65.3 centimeter dimensions with a human silhouette.
- Show the three-line signature on the man’s overalls.
- Diagram the main color blocks and vertical repetitions.

**Narration**

The original is not large: 78 centimeters high and 65.3 centimeters wide. Wood painted in oil on Beaver Board, a pressed wood-fiber composition board, rather than canvas. Its hard, smooth surface supported the clean, detailed finish and minimized visible brushwork.

On the man’s overalls, the artist signed his name in three lines: “Grant, Wood, 1930,” as if the signature itself belonged to the clothing.

The painting does not rely on violent light and shadow. Its force comes from design. The dark jacket forms a stable mass. The woman’s dress is divided by a white collar and printed apron. The white house, green plants, red building, and blue-gray sky create distinct zones. Pitchfork, suspenders, stripes, nose, window, and boards all rise together.

It behaves like an old master painting and an exceptionally efficient modern poster at the same time.

### 11. A Visual Formula That Can Be Rewritten Forever (11:50–13:00)

**Visuals**

- Reduce the composition to three silhouettes: two people, an upright tool, and a peaked house.
- Use a fast montage of appropriately licensed parodies and restagings.

**Narration**

Why has *American Gothic* been parodied so often? Because it functions as an extraordinarily compressed visual formula: two people side by side, one upright tool, one peaked house. Preserve those elements and viewers identify the source almost immediately.

The pitchfork can become any professional, domestic, or political object. The figures can become allies, rivals, relatives, celebrities, or cartoon characters. The house keeps them anchored to questions of home and national identity.

Every revision asks something new. Who protects the home? Who remains outside? Is this a pose of family unity or a diagram of power? Because the original withholds a final story, it can continue to hold new ones.

### 12. Gordon Parks: Who Gets to Represent America? (13:00–14:20)

**Visuals**

- Show Gordon Parks’s *American Gothic, Washington, D.C.* from 1942.
- Compare the frontal figure, tools, and background with Wood’s painting.
- Identify Ella Watson and her work as a government office charwoman.

**Narration**

Parody is not always comedy. In 1942, the African American photographer Gordon Parks made *American Gothic, Washington, D.C.* His subject, Ella Watson, worked as a cleaner in the offices of the Farm Security Administration. She stands before an American flag holding a mop in one hand and a broom in the other.

Parks preserved Wood’s frontal pose and vertical tools, but he replaced the wooden house with the national flag and the white rural characters with a Black woman employed inside a federal institution.

In Wood’s painting, the pitchfork may signify labor, independence, and home. In Parks’s photograph, the mop and broom reveal who cleans the nation’s rooms while being denied equal rights and respect.

Parks did not destroy the national icon. He reopened it. His photograph asks a question that remains urgent: when we imagine “the American,” whom do we picture—and whom have we left outside the frame?

### 13. Related Works by Grant Wood (14:20–15:50)

**Visuals**

- Show *Woman with Plants*, *Daughters of Revolution*, *Stone City, Iowa*, *Young Corn*, *The Midnight Ride of Paul Revere*, and *Parson Weems’ Fable*.
- Hold each for eight to ten seconds with a date and one viewing cue.

**Narration**

*American Gothic* was not an isolated accident. In *Woman with Plants*, Wood used his mother as a model. The old clothing, cameo, plant, and frontal dignity already establish his language of family memory.

*Stone City, Iowa* and *Young Corn* turn Midwestern land into smooth, rhythmic, almost model-like landscapes. These are not neutral views of nature; they are carefully designed visions of local order.

*Daughters of Revolution* reveals a sharper satirical edge. Three proud patriotic women pose before an image of Washington crossing the Delaware, raising questions about how national identity becomes social performance.

*The Midnight Ride of Paul Revere* presents history as a toy village seen from above. *Parson Weems’ Fable* turns the famous cherry-tree legend into a stage production. Together, these pictures show that Wood did not merely record America. He studied how America manufactures stories about itself.

### 14. How the House Became a Cultural Landmark (15:50–16:45)

**Visuals**

- Show the modern American Gothic House and visitors restaging the pose.
- Create a short sequence: “Real house → Painted stage → Cultural landmark.”

**Narration**

The Dibble House in Eldon is now known as the American Gothic House. Visitors dress up, hold pitchforks, and reenact the famous pose in front of it.

The result is a remarkable circle. Wood transformed a real, ordinary house into a painted symbol of America. Once the painting became famous, art returned to alter the real building, turning it into a destination and a center of local memory.

The imaginary residents who never actually lived there now attract countless real people who come to perform their roles. Life entered art, and art returned to reorganize life.

### 15. Conclusion: A Painting Without a Fixed Answer (16:45–17:45)

**Visuals**

- Return to the complete painting and sequentially highlight the house, figures, pitchfork, and gazes.
- End on the space between their lines of sight.
- Channel outro and subscription prompt.

**Narration**

When we look again at *American Gothic*, we no longer see a married farming couple standing outside their own home. We see an image assembled from a real house, a dentist, the artist’s sister, family clothing, Northern Renaissance technique, and the anxieties of America in 1930.

The man’s gaze refuses our entry while the woman’s gaze carries the story beyond the frame. The pitchfork is both tool and boundary. The window is both decoration and a formal rule that reshapes the people before it.

The work can be a satire of conformity and a tribute to labor and endurance. It can project one version of America and then be rewritten by Gordon Parks to question who has the right to represent the nation.

A great national image does not always deliver a single answer. It behaves more like a mirror in which successive generations discover their own families, values, fears, and hopes.

## Related Artwork Introductions

### 1. *American Gothic* (1930)

- **Medium:** Oil on Beaver Board, a pressed wood-fiber composition board.
- **Dimensions:** 78 × 65.3 cm.
- **Collection:** Art Institute of Chicago.
- **Introduction:** Wood used a Carpenter Gothic house in Eldon as a stage and asked his dentist and sister to play a farmer and daughter. Pitchfork, bodies, and pointed window become one vertical system.
- **Look for:** The contrast between the man’s direct gaze and the woman’s sideways glance, and the repetition of the pitchfork’s tines across clothing, face, and architecture.

### 2. *Woman with Plants* (1929)

- **Introduction:** Wood portrayed his mother through old-fashioned clothing, a cameo, and a potted plant, turning a family member into a bearer of domestic memory and Midwestern values.
- **Look for:** The cameo, apron trim, and frontal dignity that anticipate *American Gothic*.

### 3. *Daughters of Revolution* (1932)

- **Introduction:** Three patriotic women stand before a reproduction of *Washington Crossing the Delaware*. Restrained expressions and carefully staged details turn patriotic authority into social satire.
- **Look for:** The tension between the women’s self-image and the heroic national mythology behind them.

### 4. *Stone City, Iowa* (1930)

- **Introduction:** Rolling fields, roads, and trees become a smooth and rhythmic local landscape—an idealized, highly designed vision of the Midwest.
- **Look for:** The elevated viewpoint and curving terrain that transform actual land into an almost model-like world.

### 5. *Young Corn* (1931)

- **Introduction:** Spring farmland becomes a sequence of softly swelling green forms, while people and animals shrink into the larger order of the land.
- **Look for:** The artificial smoothness and repeating rhythms imposed on the natural landscape.

### 6. *The Midnight Ride of Paul Revere* (1931)

- **Introduction:** Wood views the celebrated Revolutionary story from high above. The village glows like a toy model, making national legend enchanting but not entirely trustworthy.
- **Look for:** The fairy-tale scale and theatrical lighting that reshape a heroic narrative.

### 7. *Parson Weems’ Fable* (1939)

- **Introduction:** A stage curtain opens onto the legend of young George Washington and the cherry tree. An adult Washington head appears on a child’s body, deliberately exposing the tale’s artificial construction.
- **Look for:** How Wood presents both the national myth and the person who manufactures it.

### 8. Gordon Parks, *American Gothic, Washington, D.C.* (1942)

- **Medium:** Photograph.
- **Subject:** Ella Watson.
- **Introduction:** Parks replaced Wood’s pitchfork and house with a mop, broom, and American flag, transforming a familiar national icon into a critique of race, labor, and citizenship.
- **Look for:** How tools and background cause a similar pose to acquire an entirely different political meaning.

## Historical and Production Notes

- The real models were Wood’s sister and dentist. Wood later described the characters as a farmer and daughter; the married-couple interpretation belongs to popular reception.
- The models posed separately and did not stand together before the Dibble House.
- “Gothic” refers first to the Carpenter Gothic pointed window and should not automatically be framed as horror.
