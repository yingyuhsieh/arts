This image once belonged to a book you could hold in your hands. Yet its architecture feels spacious, and its figures seem to have real weight. How did the French painter Jean Fouquet give a face, a prayer book, and even an ancient battle such presence? Twelve works offer a way into his world.
[Page 2]
Our story begins with an image we can no longer see. Around fourteen forty-four to fourteen forty-six, Fouquet’s activity in Italy included a portrait of Pope Eugene the Fourth. Written testimony preserves its reputation, but the original is lost. We therefore leave its appearance open. His Italian experience provides a useful context for the solid figures and architectural spaces in later works. These meet a northern interest in precise faces and surfaces. Still, the surviving evidence does not give us a complete travel diary or a proven list of teachers. The missing portrait reminds us to distinguish what documents establish from what surviving paintings allow us to observe.
[Page 3]
Charles the Seventh wears no crown and raises no weapon. His presence comes first from the broad shape of his body. Step back, and the hat, shoulders, and red robe form a stable silhouette. The curtains keep our attention close to him. Move nearer, and the eyes, nose, and lips become distinctly individual. Fouquet allows a recognizable person to carry the authority of kingship. The Louvre dates the portrait to about fourteen forty-five to fourteen fifty, while other studies place it later. We should therefore avoid tying it to one particular victory. Its later history brings another surprise. A nineteenth-century inventory attributed it to an unknown Greek painter. The king’s face survived, but identifying Fouquet’s hand required later scholarship. The painting preserves one identity while its collection history reveals the recovery of another.
[Page 4]
This powerful man also wears a broad red robe, but his gesture changes the encounter. Guillaume Jouvenel des Ursins joins his hands in prayer toward a sacred presence beyond the picture. His body anchors our attention inside the frame, while his hands carry it outward. Golden architecture and intricate decoration make his social position visible. He served as chancellor of France, and the painting gives that office considerable weight. Religious devotion and worldly distinction occupy the same space. Notice how the small, concentrated movement of the fingers gathers the much larger mass of the robe. We cannot confidently reconstruct a missing companion panel from that gesture alone. The portrait’s later attributions also shifted, first to Dürer and then to Wolgemut. Recognizing Fouquet meant learning to see how these particular choices belonged together.
[Page 5]
The Melun Diptych becomes easier to understand when we see both wings together. On the left, Étienne Chevalier prays beside Saint Stephen. The saint’s book and stones refer to his martyrdom by stoning. On the right, the pale Virgin and Child appear among brilliant red and blue angels. The donor’s architectural setting feels accessible, while the sacred vision seems removed from everyday life. Clear contours and rounded forms connect the two worlds. The work belonged to a commemorative setting in Melun, and its original frame included velvet, pearls, and medallions. Around seventeen seventy-three, the ensemble was dismantled. The panels eventually entered collections in Berlin and Antwerp. Placing their images together helps us follow the direction of prayer again. It cannot fully recover the precious frame or the original experience of seeing the work in its church.
[Page 6]
Look closely at the right wing and notice how a body placed so near us can still feel distant. White is the first attraction. The pale skin of the Virgin and Child stands out against the red and blue angels with an almost sculptural clarity. Our eyes then travel through the crown, pearls, and throne, where tiny reflections reward closer attention. Sharp contours make every form legible, while the restrained faces slow the emotional pace. Strong color catches us, and stillness keeps us looking. The exposed breast belongs to the religious tradition of the nursing Virgin. Here it shares the same figure with a heavenly queen’s crown. Rounded bodies and angel heads create related curves across the surface. Whether the Virgin resembles Agnès Sorel remains a matter of debate, including in the museum’s own discussion. That possibility need not dominate everything we see. Fouquet brings maternal care, courtly beauty, and sacred authority into one exceptionally controlled image. Its effect remains compelling even when the model’s identity stays uncertain.
[Page 7]
This face looks commanding on a screen, but the original measures only about seven point two centimeters across. Gold lines and shading emerge from a dark surface, while the surrounding name identifies the maker. The enamel medallion originally belonged to the frame of the Melun Diptych. Imagine the order of attention. First came the Virgin, Child, donor, and saint. Only then might a viewer discover the painter at the edge. Fouquet made himself visible within the hierarchy of a religious commission. Now preserved separately in the Louvre, the object can seem like an independent declaration of artistic identity. Its original setting also gave it the functions of ornament, signature, and remembrance. We do not need to call it Europe’s first self-portrait to appreciate the choice. A maker placed both his name and his face inside an object created for someone else.
[Page 8]
Moving from an altarpiece to a book of hours changes the viewer’s physical relationship with an image. Chevalier’s book brought pictures close to the hands and linked them with prayers organized through the day. In this scene of Saint Margaret and Olibrius, figures, animals, and distant buildings inhabit one landscape. Nearby actions draw us into the event, while the background suggests a world continuing beyond it. On a small surface, Fouquet makes bodily weight and spatial distance legible. Monumentality comes from clear relationships rather than physical size. Repeated initials elsewhere in the book also bring the patron’s identity into prayer. Such an object could display wealth and serve sincere devotion. Its functions could coexist. Enlarging the page helps us notice details today, but remembering its original scale restores something of the intimacy that shaped its design.
[Page 9]
As you look at the Crucifixion, imagine prayers and other images before and after this page. Reading once moved through that sequence and returned to familiar scenes. The Hours of Étienne Chevalier was dismantled in the eighteenth century. A book made for use gradually became a group of pictures that could circulate separately. In eighteen ninety-one, the Duke of Aumale acquired forty miniatures, creating the major surviving group at Chantilly. Their reunion allowed viewers to see them together, but it did not restore the original binding or the complete reading order. Other leaves remain elsewhere. Collecting brought preservation and transformation at the same time. An individual image gained prominence while its connections with text became harder to see. Alongside admiring Fouquet’s figures and spaces, we can ask what acts of reading this picture originally accompanied.
[Page 10]
Simon de Varie’s book shows how a painter could shape an entire reading experience without painting every page. Fouquet supplied six opening miniatures for the manuscript dated fourteen fifty-five. Other illuminators contributed to the rest. Here, the Virgin and Child, their drapery, and the decorated boundary make the beginning of reading feel like a quiet encounter. The patron’s portrait and heraldry elsewhere connect prayer with a particular person. Varie belonged to the royal financial world, and his chosen appearance should not be mistaken for a straightforward professional record. Armor in a portrait cannot prove participation in battle. The book later became three volumes, now divided between Los Angeles and The Hague. Comparing the dispersed material helped scholars reconnect images and identity. Fouquet’s contribution demonstrates the power of a carefully designed entrance into a larger book.
[Page 11]
Saint Luke seems to welcome us into a working room. He writes at a book, while the ox identifies him as an evangelist. A painting of the Virgin recalls the tradition that he painted her portrait. Glass vessels evoke his medical role. Fouquet gives these objects clear places and recognizable purposes. Notice how the figure holds the composition together while the floor and walls carry the room backward. This is a fifteenth-century visual explanation of a saint, rather than a literal record of an ancient workshop. The manuscript itself also contains layers of time. The Morgan attributes the first nine full-page miniatures to Fouquet and sixteen others to Jean Colombe. Its present name does not establish that Fouquet originally made the whole book for Robertet. Initials, later work, and rebinding preserve evidence of changing ownership and continued production.
[Page 12]
At a banquet, seating can reveal power before anyone speaks. This miniature represents Charles the Fifth receiving the emperor in thirteen seventy-eight, but Fouquet reimagines the occasion through fifteenth-century clothing and surroundings. The distant main table remains stable while attendants animate the foreground. White cloth and royal blue guide the eye. Floor tiles suggest recession without requiring every line to meet at one mathematically consistent point. The arrangement makes ceremonial relationships readable. A much larger gathering and a longer event become one memorable historical scene. Coronations, meetings, and campaigns elsewhere in the chronicle similarly give the dynasty a visible life. Charles the Seventh remains a possible original recipient rather than a proven patron. We can understand how the image organizes royal authority without settling that question. Position, scale, and direction already make political order visible.
[Page 13]
The king occupies the center, with knights arranged along both sides of a small room. Open floor separates us from the throne. Similar white robes establish a shared identity, while placement preserves differences in rank. Saint Michael’s image above the king identifies the order’s heavenly patron. This is the opening miniature of a rule book, rather than a snapshot of a particular meeting. Louis the Eleventh founded the order in fourteen sixty-nine, and its statutes explicitly required an opening image of the sovereign and the first knights. The picture therefore helped give a new institution visible form. Different faces suggest individual members, but they do not supply a securely identified attendance list. Fouquet’s compact interior makes collective belonging and hierarchy readable together. Before readers reach the rules, they encounter the organized body those rules were intended to sustain.
[Page 14]
Begin with the bodies at the front. A sword waits to fall, legs brace for combat, and fallen figures interrupt the action. Farther back, individuals merge into a dense army. Beyond them lie water, hills, and sky. Fouquet makes war readable at the scale of a body and at the scale of a crowd. Gold highlights separate the nearest armor and echo the decorated page. The Louvre attributes that border to another artist, so its details should not all become evidence of Fouquet’s hand. The leaf came from an ancient-history manuscript, and the text below belongs to its meaning. The museum retains the qualification “known as Cannae.” We should likewise avoid mapping each group onto a securely reconstructed ancient battle plan. Quiet distance and nearby violence create tension. They invite reflection without proving a documented antiwar statement by the artist.
[Page 15]
In this large religious painting at Nouans, bodily weight becomes immediate. Christ extends across the foreground while surrounding figures support him. The Virgin and the donor join their hands in prayer. Hands carrying a body and hands expressing grief occupy the same scene. White cloth and pale flesh connect the crowded group around a clear center. The French heritage register calls the subject a Descent from the Cross, while the familiar title is the Pietà of Nouans. Each directs attention to a different aspect of the Passion narrative. Attribution to Fouquet remains qualified, and alternative dates exist. The donor’s identity is also uncertain. The painting still belongs to a local church, reminding us that these works have lives beyond major museums. Across very different scales, stable bodies and clear relationships give viewers a place within the story.
[Page 16]
Across these works, Fouquet’s grandeur grows from clear relationships. A king fills a frame. Joined hands reach beyond one. Prayer books connect individuals with sacred history, while tables and rooms give political order a visible form. Panels, medallions, and pages now survive in different places, so comparison helps recover some of their connections. The next time you meet a Fouquet image, follow its directions. Who turns toward whom? Where does your eye travel? How does a limited surface open onto a larger world?
