# Turner’s Fighting Temeraire: A Farewell Still in Motion

> Our Famous Artists segment plan; an outline rather than a verbatim narration.

## A. Video Core Narrative
Turner pictured a celebrated warship on its way to the breakers, yet restored its removed masts and set the voyage against a sunset that does not match the journey’s time or direction. By looking first, then comparing archival images and evidence, viewers discover how the little tug, towering ship, smoke, and light turn a tow into a meditation on memory, labor, and change.

### Segment 1 — Why does a ghostly warship have a smokestack in front of it?

**Purpose**
Hook with scale and a historical puzzle.

**Key Message**
Reconstruction turns the last voyage into a farewell.

**Content**
Open on the little black tug, then reveal the immense pale ship it pulls. The Temeraire once fought at Trafalgar, yet in this scene it cannot move by itself. The sun is setting, and the tug’s smoke cuts across rigging that should no longer have been there. Pose the central question before giving the date: why would Turner restore a stripped ship’s masts and move its final voyage into a sunset? Let a wide view establish the balance between tall, luminous hull and low, compact engine. Crop the towing line, smoke, and the glowing reflection, then briefly show the stripped hull in William Beatson’s print. The contrast creates a promise for the film: this is a historical voyage reshaped into a farewell.

### Segment 2 — A ship with a past

**Purpose**
Establish the ship’s service and sale.

**Key Message**
The ship’s past gives the tow historical weight.

**Content**
Introduce the ship rather than a full biography of Turner. Temeraire was a 98-gun second-rate warship launched in 1798. At Trafalgar in 1805, it helped defend Nelson’s Victory while engaging the French Redoutable and Fougueux. Use a restrained diagram with three labeled ships over an institutional battle image; avoid making the print stand in as exact documentary footage. The ship later served other roles before the Admiralty sold it in 1838 to shipbreaker John Beatson for £5,530. This was the vessel’s sale price, not the painting’s value. A dockside print shows the stripped hull and gives viewers a material baseline. Return to Turner’s pale, upright image: the words “Fighting Temeraire” and the painting’s restored masts carry the earlier life into the final trip.

### Segment 3 — The two-day tow and the invented sunset

**Purpose**
Compare the tow with Turner’s reconstruction.

**Key Message**
Masts and sunset are meaningful pictorial decisions.

**Content**
On 5–6 September 1838, two steam tugs, London and Samson, moved the stripped Temeraire from Sheerness toward Beatson’s wharf at Rotherhithe. A simple labeled Thames route can be made in-house; it is a modern orientation graphic, not an eighteenth-century artifact. The National Gallery says Turner probably did not witness the event, and contemporary reports supplied his imagined scene. Compare the dock print’s mastless hull with his rigged, glowing ship. The vessel reached the wharf in the afternoon, while Turner supplied an evening sky; the sun’s position also conflicts with the ship’s westward route. His chief tug is prominent, with a second tug barely discernible in the distance. These changes concentrate memory, labor, and ending in one image rather than furnish a navigational record.

### Segment 4 — The eye travels from sail to steam

**Purpose**
Teach the composition through close looking.

**Key Message**
The smaller, darker tug has the power to move the ship.

**Content**
Now read the painting slowly from left to right. The tall masts and pale hull appear almost weightless, while the short, dark tug has the force to move the entire ship. Smoke crosses the old rigging. The ship has no naval flag, whereas the tug carries a commercial flag: a shift of ownership and purpose becomes visible in small details. Below, broken reflections turn solid bodies into dissolving marks. A dark buoy in the foreground anchors the viewer’s position; distant sails and masts remind us that the age of sail did not end overnight. A close crop of Willmore’s later engraving reveals how moving the tug’s funnel behind the mast altered Turner’s composition. Let the viewer see why the arrangement, not merely the identity of the ships, matters.

### Segment 5 — A sunset made of paint

**Purpose**
Explain how light and paint carry emotion.

**Key Message**
Sunset and paint give the event an elegiac scale.

**Content**
Cover the sunset for a moment, leaving only the two vessels. The basic story survives, but much of the painting’s emotional scale vanishes. Restore the fiery right side: orange, gold, and red run into the river while a pale crescent sits high at left. Sunset can suggest an ending, but the moon need not be assigned one fixed symbol. In close view, Turner’s impasto and transparent glazes create different kinds of luminosity; precise rigging contrasts with freer sky and water. The National Gallery’s X-ray evidence indicates a larger underlying sail, but it does not prove Turner rushed under a deadline. Describe what can be seen on the surface and what technical examination reveals, then return to the full image. The sunset gives a practical journey the feeling of a public elegy.

### Segment 6 — Did Turner fear the machine?

**Purpose**
Examine the ambivalence toward industry.

**Key Message**
Grief and curiosity about machinery can coexist.

**Content**
The old ship looks like a noble ghost and the tug like a smoky intruder, so an anti-industrial reading is tempting. Yet the tug is also a working vessel doing the job that moves the narrative forward. Compare Turner’s 1844 Rain, Steam and Speed, where a train, weather, and motion are central subjects. His art can register loss and fascination with new power at once. The 1839 Royal Academy exhibition attached adapted lines from Thomas Campbell about a flag no longer belonging to the ship; this sharpened memory of its fighting past. Thackeray’s hostile description of the tug is evidence of a contemporary response, not a transcript of Turner’s intentions. The artist was about sixty-four when the work appeared, but calling the ship his self-portrait would turn an evocative analogy into an unsupported claim.

### Segment 7 — The image after the voyage

**Purpose**
Trace the image in engraving and currency.

**Key Message**
Reproduction preserves and changes an image.

**Content**
Turner kept the painting rather than selling it; it passed to the National Gallery through his bequest in 1856. Yet its image traveled through Willmore’s engraving, whose altered tug details and title, The Old Temeraire, show how reproduction can reshape a work. Keep the British Temeraire distinct from the French Téméraire captured in 1759 and from HMS Dreadnought, a separate ship despite a potentially confusing Chinese translation. The work’s public life continued into the polymer £20 note issued in 2020. A careful comparison of painting, engraving, and banknote asks a useful question: what survives when a richly textured oil painting becomes a portable image? The episode belongs to the history of looking, not proof that every later use means the same thing.

### Segment 8 — A farewell that has not finished

**Purpose**
Answer the opening question with a viewing method.

**Key Message**
The ending lies beyond the frame, extending the farewell.

**Content**
Return to the whole painting and the question from the opening. Turner gives the vessel its masts, moves the hour toward sunset, and lets a small steam tug pull a much larger body into the future. The breaker’s yard is outside the frame, so the ship remains present at the very moment we know it will disappear. Offer viewers a final method for looking: identify what the title tells you, follow the towing line and smoke, compare the actual dock print, then ask what the invented light changes. The painting need not announce that steam instantly replaced sail or that national power died with one ship. Its more durable subject is the interval between an ending already decided and something still visible in the light.

## G. Fact-Checking Notes
- The 1838 in the full title refers to the tow; the painting was made and exhibited at the Royal Academy in 1839.
- Temeraire was designed as a 98-gun ship; the dock print’s “104 guns” title is not the design rating.
- The £5,530 paid to John Beatson in 1838 was the ship’s sale price, not the painting’s.
- London and Samson performed the actual tow; Turner made one tug prominent and the second faint.
- Turner probably did not witness the tow; the masts, coloring, funnel placement, and sunset are reconstructed.
- An afternoon arrival and westbound course conflict with the painted sunset; the picture is not a navigational record.
- An anti-industrial manifesto or an intentional self-portrait of the aging artist remains interpretation, not established intention.
- Willmore’s engraving altered vessel details. Distinguish British Temeraire, French Téméraire, and HMS Dreadnought.
- Rights in the painting, later photographs, engravings, and banknote imagery are separate and require image-level verification.
