In 1966, Andrei Rublev entered pop culture history with Andrei Tarkovsky’s legendary film, Andrei Rublev.
But fair warning: this isn't a textbook biopic. Key storylines—like his vow of silence or the famous bell-casting scene—were imaginative leaps to fill the massive voids in Rublev’s biography. Tarkovsky’s real obsession was deeper: How does an artist protect their soul when surrounded by brutality, censorship, and doubt?
The cinematic payoff is incredible. After hours of bleak, brutal black-and-white imagery, the film's finale suddenly explodes into vivid color, showcasing Rublev's actual icons. It’s as if divine beauty is being reborn right out of human agony.
Unsurprisingly, the Soviet government hated it. They locked away the initial 205-minute cut. After sneaking out to Cannes in 1969 and winning the Critics' Prize, Tarkovsky’s official 183-minute edit finally got a restricted Soviet release in 1971.
Tarkovsky’s movie might be fiction, but it redefined Rublev forever—turning a forgotten medieval painter into the ultimate symbol of artistic integrity under pressure.
