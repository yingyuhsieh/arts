[01] Masaccio: Figures on Solid Ground  (00:00–00:48)
In Florence’s Brancacci Chapel, Adam covers his face while Eve cries out. Their feet press against the ground, and one light strikes both bodies. The grief feels physical rather than ornamental.
Masaccio lived only into his twenties. In less than a decade of work, he gave painted figures weight and made a wall seem to open into space. We will follow those bodies and spaces through his short career, keeping his collaborations and the uncertain edges of his story in view.

[02] From San Giovanni to Florence  (00:48–01:36)
Masaccio was born Tommaso di ser Giovanni in 1401, in what is now San Giovanni Valdarno. His father was a notary. The details of his apprenticeship remain uncertain, but by 1422 he had registered with a Florentine guild.
Florence was testing new ways to represent ancient forms, human bodies, and geometric space. Masaccio did not invent perspective on his own. He brought these experiments into sacred painting, where saints and ordinary people seem to inhabit the same measurable world.

[03] The San Giovenale Triptych  (01:36–02:24)
The triptych dated 1422 still uses a gold ground and pointed Gothic frames. The Virgin sits at the center while the saints approach her from the wings. The traditional format remains, but the figures begin to change within it.
Look at the throne’s angle and the volume of the Virgin and Child beneath their clothing. They occupy space instead of lying flat against the gold. This is not yet the fully developed style of the Brancacci frescoes. It shows a young painter looking for a firm center of gravity.

[04] Masaccio and Masolino  (02:24–03:12)
Masaccio worked with the older painter Masolino. Place Masolino’s Temptation beside Masaccio’s Expulsion in the Brancacci Chapel. One scene is quiet and softly outlined. In the other, bodies move under strong light, and emotion runs through muscle and gesture.
They are different solutions to a shared story. Masolino should not become a mere foil for a supposedly inevitable advance. Their collaboration also complicates attribution: a commissioned cycle can contain more than one hand, and later painters completed parts of the chapel.

[05] Virgin and Child with Saint Anne  (03:12–04:00)
Saint Anne stands behind the Virgin, who holds the Child in front of her. Masaccio and Masolino collaborated on this panel, though scholars still discuss the division of labor. The substantial Virgin and Child are often associated with Masaccio’s contribution.
Study the Child’s legs, the Virgin’s supporting hand, and the light across their faces. The gold ground remains solemn, but these bodies are more than elegant contours. They have mass and seem to project toward us like sculpture.

[06] The Brancacci Chapel  (04:00–04:48)
The Saint Peter cycle in the Brancacci Chapel was not painted by Masaccio alone. Masolino also worked there. After a long interruption, Filippino Lippi completed parts of the unfinished program. The chapel we see today is the result of work across decades.
A fresco belongs to a wall rather than a movable museum panel. Window light, wall position, and the viewer’s movement shape the order of the story. Within this actual space, Masaccio arranged three moments of time in The Tribute Money.

[07] The Tribute Money  (04:48–05:36)
At the center of The Tribute Money, a tax collector confronts Christ and the apostles. Christ points toward the lake, and Peter echoes the gesture. On the left Peter finds a coin in a fish; on the right he pays the collector. Three moments share one landscape.
Gazes and hands guide us through the story without dividing it into three framed scenes. Light comes from one direction and the figures cast shadows. Hills and buildings hold the setting together. Narrative, light, and perspective serve the same purpose.

[08] Expulsion from Eden  (05:36–06:24)
Adam covers his face with both hands. Eve opens her mouth and leans forward as an angel drives them from Eden. Masaccio needs little landscape to explain their state of mind. Bare bodies, harsh light, and their steps carry the loss.
Later additions once covered their nudity, and restoration revealed the bodies again. What we see has its own history of alteration and repair. The scene stays with us because the weight of the figures brings a biblical event close to ordinary bodily experience.

[09] The Baptism of the Neophytes  (06:24–07:12)
Saint Peter baptizes a convert while another man waits behind him, holding his arms close as though shivering in the cold. That small gesture gives the ritual a temperature we can imagine.
Light models shoulders, chests, and knees, placing the figures in a shared outdoor world. The bodily detail does not diminish the sacred event. It makes the change of faith feel as though it happens to real people who can feel water and cold.

[10] Saint Peter in the City Street  (07:12–08:00)
In Saint Peter Healing with His Shadow, a miracle unfolds along a street that resembles Florence. Peter and his companion walk past people waiting beside the buildings. His moving shadow links the bodies and the story.
The Distribution of Alms and the Death of Ananias brings charity and deceit into the same community. Masaccio positions people against architectural edges so the religious episode becomes more than a close view of saints. The city organizes a crowd and its moral choices.

[11] The Pisa Altarpiece  (08:00–08:48)
In 1426 Masaccio made a large polyptych for the Carmelite church in Pisa. It was later dismantled, and its panels now live in different museums. No single photograph can restore the original experience of the whole altar.
The Adoration of the Magi belonged to its long, low predella. Figures line up across firm ground, while hills and horses set the depth. A Gothic framework could still contain bodies that have weight and movement. These dispersed fragments make us reconstruct a lost whole from what survived.

[12] Bodies in the Pisa Altarpiece  (08:48–09:36)
In the central Virgin and Child, Mary sits firmly on her throne. The halos tilt in space, and the throne’s steps and sides tell us how it is built. Mother and Child have palpable volume.
The Crucifixion belonged above the central panel, where a viewer would look up from below. Christ’s body and the Magdalen’s raised arms gain force from that intended position. The two panels are now far apart. Seeing them together helps us understand how Masaccio designed figures for different levels of one altar.

[13] The Limits of Attribution  (09:36–10:24)
The Story of Saint Julian belongs to the context of the Carnesecchi altarpiece. Its similar date does not make it a panel of the Pisa altarpiece. A surviving panel with Saints Jerome and John the Baptist points to Masaccio’s late Roman work, where dating, collaboration, and attribution need careful wording.
His surviving output is small, and losses and dismantling complicate the list. Attribution means weighing the objects, documents, and technical evidence rather than attaching a certain name to every fragment.

[14] The Holy Trinity  (10:24–11:12)
In Santa Maria Novella, the painted vault of The Holy Trinity appears to recede into the actual wall. Christ on the cross, God the Father, Mary, and John occupy an ordered architectural space. The donors kneel near the edge, close to our own level.
Our eyes then move downward to a skeleton and a reminder of death. The force of the fresco lies not only in its geometry. Spatial order turns looking into a meditation on mortality and salvation. Scholars still differ on its exact date within the mid to late 1420s.

[15] Perspective, Light, and the Body  (11:12–12:00)
Compare The Holy Trinity with The Tribute Money. Architectural perspective draws our gaze into one wall; hills, gestures, and buildings order time across the other. They do not apply one visual formula.
What they share is weight in the figures, a direction to the light, and a relationship between painted space and the place where we stand. Masaccio absorbed Florence’s experiments in geometry and sculpture, then put them to work in different sacred narratives. Their apparent modernity grows from these concrete choices.

[16] From Florence to Rome  (12:00–12:48)
Masaccio went to Rome in the late 1420s, possibly continuing to work with Masolino. He died around 1428. The precise date and cause of death are unknown. Parts of the Brancacci project remained unfinished and were completed by others much later.
A short life invites a legend of the solitary genius, but missing documents should not be filled with dramatic guesses. The paintings give us firmer ground: from a gold-ground triptych to city streets, shivering bodies, and an opened architectural wall, he kept testing the relation between people and their world.

[17] A School for Later Painters  (12:48–13:36)
After Masaccio’s death, the Brancacci Chapel remained a place where later artists studied bodies, groups, and space. He is often placed at the beginning of Renaissance painting, with one useful qualification: he also extended Giotto’s sense of weight and worked beside Masolino, sculptors, and architects.
When you look at Masaccio, start with the ground under a figure’s feet. Follow the light, then see how people meet in a shared setting. Return to Adam and Eve or the shivering convert. Geometry makes the space credible; bodies make the story felt. This is Our Famous Artists. Thank you for watching.
