A dentist, an artist’s sister, and a three-tined pitchfork: the real story behind Grant Wood’s *American Gothic* is far stranger—and more carefully constructed—than its familiar image suggests.

Painted in 1930, *American Gothic* is often mistaken for a portrait of a stern farming couple. In reality, the models were Wood’s dentist, Dr. Byron H. McKeeby, and his sister, Nan Wood Graham. They posed separately, never lived in the house, and were not married. Wood later described the painted characters as a farmer and his daughter.

The house came first. Wood spotted the Dibble House in Eldon, Iowa, and was fascinated by its Carpenter Gothic window—a pointed arch borrowed from European church architecture and placed on an ordinary wooden home. From that architectural contrast, he imagined the people who might inhabit it.

This episode reveals how the three-tined pitchfork became the painting’s geometric skeleton. Its vertical prongs echo the man’s overalls, striped shirt, long nose, the house’s siding, and the pointed window behind him. We also follow the psychological tension created by two opposing gazes: the man confronts us directly while the woman looks beyond the frame, leaving the story unresolved.

Was Wood mocking the rigidity of small-town America, or honoring Midwestern discipline and resilience? The painting supports both readings. Its precise surfaces owe much to Northern Renaissance masters, while its compressed forms and repeated lines work with the clarity of modern graphic design. Painted in oil on Beaver Board and measuring only 78 × 65.3 centimeters, this surprisingly small object became one of the largest symbols in American visual culture.

We also examine why its simple formula—two figures, one upright tool, and a peaked house—has inspired endless parodies. In 1942, photographer Gordon Parks transformed that formula in *American Gothic, Washington, D.C.*, portraying government charwoman Ella Watson with a mop and broom before the American flag. His image reopened the question at the heart of Wood’s painting: who is allowed to stand for America, and who remains outside the frame?
