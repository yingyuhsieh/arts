How much work goes into making a portrait look effortless? Explore Ingres's Comtesse d'Haussonville, completed in 1845, and discover the drawings, revisions, and unexpected visual choices behind its seemingly casual grace.

Our Famous Artists takes a close look at Louise de Broglie leaning beside a mantel, meeting our gaze in a shimmering blue-gray dress. Opera glasses, calling cards, a blue evening bag, and a yellow shawl suggest a life of theater visits and social calls. Together, they create the impression of a return from the opera, a scene to imagine rather than a documented evening.

The composition developed between 1842 and 1845, through revisions and interruptions. The Frick estimates that Ingres made around eighty preparatory drawings. An abandoned early oil version reverses the pose and lacks the final mirror; a drawing places the shawl around her shoulders before it finds its apparently casual position on the chair.

We follow the portrait's lines from the hand beneath her chin to the satin folds and reflected red ribbon. The unusually low right arm reveals a tension between anatomy and compositional harmony. Critic Christopher Knight's analysis of the mirror adds another way to examine how convincing appearances can depart from expected spatial relationships.

Louise's life extends beyond the image. Madame de Staël's granddaughter later published books on Robert Emmet and Lord Byron, anonymously at her husband's insistence, according to the Frick. Her direct gaze invites renewed attention when we learn that history, without revealing exactly what she was thinking.

The painting remained in the family before the Frick Collection acquired it in 1927. In 1986, it reunited with Ingres's portrait of Louise's sister-in-law, the Princesse de Broglie, for their first joint display since 1867.
