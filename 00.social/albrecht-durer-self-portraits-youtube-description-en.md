What happens when the artist looks back at you? Explore Albrecht Dürer's self-portraits, where a face becomes a place to investigate artistic skill, social standing, faith, and the vulnerable human body.

Our Famous Artists follows Dürer from his 1484 silverpoint drawing at thirteen to the commanding frontal portrait of 1500. A later inscription on the childhood drawing records his use of a mirror; a 1493 sheet of studies brings his face together with a hand and a pillow. Looking at himself was also a way of learning how to represent the world.

His three celebrated surviving independent painted self-portraits reveal different forms of self-presentation. The plant in the 1493 Portrait of the Artist Holding a Thistle invites discussion of fidelity, faith, and a possible betrothal context. In 1498, fine clothing, gloves, and a distant landscape present an artist with the bearing of a gentleman. In 1500, the frontal pose and fur-trimmed robe bring an individual likeness into dialogue with images of Christ, artistic creation, and humanist ideals.

Drawings of his own body widen the story. The nude self-portrait, often dated around 1509, and the small drawing identifying a painful spot show how self-representation can address physical experience. The sick self-portrait's precise function remains uncertain. We also consider the 1522 Man of Sorrows self-image, recorded as a wartime loss in a 2021 exhibition catalogue.

The journey reaches later responses, including Thomas Struth's photographic self-portrait before Dürer's 1500 painting in 2000. Across these encounters, Dürer's images offer a rich comparison with the ways we choose to present ourselves today.
