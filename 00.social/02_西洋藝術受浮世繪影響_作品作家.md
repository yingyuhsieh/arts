A ship appears on the horizon. But has anyone aboard noticed the people waving from the raft? In Théodore Géricault’s The Raft of the Medusa, hope is barely a speck, while suffering fills a canvas almost five meters high and more than seven meters wide.

Join Our Famous Artists as we explore the 1816 disaster behind this landmark of French Romanticism. After the frigate Méduse ran aground during a French colonial expedition to Senegal, around 150 people were left adrift on an improvised raft. When the Argus found them, only fifteen remained alive; five more died after rescue. Géricault drew on survivors’ accounts and extensive studies to bring their ordeal before the public at the 1819 Paris Salon.

Follow the painting’s two overlapping pyramids, from the heavy bodies in the foreground to the man raising a cloth toward the distant ship. Discover how scale, storm clouds, sculptural figures, and opposing gestures keep rescue uncertain. We also consider the prominence of the Black figure at the summit, the distinction between the model Joseph and the survivor Jean-Charles, and the painting’s racial and political implications.

Now in the Louvre, this monumental work gives abandoned victims the scale once reserved for heroes. Watch with us to see how Géricault holds hope and irreversible loss within the same view, and subscribe to Our Famous Artists for more close readings of great art.
