Stand at the feet of the dead Christ, then look up into a sky painted on a ceiling. Discover how Andrea Mantegna used perspective to shape the viewer's position—and turn that position into an emotional encounter.

Our Famous Artists explores the Italian Renaissance master through his sculptural figures, classical imagination, and carefully constructed spaces. From his early formation in Padua to his career at the Gonzaga court in Mantua, Mantegna brought sharp contours and solid bodies into scenes of devotion, power, and myth.

A comparison with Giovanni Bellini's Agony in the Garden reveals different approaches to a shared subject: Mantegna's angular rocks and firm forms beside Bellini's warmer dawn. Their family ties also connected two distinctive artistic worlds. In the San Zeno Altarpiece, painted architecture and the physical frame work together to suggest a sacred space extending beyond the church wall.

The Camera degli Sposi, also known as the Camera Picta, expands the illusion to an entire room. Its painted oculus opens onto blue sky, with figures looking down and a precariously placed plant pot heightening our awareness of the space overhead. Lamentation over the Dead Christ reverses that experience, bringing the body close through dramatic foreshortening and adjusted proportions that keep the face visible.

Courtly subjects add further dimensions. The nine canvases of The Triumphs of Caesar reconstruct an imagined ancient procession. Madonna della Vittoria joins thanksgiving with the contested memory of the Battle of Fornovo. Parnassus and Minerva Expelling the Vices bring classical mythology and moral allegory into Isabella d'Este's private study. Prints such as Battle of the Sea Gods carried Mantegna's imagery beyond Italy; Dürer copied it in 1494.
