That yellow shawl looks as though someone has just tossed it onto a chair. Ingres had tried putting it around her shoulders first. A small change helps make years of preparation look like a passing moment.

Completed in 1845, Comtesse d'Haussonville presents Louise de Broglie beside a mantel, one hand raised to her chin. Her apparent ease rewards a second look: the portrait's naturalness emerges through carefully developed choices.

🎭 🧣 A room suggests an evening
Opera glasses, calling cards, and a blue evening bag help establish her social world. The Frick describes the scene as though she has returned from the opera. These objects invite that imagined story without recording a particular night. The shawl on the chair makes the room feel inhabited and the pose less formal.

✏️ 🎨 Three years behind a moment
Work developed from 1842 to 1845, with revisions and interruptions, including Louise's pregnancy. The Frick estimates around eighty preparatory drawings. An abandoned early oil version reverses her pose and has no mirror behind her. The finished composition conceals a long process of testing relationships among body, dress, and room.

🪞 👁️ Follow the lines closely
Trace the right arm crossing her body toward its shoulder. The Frick identifies its unusually low placement as a compositional adjustment. In a 2009 review, Christopher Knight also questioned the visibility of the reflected hand gesture. His observation offers a critical reading of the mirror. Together, these details invite us to consider how flowing lines can remain persuasive when anatomical or spatial relationships become puzzling.

📚 💬 A life beyond the satin
Louise was Madame de Staël's granddaughter and later wrote books about Robert Emmet and Lord Byron. According to the Frick, her husband insisted on anonymous publication. Her literary life adds depth to our encounter with the portrait. We can see intelligence in her thoughtful pose without assuming that we know her thoughts—or that Ingres deliberately concealed her interests.

🏛️ 🤝 A family reunion in a museum
The portrait stayed in the family before the Frick Collection acquired it in 1927. In 1986, it was shown beside Ingres's portrait of Louise's sister-in-law, the Princesse de Broglie. The two had last appeared together in the 1867 memorial exhibition for the artist. Their reunion offered a rare comparison of distinct presences within the same family.

Next time a painting feels spontaneous, linger over an accessory, a sleeve, or a reflection. Apparent ease can hold a remarkable history of decisions.
