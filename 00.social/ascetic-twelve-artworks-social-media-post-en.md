I looked up one English word—and ended up exploring twelve artworks.

The word was ascetic: someone who practices self-discipline and restraint, often for spiritual reasons. Its Greek roots connect with exercise and training. Soon, a vocabulary question had become a journey through monsters, wilderness, quiet rooms, and candlelight.

👀 📖 A word changes the way we look
An ascetic and a hermit can be the same person, but the words emphasize different things: discipline and solitude. In these twelve works of Western Christian art, that difference helps us notice what each figure is doing—and where their attention goes.

👹 🧭 Finding a center amid temptation
Michelangelo surrounds Anthony with monsters pulling him through the air, yet the saint’s face remains composed. Bosch spreads the trials across a crowded triptych. Follow its strange creatures, then find Anthony again: our wandering gaze becomes part of the experience of looking.

🌿 🕯️ Making room for prayer
Bellini’s Francis opens his hands toward the light in a landscape alive with plants and distant settlements. Zurbarán draws us close to rough cloth, a skull, and a face half hidden in shadow. The same saint’s choice of poverty gives two artists very different ways to guide our attention.

✍️ 🍞 Study, effort, and sharing
Jerome appears four times: through Leonardo’s tense anatomy, Dürer’s patient engraving, El Greco’s compressed space, and La Tour’s aging body. Then Velázquez takes us to Anthony’s meeting with Paul the Hermit, where a raven’s bread belongs to a sacred story of hospitality. Ribera brings Paul close enough for us to feel his physical vulnerability. Solitude still leaves room for food, work, and another person.

🖼️ 🔎 Different stories need different care
Zurbarán’s Serapion brings us to martyrdom and violence inflicted by others. La Tour’s Magdalen draws on a later tradition of repentance. Keeping those contexts distinct makes the details more revealing: in the New York Magdalen, two flames come from one candle and its reflection. Her mirror holds light rather than her face.

What began as a definition became a way of looking at hands, fabric, books, and light—the visible forms artists give to inward commitments.
