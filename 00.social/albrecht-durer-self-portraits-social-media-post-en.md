Before you meet Dürer's eyes, look at his hands. One holds a plant, another wears a glove, and another points to pain. Each changes what his self-image can communicate.

Born in Nuremberg in 1471, Albrecht Dürer returned to his own likeness in drawings and paintings. These works invite us to follow an artist exploring how he sees, how others see him, and what an image can reveal about a life.

🪞 ✏️ A mirror becomes a workshop
In 1484, thirteen-year-old Dürer drew himself in silverpoint, a medium whose lines are difficult to erase. He later added an inscription recording that he had worked from a mirror. His 1493 studies of a face, hand, and pillow extend that inquiry: his own features and an ordinary object could both teach him about shape, shadow, and volume.

🌿 🤲 A plant leaves the story open
In the 1493 painted portrait, he holds Eryngium, often called a thistle in the work's English title. A possible betrothal context connects the plant with fidelity, while religious interpretations also enter the discussion. The uncertainty matters. A carefully constructed image can carry several meanings without giving us a complete account of its maker's intentions.

🧤 🏔️ Hands dressed for recognition
The 1498 self-portrait presents fine clothing, gloves, and a window opening onto distant scenery. The inscription identifies him as twenty-six. His working hands appear in the attire of a gentleman, helping connect artistic skill with social dignity. The portrait invites recognition of the painter's knowledge and standing.

👁️ 🎨 A face meets sacred imagery
In 1500, Dürer adopts a frontal arrangement associated with images of the Savior. His fingers touch the fur of his robe; his name and age accompany the likeness. Imitation of Christ, creative ability, and humanist models offer ways of interpreting the image. Its visual relationship to sacred art is clear; its meanings continue to invite discussion.

🖐️ 💛 A hand points to vulnerability
The nude self-portrait and the small sick self-portrait bring the body into focus. In the latter, a finger and written note identify a painful spot. It may have helped communicate symptoms to a physician, although its precise purpose remains uncertain. Self-representation could make physical difficulty visible as well as project confidence.

In 2000, Thomas Struth photographed himself looking at Dürer's 1500 portrait. That encounter gives us another way into the story: our own position as viewers becomes part of the image.
