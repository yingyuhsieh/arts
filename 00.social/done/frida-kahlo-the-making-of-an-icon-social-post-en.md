🌿 Why is Frida Kahlo still one of the most recognizable artists in the world, more than seventy years after her death?

If you are in London, there is a particularly timely reason to revisit that question. Tate Modern is presenting Frida: The Making of an Icon, running from 25 June 2026 to 3 January 2027.

The exhibition asks a fascinating question:

How did Frida Kahlo become not only an artist, but a global cultural icon?

Rather than treating Kahlo only through a conventional retrospective, the exhibition considers her artworks, photographs, personal objects, and her continuing cultural afterlife—showing how Frida has been repeatedly interpreted and reinvented far beyond the boundaries of art history. 🎨🌎

My latest video approaches that larger story through one extraordinary painting:

Self-Portrait with Thorn Necklace and Hummingbird (1940).

🌵🩸 Why turn pain into a “necklace”?

The thorns inevitably recall the Crown of Thorns from Christian imagery. But Kahlo makes a crucial transformation: instead of placing them on her head, she wraps them around her neck like jewelry.

The thorns pierce her skin and draw blood.

Yet she does not cry, scream, or even frown.

🐦⚫ Why can the hummingbird no longer fly?

A real hummingbird is a creature of extraordinary speed and vitality. Kahlo does the opposite: its wings are spread, but the bird hangs motionless from her chest like a lifeless black pendant.

Within Mexican cultural contexts, the hummingbird can evoke love, luck, and Huitzilopochtli, the Mexica god of war and the sun. But there is no evidence that Kahlo assigned this image one fixed meaning.

🐒💔 Companion—or source of pain?

Kahlo really did live with monkeys, and they appear repeatedly in her art. But here the monkey is touching and pulling at the thorns already piercing her skin.

A popular interpretation connects the monkey with Diego Rivera. It is compelling, but not proven by sufficient primary evidence.

Perhaps the more interesting point is that Kahlo allows intimacy, companionship, and harm to exist simultaneously.

🐈👁️ The frightening thing may be the waiting.

The monkey moves. The thorns pierce her skin. The hummingbird remains completely still. The black cat waits behind her.

And yet the calmest figure in the entire painting is Frida herself.

🦋🌱 Beyond death, the painting is full of life.

Butterfly-like ornaments and dragonflies appear around her hair, while dense vegetation fills the background.

The plants suggest abundant life, yet they also block the sky and horizon, pushing Frida toward the front of the picture.

Vitality and oppression exist at the same time.

👁️ Finally, look again at Frida’s eyes.

She does not look at the monkey.

She does not look at the black cat.

She does not look at her wounds.

She does not even look at the hummingbird.

She is looking at us.

And this may be where the painting connects most powerfully with Tate Modern’s The Making of an Icon.

Frida could not completely control her body, her injuries, or the relationships in her life. But on the canvas, she could decide where the thorns would go, how the hummingbird would hang, how the black cat would wait, how the monkey would touch her—and, ultimately, how we would see Frida Kahlo.

She allows us to see the wound, but she does not ask for our pity.

Perhaps the making of “Frida the icon” had already begun inside these self-portraits. 🌺
