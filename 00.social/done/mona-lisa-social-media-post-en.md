What if the most important image in a museum were suddenly replaced by an empty wall? 🖼️

On August 21, 1911, Vincenzo Peruggia—a former Louvre worker and Italian glassworker—removed the Mona Lisa from the museum. The painting remained missing for more than two years. Yet its absence created an extraordinary paradox: crowds came to look at the vacant space, while newspapers carried Lisa’s face far beyond the art world. The theft did not invent the painting’s artistic importance, but it transformed an admired Renaissance portrait into a global media celebrity.

📏🌳 **A small and fragile icon**  
The physical object is remarkably intimate: oil on a poplar panel measuring only 79.4 × 53.4 cm (31.26 × 21.02 in). Wood expands and contracts as humidity changes, so the famous face depends on a carefully controlled microclimate, protective glazing, and strict limits on travel.

📜👤 **A woman, not a conspiracy**  
A handwritten note dated October 1503 records Leonardo working on the “head of Lisa del Giocondo.” Together with other historical evidence, it supports the identification of the sitter as Lisa Gherardini, wife of Florentine silk merchant Francesco del Giocondo—not a secret lover or a disguised self-portrait.

🎨👁️ **A smile made through seeing**  
Leonardo softened hard boundaries around the eyes and mouth with sfumato, building shadows through exceptionally thin translucent layers. Modern visual science offers one explanation for the changing smile: blurred, low-frequency shadows register more strongly in peripheral vision, so her expression seems to shift when our gaze moves.

🔍🤝 **The master was not alone**  
Infrared examination of the Prado Museum’s workshop version revealed underlying revisions that correspond to changes in the Louvre painting. A later copyist could not have seen those buried adjustments. The evidence suggests a pupil painted alongside Leonardo as the composition developed—a glimpse of Renaissance collaboration behind the myth of solitary genius.

📰🚂 **The theft that amplified a legend**  
Peruggia claimed he wanted to return the painting to Italy, mistakenly believing Napoleon had stolen it. After he surfaced in Florence in December 1913, the work was recovered and returned to its Louvre display on January 4, 1914. It came back with a new identity: not merely a masterpiece, but a face shared by mass culture.
