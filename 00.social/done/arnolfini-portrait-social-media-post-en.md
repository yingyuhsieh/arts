What if the most famous answers about the *Arnolfini Portrait* are not facts, but interpretations repeated until they began to sound certain? 🪞🎨

Jan van Eyck painted this modest oak panel in Bruges in 1434. Nearly six centuries later, viewers still call it a wedding portrait, identify the woman as pregnant, and translate every dog, candle, shoe, and orange into a fixed symbol. Yet current evidence invites a more interesting question: how did van Eyck make a selected, altered, and staged world feel more convincing than reality?

👥📜 **A double portrait, not a proven wedding**

The National Gallery considers Giovanni di Nicolao di Arnolfini the most plausible identity of the man, but the attribution is not absolute and the woman remains unknown. Their joined hands and his raised gesture may relate to marriage, but they do not prove a ceremony. The earliest reliable inventory, from 1516, simply describes Arnolfini and his wife in a room.

👗🌿 **The “pregnancy” that clothing created**

The woman gathers a large quantity of fur-lined green fabric before her body. Similar silhouettes appear in fifteenth-century images of women who are not shown as pregnant. Fertility may still belong to the picture’s social world, but her body cannot be used as evidence of an actual pregnancy. Precision matters: an association is not a diagnosis.

🪞👁️ **A mirror that changes the act of looking**

The convex mirror reflects the couple from behind—and admits two visitors from the space where we stand. Ten tiny scenes of Christ’s Passion circle its frame. Above it, van Eyck writes, “Johannes de eyck fuit hic. 1434”: “Jan van Eyck was here.” The reflected figure cannot be confirmed as a self-portrait, yet mirror and inscription together make artistic presence impossible to ignore.

✨🖐️ **Realism built through light and touch**

Van Eyck did not invent oil painting, but he used layered oil glazes with exceptional control. Fur absorbs light, brass reflects it, glass transmits it, and orange peel appears rough. Technical research even records a thumbprint near the dog’s leg—a physical trace accompanying the written declaration of presence.

🔬🐕 **The room changed while he painted**

Infrared reflectography shows that the dog, chandelier, candle, chair, beads, shoes, and several smaller objects were absent from the visible initial underdrawing. Van Eyck revised faces, clothing, feet, and furnishings as the work developed. These additions complicate the idea of a symbolic marriage program fixed from the start. What survives is something richer: a sequence of artistic decisions.
