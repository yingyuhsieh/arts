Van Gogh’s Sunflowers is not a single painting—it is a seven-canvas story of friendship, radical color, repetition, loss, and memory. Why, then, can the public see only five of them today?

This video follows all seven Arles vase paintings created from 1888 to early 1889. Four emerged during an intense burst of work in August 1888, while Van Gogh was preparing the Yellow House for Paul Gauguin and dreaming of a “Studio of the South.” The flowers were more than still lifes: they were decorations for a friend’s room and an invitation written in color.

The earliest paintings begin with blue grounds and relatively few blooms. Van Gogh then pushes the experiment toward the celebrated yellow-on-yellow compositions now in Munich and London. With a limited palette, emphatic outlines, and thick impasto, he transforms buds, open flowers, seed heads, and collapsing petals into a portrait of time.

The story also follows the very different fate of each canvas. F453 remains privately owned and has not been publicly exhibited since 1948. F459, once owned in Japan, was destroyed during the bombing of Ashiya in 1945. Five surviving paintings entered museums in Munich, London, Amsterdam, Philadelphia, and Tokyo.

The later versions were not mechanical copies. Van Gogh reconsidered color, rhythm, and the character of individual flowers, and imagined pairing Sunflowers with La Berceuse as a triptych. Repetition became another form of invention.
