# Yayoi Kusama: From Childhood Hallucinations to Infinity Mirror Rooms

Yayoi Kusama transformed the patterns that once threatened to overwhelm her into one of the most recognizable visual languages in modern art.

Born in Matsumoto, Japan, in 1929, Kusama experienced vivid hallucinations from childhood: flowers, dots, and nets seemed to spread across rooms and engulf her body. Drawing became a means of survival. Over more than seventy years, she turned that private experience into paintings, sculpture, performance, film, public art, and immersive environments that invited the world to enter her vision.

This episode follows that development through her works. We begin with the dense lines of *Capturing Net* (1951) and the multiplying dots of *Accumulation* (1952), then move to New York, where the monumental *Infinity Nets* rejected the heroic gestures of Abstract Expressionism in favor of repetition, duration, and bodily labor.

In the 1960s, Kusama allowed repetition to escape the canvas. *Accumulation No. 1* covered an armchair with hand-sewn soft forms. *Aggregation: One Thousand Boats Show* multiplied a sculpture through hundreds of photographic reproductions. In *Infinity Mirror Room—Phalli’s Field* (1965), mirrors made finite objects appear endless—and placed the viewer inside the work.

We also revisit her unofficial intervention at the 1966 Venice Biennale, *Narcissus Garden*; her antiwar happenings and experimental film *Kusama’s Self-Obliteration*; her return to Japan; and the international reassessment that led to her official representation of Japan at Venice in 1993.

Later works—including the yellow *Pumpkin* on Naoshima, *The obliteration room*, *Fireflies on the Water*, *Filled with the Brilliance of Life*, *The Souls of Millions of Light Years Away*, *LOVE IS CALLING*, and *Longing for Eternity*—show that the mirror room was never a single formula. Kusama continually rewrote it to explore fear, identity, love, death, time, and the cosmos.

Her art leaves us with a distinctly contemporary paradox: spaces designed to destabilize the individual self have become some of the world’s most photographed places. When our image is repeated without end, do we become more visible—or do we disappear among the copies?

Kusama did not escape the patterns that once engulfed her. She drew them, sewed them, filmed them, reflected them, and ultimately learned to direct them.
