What if the most famous thing about a portrait—the title everyone remembers—was also the evidence of what history tried to erase? ✨

Gustav Klimt completed *Portrait of Adele Bloch-Bauer I* in 1907. Its gold is mesmerizing, but the woman inside it was never merely a decorative muse. Adele was a well-read Jewish patron in fin-de-siècle Vienna who supported social reform and women’s suffrage, while her salon welcomed figures such as Gustav Mahler, Arthur Schnitzler, Richard Strauss, and Stefan Zweig. The painting’s journey from named portrait to anonymous *Woman in Gold* turns beauty into a record of power, dispossession, and remembrance.

👁️✨ **A body dissolving into gold**

Klimt used actual gold and silver on the 138-centimeter-square canvas. Adele’s face and clasped hands remain startlingly real, while her chair, robe, and background merge into a depthless field of spirals, geometry, “AB” initials, and watchful eye-shaped forms. She seems at once present and on the verge of disappearing.

👑🟡 **A modern Byzantine icon**

After seeing the sixth-century mosaics of Empress Theodora in Ravenna in 1903, Klimt translated their frontal grandeur and shimmering golden ground into a new visual language. More than one hundred preparatory studies trace his transformation of a physically grounded modern woman into an almost sacred image.

📜⚫ **A theft followed by erasure**

After Nazi Germany annexed Austria in 1938, Adele’s husband Ferdinand fled and his property was systematically seized. The portrait entered the Belvedere in 1941 through coerced liquidation—not a voluntary gift. In Nazi-era and later museum display, Adele’s name faded behind the sanitized title *Woman in Gold*. The object remained visible while the Jewish family and cultural world behind it were pushed out of view.

⚖️🖼️ **A name restored**

Adele’s niece Maria Altmann fought Austria’s claim for years. The U.S. Supreme Court ruled in 2004 that her case could proceed, but it did not award her the paintings. Austrian arbitration did that in 2006, ordering five Klimt works returned to the heirs. Later that year, Neue Galerie New York acquired the portrait for a reported $135 million and displayed it under Adele’s name.
