🌀 A moon hangs in front of a tree. An iron sprouts fourteen tacks. A face multiplies through masks and mirrors. Surrealism begins at the instant when recognition falters—and the ordinary world stops obeying its own rules.

This close look at 27 works by 16 artists at the Taipei Fine Arts Museum moves beyond the idea that surrealism simply “illustrates dreams.” These artists unsettle function, identity, scale, and classification so that seeing becomes an active—and sometimes uncomfortable—process.

✍️🌱 Forms that think for themselves

In André Masson’s *Animals (Centaur)* (1944–1945), quick black lines allow a body to emerge and dissolve between human, animal, and plant. Yves Tanguy takes a different route: his precisely lit, nameless forms occupy landscapes that feel physically convincing but cannot be located. One lets chance generate life; the other gives the impossible the calm authority of reality.

🎁⚠️ The dangerous everyday

Man Ray’s *Gift* (1921/1974) turns a tool meant to smooth and protect cloth into an object that would tear it. René Magritte’s *The Sixteenth of September* (1957) makes an equally economical intervention, placing a distant moon in front of a nearby tree. Tiny changes overturn function and perspective—and expose how much trust we place in familiar objects and pictorial rules.

🎭🪞 Identity under construction

Claude Cahun’s self-portraits use cropped hair, theatrical dress, masks, and reflections to reject the idea of one “true” self. Decades later, Cindy Sherman stages historical portrait types until their femininity looks visibly manufactured, while ORLAN treats the face and body as structures shaped by art history, technology, and cultural power.

🦋🐊 A world beyond categories

Leonora Carrington’s *Mariposa Mantarraya* (2008) joins human, butterfly, and ray without asking which identity is primary. Her *The Other Is Always Right* (2010) turns failed classification into an ethical invitation: perhaps the creature is not irrational; perhaps our categories are too narrow. Wifredo Lam’s *The Lovers* (1970) similarly fuses masks, plants, and bodies into a shared field of energy.
