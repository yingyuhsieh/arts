What can six almost invisible letters tell us about the changing identity of a medieval artist?

This video follows Conrad von Soest through the brilliant visual world of International Gothic—from the 1403 Wildunger Altarpiece, with its thirteen-scene sacred theater, to the quieter emotional power of the Marienaltar around 1420. Along the way, we look closely at flowing drapery, saturated gold, blue, red and green, intimate gestures, everyday objects, and even an apostle wearing spectacles.

The story begins and ends with a tiny clue: the fragmentary letters “con..d,” noticed by Rolf Fritz in 1950 in the Death of the Virgin. Together with civic records and other evidence, that detail helps us understand why Conrad’s name matters—not as proof of a modern solitary genius, but as evidence that the identity, workshop and reputation of a medieval master were becoming increasingly visible.

Rather than judging Gothic painting by Renaissance perspective, this video offers another way to look: follow figures, drapery, color, bodily distance and familiar objects, then look for the traces of the artist behind the work.
