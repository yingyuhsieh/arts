What can a thief, an empty wall, and a mysterious smile tell us about the making of a global icon? Follow the Mona Lisa from Leonardo da Vinci’s workshop to the 1911 heist that sent her face around the world.

This is not simply a story about a famous smile. We look closely at the painting’s surprisingly intimate scale—just 79.4 × 53.4 cm (31.26 × 21.02 in)—and the fragile poplar panel beneath its paint. We trace the archival evidence identifying the sitter as Lisa Gherardini, wife of the Florentine silk merchant Francesco del Giocondo, and examine how Leonardo used a turning pose, extraordinarily thin layers of glaze, and sfumato to make a still image seem alive.

The investigation also reveals a studio discovery hidden in plain sight: infrared imaging of the Prado Museum’s version uncovered matching changes beneath the surface, suggesting that a pupil worked beside Leonardo as the composition evolved.

Then, on August 21, 1911, former Louvre worker Vincenzo Peruggia removed the painting from the museum. Newspapers reproduced her face, visitors queued to see the vacant space, and a work already admired by artists and writers became a global mass-media phenomenon. By the time it returned to the Louvre display on January 4, 1914, the Mona Lisa had become far more than a Renaissance portrait.
