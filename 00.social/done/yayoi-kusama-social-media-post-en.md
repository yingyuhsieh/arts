# Yayoi Kusama: She Turned Fear into Infinity

🔴 **Before the dots became a global icon, they were a way to survive.**

As a child in Matsumoto, Yayoi Kusama experienced flowers, nets, and dots spreading beyond their surfaces and engulfing rooms—and even her own body. She began drawing what she saw. Art was not initially a career plan. It was a way to transfer an uncontrollable inner experience onto paper.

🕸️ **One small mark became an entire world.**

The dense lines of *Capturing Net* (1951), the multiplying dots of *Accumulation* (1952), and the monumental *Infinity Nets* of her New York years share the same logic: repeat a tiny unit until the local becomes limitless. Unlike the heroic gestures of Abstract Expressionism, her marks had no center or climax. They recorded time, fatigue, and the rhythm of a working body.

🪑 **Then repetition escaped the canvas.**

In *Accumulation No. 1* (1962), hand-sewn soft forms covered an armchair. In *One Thousand Boats Show* (1963), a sculpture’s photograph multiplied across the walls and ceiling. With *Infinity Mirror Room—Phalli’s Field* (1965), mirrors transformed a finite number of objects into an apparently endless landscape—and reflected the viewer into it.

🪞 **The mirror room was never merely a beautiful backdrop.**

An ordinary mirror confirms that “I am here.” Kusama’s opposing mirrors reproduce that “I” until it becomes difficult to locate. This makes the twenty-first-century popularity of mirror-room selfies wonderfully paradoxical: a work designed to destabilize the ego becomes a place where visitors urgently document their own presence.

🎃 **The pumpkins and lights were not a break from her serious early art.**

Naoshima’s yellow *Pumpkin*, *The obliteration room*, *Fireflies on the Water*, *LOVE IS CALLING*, and the later cosmic mirror rooms continue a line stretching back more than seventy years. Private hallucination became shared environment; fear became form; repetition became a way to think about love, death, identity, and the universe.

Yayoi Kusama did not escape the patterns that once threatened to engulf her. She drew them, sewed them, filmed them, reflected them—and learned to direct them.
