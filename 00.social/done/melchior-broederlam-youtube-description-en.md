Before Jan van Eyck transformed northern painting through light, matter, and reflection, Melchior Broederlam opened a subtler door. His gold grounds still belong to the sacred world of International Gothic art, yet buildings begin to acquire interiors, landscapes become roads, and holy figures move through a world that can be entered and crossed.

This episode follows the Burgundian court painter through the surviving records of 1381–1409 and the two painted wings of the Crucifixion Altarpiece, now in the Musée des Beaux-Arts de Dijon. Commissioned by Philip the Bold for the Chartreuse de Champmol, the altarpiece was a collaborative, movable object: Jacques de Baerze carved its interior in Dendermonde, Broederlam painted and gilded it in Ypres, and the completed work was installed near Dijon in 1399.

Across the *Annunciation*, *Visitation*, *Presentation in the Temple*, and *Flight into Egypt*, gold and courtly elegance coexist with habitable architecture, bodily encounter, ritual space, directional landscape, and Joseph’s strikingly ordinary drink from a flask. These are not failed attempts at Renaissance perspective, but evidence of two visual systems overlapping as change unfolds.

Broederlam should not be valued only as a “bridge to van Eyck.” He was a major International Gothic court artist whose surviving work reveals how patronage, workshops, materials, transport, and visual exchange prepared the conditions for early Netherlandish painting.
