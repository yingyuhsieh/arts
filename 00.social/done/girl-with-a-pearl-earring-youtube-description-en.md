What if Vermeer’s most famous “portrait” is not a portrait—and its celebrated pearl may not be a pearl at all? Modern imaging reveals how *Girl with a Pearl Earring* became an encounter shaped by paint, perception, global trade, and more than three centuries of change.

Painted around 1665 and now held by the Mauritshuis in The Hague, this surprisingly intimate 44.5 × 39 cm canvas is a *tronie*: a study of character, expression, costume, and light rather than a record of an identified sitter. Follow the natural ultramarine in her headscarf back to lapis lazuli from the mountains of present-day Afghanistan, then look closely at the ear ornament—an astonishing optical illusion assembled from a thin gray wash and a few white highlights.

The 2018 *Girl in the Spotlight* project uncovered even more. The near-black background originally contained a folded green curtain. Tiny eyelashes once framed her eyes. Beneath the painting’s calm surface, broad brown-and-black strokes reveal Vermeer working with unexpected speed and force. Even the blue pigment continues to change chemically, making time part of the picture’s visible history.

The story continues from Vermeer’s studio to an 1881 auction, where the darkened painting sold for two guilders plus commission, and finally to the Mauritshuis, which received it as a bequest in 1903.
