For generations, Jan van Eyck’s *Arnolfini Portrait* has been explained as a wedding scene filled with fixed symbols: a pregnant bride, a faithful dog, sacred shoes, a divine candle. But how much of that familiar story can the evidence actually support?

Painted in Bruges in 1434, this small oak panel is better approached first as a double portrait—and as an extraordinary experiment in seeing. The man is most plausibly Giovanni di Nicolao di Arnolfini, while the woman remains unidentified. Her silhouette reflects costly fifteenth-century dress rather than proving pregnancy, and no reliable evidence confirms that a wedding ceremony is taking place.

We examine the convex mirror that opens the room behind the viewer, the audacious inscription “Johannes de eyck fuit hic”—“Jan van Eyck was here”—and the layers of oil paint that make fur, brass, glass, skin, and fruit respond differently to light. Infrared reflectography also reveals that the dog, chandelier, beads, chair, shoes, and other famous details were introduced as van Eyck revised the composition.

This is not a codebook waiting for one final solution. It is a carefully selected, altered, and staged world in which portraiture, trade, wealth, devotion, artistic presence, and the act of looking overlap.
