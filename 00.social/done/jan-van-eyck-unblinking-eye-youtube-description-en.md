Jan van Eyck could make a faint rosary shadow, a glint on polished metal, or a distant figure on a bridge feel worthy of permanent attention. Discover how this fifteenth-century master transformed close observation into a new way of seeing the visible—and the sacred—world.

“The unblinking eye” is a later metaphor for Van Eyck’s extraordinary powers of observation, not a name he used for himself. His surviving works ask us to move between scales: step back to understand their structure, then come close enough to see glass, fur, skin, jewels, inscriptions, and reflected light behave in entirely different ways.

Van Eyck first appears securely in records in 1422 as an established painter in The Hague. In 1425, he entered the service of Philip the Good, Duke of Burgundy, as court painter and valet de chambre. His role extended beyond the studio. In 1428–1429, he joined a diplomatic mission to Portugal and produced portraits of Isabella of Portugal for the duke’s proposed marriage. The lost portraits were only one element in a larger political negotiation, but they show how visual credibility could become a tool of international diplomacy.

Van Eyck did not invent oil painting. Oil-based media had been used in Europe before him. His achievement was to control opaque underlayers, transparent and translucent glazes, drying time, minute touches, and the behavior of light with remarkable precision. His realism was never simple copying: technical study shows that he revised figures and objects while building spaces that look convincing even when their scale and geometry are deliberately impossible.

The Ghent Altarpiece, completed in 1432, is the monumental center of his career. Its inscription states that Hubert van Eyck began the project and Jan completed it, although their exact division of labor remains debated. Closed, the polyptych presents a restrained world of the Annunciation, donors, prophets, and painted sculpture; opened, it erupts into color, music, landscape, and the adoration of the Mystic Lamb. Its history includes wartime seizure, Nazi looting, rescue from an Austrian salt mine, and the still-unsolved 1934 theft of the original Just Judges panel.

In the 1433 Portrait of a Man (Self Portrait?), a direct gaze meets the viewer beneath a red headdress. The original frame bears Van Eyck’s motto, ALS ICH KAN—“as I can” or “to the best of my ability”—a modest phrase that also plays on his name. The identification as a self-portrait is highly plausible, but not proven by an inscription.

The Arnolfini Portrait remains equally resistant to a single answer. It is not securely established as a wedding scene, and the woman’s gathered dress is not evidence of pregnancy. The convex mirror, imported oranges, dog, bed, candle, and famous inscription—Johannes de Eyck fuit hic. 1434, “Jan van Eyck was here”—can carry social, material, devotional, and narrative meanings at the same time.

From the impossible scale of Madonna in the Church to the aging face and spectacles of Canon van der Paele, Van Eyck made precise observation serve faith, memory, intimacy, and power. His achievement was not detail for its own sake. He organized detail so that looking became a form of thought.
