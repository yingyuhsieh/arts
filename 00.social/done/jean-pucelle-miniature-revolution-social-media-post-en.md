What if an artistic revolution could fit in the palm of your hand? 📖✨

Around 1324–1328, a tiny royal prayer book was made for Jeanne d’Évreux, Queen of France. Measuring just 9.2 × 6.2 centimeters, *The Hours of Jeanne d’Évreux* asks us to come close. Only then does its astonishing world appear: sacred figures modeled like ivory sculpture, rooms with unexpected depth, and margins crowded with rabbits, monkeys, musicians, beggars, and monsters.

🔍🏛️ **A master in the historical fog**

Jean Pucelle’s birth and training remain unknown. Documents place him in Paris from about 1319 to 1334, first designing a seal for a hospital confraternity. Even at that scale, his gift was already clear: compressing authority, faith, and narrative into a minuscule space.

🤝📜 **A workshop, not a lone genius**

Medieval manuscripts passed through many expert hands—from parchment makers and scribes to illuminators, gilders, and binders. Notes associated with the *Belleville Breviary*, along with the Bible copied by Robert de Billyng, preserve rare traces of Pucelle’s collaborators. These books were intricate micro-architectures built by teams.

🌬️🇮🇹 **The wind from Italy**

Pucelle gave Northern Gothic painting new weight and space. In the *Annunciation*, walls, arches, and furniture form a room that feels almost enterable, recalling innovations associated with Duccio and Giotto. Whether Pucelle personally traveled to Italy remains uncertain; the artistic dialogue is unmistakable.

⚪🎨 **Color restrained, vision intensified**

Instead of relying on lavish gold, Pucelle modeled figures through grisaille—subtle ink, tempera, and shades of gray. Tiny touches of blue, red, and flesh tone become electric. At the bottom of the page, solemn devotion gives way to comic drolleries and the recognizable warmth of daily life.

🔥🐇 A man warming his bare toes beside a fire, sausages drying overhead; a rabbit racing through the margin; a dove returning Saint Louis’s lost prayer book. Seven centuries later, these details make the Middle Ages feel startlingly close.
