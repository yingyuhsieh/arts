Frida Kahlo’s Self-Portrait with Thorn Necklace and Hummingbird (1940) looks calm at first glance. Look closer, and the image becomes a tightly controlled world of thorns, blood, a motionless hummingbird, a spider monkey, a watchful black cat, insects, and dense vegetation.

This video explores how Kahlo transformed lived experience into a visual language that resists a single explanation. Painted during a turbulent period around her 1939 divorce from Diego Rivera, her relationship with photographer Nickolas Muray, and her remarriage to Rivera in late 1940, the work can easily be read through biography—but it becomes much richer when we also consider religion, Mexican culture, nature, the body, and Kahlo’s deliberate construction of her own image.

We look closely at the thorn “necklace” and its connection to the Crown of Thorns; the hummingbird suspended between vitality and stillness; the monkey as both companion and possible source of pain; the black cat waiting behind her; and the plants, butterflies, and dragonflies that bring life into an image filled with injury and danger.

The central mystery is Frida herself. Her body is wounded, yet her face remains composed. She does not look at the thorns, the animals, or the blood. She looks directly at us.

Rather than giving us a dictionary of symbols, Kahlo creates contradictions—and controls how we encounter them. She allows us to see the wound without asking for pity.

Artwork: Frida Kahlo, *Untitled [Self-portrait with thorn necklace and hummingbird]*, 1940, Harry Ransom Center, The University of Texas at Austin.
