You know Van Gogh’s Sunflowers—but which one? 🌻🎨

The famous bouquet is not a single masterpiece. It belongs to a family of seven vase paintings made in Arles from 1888 to early 1889. Only five can now be seen in museums: one remains in a private collection, while another was destroyed in wartime. Together, the seven canvases tell a story much larger than a vase of flowers—a story of friendship, experiment, repetition, separation, and survival.

🏠💛 **A bouquet written as an invitation**
Van Gogh rented the Yellow House in Arles and dreamed of creating a “Studio of the South,” where artists could live and work together. While waiting for Paul Gauguin, he planned sunflower decorations for his guest room and imagined a “symphony in blue and yellow.” The flowers carried his excitement, admiration, and intense hope of building an artistic community.

🔵➡️🟡 **From blue contrast to yellow on yellow**
The first paintings use blue grounds and only a few large blooms. In the Munich and London canvases, Van Gogh moves toward an audacious yellow vase, yellow flowers, yellow table, and yellow background. Rather than flattening the image, the close shades make every outline, brushstroke, and shift in temperature more visible. A limited palette becomes a radical experiment.

🌱🌻 **A still life that contains time**
These are not seven identical bouquets. Buds, fully opened flowers, seed-heavy centers, and collapsing petals coexist in a single vase. Thick impasto gives petals and seed heads an almost sculptural surface. Van Gogh turns a brief flowering season into a portrait of growth, maturity, and decay.

🔥🖼️ **Two missing paintings**
F453, with three large flowers, has remained out of public view since 1948. F459—described as five flowers, with a sixth revealed after the support was enlarged—was bought by Koyata Yamamoto in 1920 and destroyed during the 1945 bombing of Ashiya. Historical images are now all that remain.

🔁🎼 **Repetition without duplication**
The three later versions in Philadelphia, Amsterdam, and Tokyo were reconsiderations, not mechanical copies. Van Gogh adjusted color, rhythm, and individual flower forms. He even imagined placing Sunflowers on either side of La Berceuse, transforming separate paintings into a comforting triptych.

For viewers in Asia, one of the five publicly displayed versions is at the SOMPO Museum of Art in Tokyo. Whether you encounter the series there, in London, Munich, Amsterdam, or Philadelphia, look beyond the yellow: compare the flowers’ life stages, follow the directional brushwork, and notice how repetition changes meaning.
