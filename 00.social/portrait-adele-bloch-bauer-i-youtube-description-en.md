Behind the dazzling gold of Gustav Klimt’s *Portrait of Adele Bloch-Bauer I* lies the story of a woman whose name was nearly erased—and a family’s decades-long pursuit of justice.

Completed in 1907, this radical portrait transforms Adele Bloch-Bauer, a prominent patron and participant in Vienna’s modern culture, into an icon suspended in gold and silver. We look closely at Klimt’s Byzantine inspiration, the painting’s eye-shaped motifs and mysteriously clasped hands, and the real woman who supported social reform, women’s suffrage, and a vibrant intellectual salon.

The story then moves beyond the canvas: Ferdinand Bloch-Bauer’s collection was seized after Nazi Germany annexed Austria in 1938, and Adele’s name gradually disappeared behind the anonymous title *Woman in Gold*. Decades later, her niece Maria Altmann challenged Austria’s claim to the paintings. The U.S. Supreme Court’s 2004 decision allowed her case to proceed; Austrian arbitration in 2006 ultimately ordered five Klimt works returned to the family.
