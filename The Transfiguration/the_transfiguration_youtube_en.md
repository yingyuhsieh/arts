## A. Video Core Narrative

*The Transfiguration* was Raphael's last large painting, and it compresses two questions — "Who is Christ?" and "What can Christ do for the suffering?" — into a single altarpiece split into upper and lower registers. This isn't decorative pairing; it's a deliberate viewing puzzle about who gets to see the whole picture. The figures inside the painting are each blinded by light or blinded by grief, while only the viewer standing outside the frame can hold both the summit's revelation and the valley's chaos in view at once. The painting itself was born out of rivalry: Cardinal Giulio de' Medici commissioned Raphael and Sebastiano del Piombo (backed by Michelangelo) to each paint an altarpiece for the same cathedral, and Raphael reworked a single-scene design into a two-register masterpiece as his rival's painting neared completion. The video follows the arc of commission → rivalry → the composition's evolution → close reading of the picture → the mystery of its unfinished passages → its afterlife, to explain why this single panel was regarded for nearly four centuries as the most famous oil painting in the Western canon.

## D. Detailed Segment Plan

### Segment 1 — Hook: Half Light, Half Cry

**Purpose**
Open on the painting's own visual contradiction to hook curiosity immediately, rather than starting with Raphael's birth date.

**Key Message**
The painting places divine revelation and human suffering in the same frame, forcing viewers to ask how light is supposed to answer pain.

**Content**
The upper half of the panel is almost weightless white light; the lower half is a crowd in crisis — a boy contorted in pain, a family pleading for help, onlookers questioning each other. This kind of vertical pairing is unusual in Italian religious art. Open by letting viewers feel that contrast before posing the question the whole video will circle back to: why did Raphael paint heaven's calm and earth's panic on the same board?

### Segment 2 — Two Cardinals' Painters: A Medici-Engineered Contest

**Purpose**
Establish the commission and the Raphael–Michelangelo rivalry so viewers understand the political and artistic motives behind the painting.

**Key Message**
The Transfiguration wasn't a solitary flash of inspiration — it was the product of a contest Cardinal Giulio de' Medici deliberately set up between two rival camps.

**Content**
The patron was Cardinal Giulio de' Medici (later Pope Clement VII), Archbishop of Narbonne from 1515. Around 1516 he commissioned both Raphael's Transfiguration and Sebastiano del Piombo's Raising of Lazarus, originally intended for the same cathedral in Narbonne. Michelangelo, an ally of Sebastiano, supplied designs for the main figures in Lazarus specifically to help Sebastiano outdo Raphael — turning the commission into a Renaissance *paragone*, a contest between drawing-and-sculptural body language and color-and-composition.

### Segment 3 — From One Scene to Two: How the Composition Grew

**Purpose**
Show that the painting wasn't decided in one sitting but evolved through multiple preparatory designs, while flagging the "nineteen figures added after seeing the rival's work" claim as inference rather than documented fact.

**Key Message**
Raphael originally planned only the summit scene; the boy and the crowd below were added later, producing today's two-register altarpiece.

**Content**
Earlier compositional studies (*modelli*) focused on the summit scene alone; a later design brought in the boy and crowd below. A two-scene modello now in the Louvre is usually associated with Raphael's assistant Gianfrancesco Penni and the workshop — more precisely, it may be a workshop copy of a lost design rather than an autograph sheet. Related studies also survive at the Albertina in Vienna, useful for visualizing how the composition changed. The idea that Raphael added nineteen figures after seeing Sebastiano's painting is a common scholarly inference, not something directly confirmed by surviving correspondence.

**Artwork / Person / Place Mentioned**

- Museum: Musée du Louvre; Albertina Museum
- City: Paris; Vienna

### Segment 4 — The Summit: Christ, Moses, Elijah, and the Blinded Apostles

**Purpose**
Guide a close reading of the upper register, showing how Raphael renders the abstract idea of divinity through composition and color.

**Key Message**
Raphael isn't painting ordinary light — he's turning composition, color, and posture into the visible form of Christ's divinity.

**Content**
Christ, in white robes, arms outstretched, rises weightlessly between cloud and light — with no throne or architecture supporting him, which is exactly how Raphael conveys divinity. Moses and Elijah lean inward on either side, representing Law and Prophets; Peter, James, and John crouch along the lower edge of the upper register, thrown down or shielding their eyes from the light. Whites, silvery blues, and pale violet dominate this half; the prophets' diagonal bodies, the apostles' curved postures, and Christ's vertical form all funnel the eye upward toward the source of light.

### Segment 5 — The Valley: The Boy, the Father, and the Twisting Woman

**Purpose**
Guide a close reading of the crowd below, carefully separating gospel text from later interpretation rather than settling on a single reading.

**Key Message**
The lower scene isn't decorative filler — it's a narratively charged human crisis: a family waiting to be rescued, while the viewer outside the frame can already see the answer arriving above.

**Content**
A father supports or presents his son to the apostles, pleading for help; the boy's body is contorted, mouth open, while figures around him reach, point, and question each other. On the left, a woman kneels with her body twisted, acting as the visual hinge connecting the different clusters of figures — often compared to the wrung-towel torsion of *figura serpentinata*. Most museum accounts read this as the apostles not yet able to heal the boy, waiting for Christ to descend; but the boy's upright, open-mouthed posture has also led some scholars to read the moment as healing already underway, so "not yet healed" shouldn't be treated as the only answer. Ochres, browns, deep greens, and dark umber dominate here, in strong contrast to the whites and silvery blues above.

- Historical Person: the boy tormented by an unclean spirit; the boy's father

### Segment 6 — Whose Hand? Unfinished Passages and the Workshop Question

**Purpose**
Explain the painting's state of completion at Raphael's death and the likely extent of workshop involvement, giving viewers an accurate sense of how a large Renaissance workshop actually operated.

**Key Message**
Rather than asking whether Raphael painted every inch himself, the better question is which layers he designed, which he executed, and which the workshop may have assisted with.

**Content**
When Raphael died on April 6, 1520, the painting was nearly finished, though some areas remain unfinished or show workshop involvement. Some scholarship holds that Raphael controlled the overall design, the upper sacred scene, and the painting's core; certain minor figures or passages in the lower register may involve Giulio Romano, Gianfrancesco Penni, or other workshop members — though the entire lower half can't simply be attributed to assistants. Public records on the panel's support also disagree; this plan conservatively describes it as oil tempera on wood rather than treating a later transfer to canvas as an established fact — a confusion that likely stems from the support-transfer history of the companion painting, The Raising of Lazarus, from the same commission.

**Artwork / Person / Place Mentioned**
- Artist: Raphael; Giulio Romano; Gianfrancesco Penni
- Artwork: The Transfiguration

**Visual Assets and Image URLs**

### Segment 7 — A Painting at a Funeral: Raphael's Death and San Pietro in Montorio

**Purpose**
Recount the moment of Raphael's sudden death and the painting's early display history, establishing where the "spiritual testament" reading comes from while flagging it as a later interpretation.

**Key Message**
The painting was displayed at the head of Raphael's bier — and that single image is largely why later generations came to read it as the artist's spiritual testament, even though that reading was formed after the fact, not planned by Raphael himself.

**Content**
According to Vasari, after Raphael's death the painting was displayed for several days at the head of his bier at his house in the Borgo. Since the painting had been meant for Narbonne but Raphael was dead, the patron Giulio de' Medici chose to keep it in Rome instead; in 1523 he donated it to the high altar of the church of San Pietro in Montorio. To meet the original French commission, Raphael's workshop separately produced a full-scale copy, now in the Museo del Prado in Madrid — not identical to the original in every dimension or detail, but an important document of how the workshop disseminated the design.

- Museum: Museo del Prado

### Segment 8 — Paris, Return, and a Mosaic in St. Peter's

**Purpose**
Trace the painting's full journey from altarpiece to museum object and public devotional image, showing how it kept being re-discussed across European art history.

**Key Message**
From a Roman altar, through Napoleon's forced removal, public display in Paris, papal diplomacy to secure its return, and finally a double afterlife — the original in the Vatican Museums and a mosaic copy in St. Peter's — the painting's journey shows how it turned from a religious image into cross-century cultural heritage.

**Content**
After the 1797 Treaty of Tolentino, Napoleon's forces shipped The Transfiguration and a mass of papal and Italian artworks to Paris — a forced transfer driven by military and political treaty, not an ordinary acquisition. In the Louvre, it became a key reference point for European artists studying Raphael; the British painter J. M. W. Turner viewed it in Paris in 1802 and later made it a centerpiece of his perspective lectures. After Napoleon's fall, the papacy negotiated its return through diplomatic channels, with the Vatican's own records placing its arrival back in Rome in 1816. The mosaic copy in St. Peter's Basilica was made between 1759 and 1767 and formally completed in 1768, meaning the composition now survives in two parallel forms — the original panel and the basilica mosaic.

### Segment 9 — Closing: What the Painting Still Asks

**Purpose**
Return to the opening visual contradiction and question, giving viewers a way of looking they can carry into a museum, while stressing that some readings should stay open rather than closed.

**Key Message**
The painting asks viewers to move back and forth between detail and the whole: up close, the boy, the woman, and the gestures pull you in; step back, and the white Christ, the dark valley, and the whole vertical axis come back into focus.

**Content**
Return to the opening question: how does divinity answer human suffering? Raphael doesn't offer a tidy answer — instead he lets the summit's light and the valley's chaos coexist on the same panel, leaving the answer to whoever keeps moving between detail and whole. Next time you stand in front of this painting, or its mosaic counterpart, try finding the white-robed Christ first, then the twisting woman, then step back and watch how the single vertical axis ties both worlds together.

## G. Fact-Checking Notes

- **Established:** The painting is Raphael's last large work, painted 1516–1520; commissioned by Cardinal Giulio de' Medici for the Cathedral of Saints Justus and Pastor in Narbonne, France; the composition combines the Transfiguration above with the story of the possessed boy below; the work passed through San Pietro in Montorio, Paris, and the Vatican collections. Michelangelo did supply figure designs for The Raising of Lazarus; at least three related red-chalk studies survive, held by the British Museum and the Musée Bonnat in Bayonne.
- **Material description kept conservative:** public sources disagree on the panel's support (oil tempera on wood versus oil on canvas); this plan conservatively describes it as oil tempera on wood and does not treat a transfer to canvas as an established fact, nor does it apply the documented support-transfer history of The Raising of Lazarus to this painting. Dimensions are given as approximately 410 × 279 cm, with some sources recording 405 × 278 cm.
- **Flagged as inference, not settled fact:** whether the *Apocalypsis Nova* text directly shaped the two-register design; whether Raphael added nineteen figures specifically because he saw Sebastiano's painting; whether the boy is not yet healed, being healed, or already healed; the exact identity of the two kneeling saints at left (usually identified as Saints Justus and Pastor, with an alternative identification as Saints Felicissimus and Agapitus also proposed in the literature); and the painting's reputation as Raphael's "spiritual testament" (a reading formed after his death, not a plan he made in advance).
- **Limits of this session's image verification:** the main painting (IMG-001), The Raising of Lazarus (IMG-002), and Raphael's self-portrait (IMG-009) are confirmed as public-domain files on Wikimedia Commons and can be treated as stable sources. The two Michelangelo drawings at the British Museum, the Louvre and Albertina compositional studies, the Prado copy, the St. Peter's mosaic, and the Portrait of Leo X all have confirmed institutional collection pages, but this session could not confirm stable, embeddable direct image files or explicit rights statements for them; all are marked Needs verification and should be rechecked directly against each institution's site before production.
