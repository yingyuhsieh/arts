Heaven blazes above while a father pleads for help below. Raphael’s *The Transfiguration* brings divine revelation and human suffering into one monumental image—and turns the distance between them into the painting’s central drama.

Created between 1516 and 1520, this was Raphael’s final large-scale work. Christ rises weightlessly between Moses and Elijah as Peter, James, and John recoil from the light. Beneath them, a crowd surrounds a tormented boy whose father has brought him to the disciples. The Gospel episodes are sequential, but Raphael makes us see them at once: the upper scene reveals who Christ is, while the lower asks what that revelation means for people trapped in pain.

Follow the painting through three visual pathways. White and silvery blue dissolve the bodies above, while reds, greens, ochres, and deep shadows give the lower figures physical urgency. Arcs and stable triangles govern the mountaintop; twisting bodies and intersecting limbs agitate the valley. A web of gazes and pointing hands repeatedly draws the eye from the suffering child toward the light.

The boy is traditionally understood as awaiting Christ’s healing, although his strained pose has also prompted interpretations that the cure may already be beginning. Raphael leaves the moment unresolved.

Cardinal Giulio de’ Medici commissioned the work for the Cathedral of Saints Justus and Pastor in Narbonne alongside a rival altarpiece by Sebastiano del Piombo, whose project incorporated designs by Michelangelo. Competition forms an important context, but no surviving document proves that it alone caused Raphael to expand his design into two scenes.

Raphael died in April 1520 at age thirty-seven, leaving questions about how much of the nearly finished painting was completed with workshop assistance. Vasari later recorded that it stood near the artist’s head during his funeral display, helping shape its reputation as his final spiritual statement.

Taken to Paris after the 1797 Treaty of Tolentino, the painting returned to Rome in 1816. Today it is displayed in Room VIII of the Pinacoteca Vaticana, while a monumental mosaic version carries Raphael’s composition into St. Peter’s Basilica.
