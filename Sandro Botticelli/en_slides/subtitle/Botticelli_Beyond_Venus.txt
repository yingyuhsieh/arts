Botticelli also explored narrative through drawings for Dante's Divine Comedy. These parchment sheets are distinct from the illustrations designed for the printed edition of 1481. Repeated appearances of Dante and Virgil allow a single page to hold several moments of their journey. Many sheets depend on fine contours and open space rather than rich color, and the ambitious project remained unfinished. His line could therefore do more than describe a graceful body: it could guide a reader through an imagined world.

Our familiar Botticelli is also a product of later taste. His reputation declined after his death in 1510, but his name and works were never entirely forgotten. Nineteenth-century collectors, critics, and artists renewed interest in him. Pre-Raphaelite admirers especially valued his elongated figures and flowing contours. Later photography, fashion, and mass media helped make Venus a widely recognizable image. Those choices expanded his influence while sometimes obscuring the civic, religious, and workshop contexts explored in this video.
