Hi everyone, welcome back to Our Famous Artists. When a mother sees her child moving toward danger, surely she pulls him back.
Yet in Leonardo da Vinci’s The Virgin and Child with Saint Anne, Mary reaches forward and leans toward Jesus without fully stopping him. The child plays with a lamb. To a Christian viewer, however, the lamb also evokes the Lamb of God and Christ’s future sacrifice.

[Page 2]
The Louvre dates Leonardo’s painting to about 1503 to 1519. It is oil on a poplar panel, now approximately 168 centimeters high and 130 centimeters wide, or 66 inches by 51 inches, and carries the inventory number INV 776. It hangs in the Grande Galerie on the first floor of the Denon wing, beside the room containing the Mona Lisa. If the Mona Lisa concentrates mystery within one face, Saint Anne distributes uncertainty across four bodies: Anne, her daughter Mary, the Christ Child, and a lamb.
This was not a picture completed in a single campaign and delivered. Leonardo adjusted figures, drapery, and landscape over many years. When he died in 1519, parts of the foreground and terrain remained visibly unfinished. The painting is both a masterpiece and the record of an extended working process.

[Page 3]
Saint Anne is Mary’s mother and Christ’s grandmother. Because certain traditions hold that Anne passed away before Christ was born, this image does not depict a literal family gathering. Instead, it is a deliberate theological structure that brings generation, motherhood, and salvation together in one space. Anne occupies the highest, most distant position, while Mary leans forward as if perched directly across her mother’s lap, and Christ twists downward toward the lamb.
Rather than placing these generations side by side, Leonardo aligns them along a continuous flow of movement—passing from Anne to Mary, and from Mary to Jesus. The grandmother anchors the composition with complete stability, the mother remains caught in the tension between holding on and letting go, and the child moves with the greatest energy toward his future. In this way, Leonardo condenses generations of family and sacred destiny into the fluid rhythm of a single moment.

[Page 4]
At first, the figures form a stable pyramid, with Anne’s head at the summit while Mary and Christ expand below. Look longer, however, and Mary’s posture becomes surprisingly complex. She appears to sit across Anne’s lap, rotate her torso, lean forward, and reach out with both arms toward her child. Leonardo did not structure these figures for ordinary anatomical ease; every posture is designed to serve the unfolding narrative.
Anne acts as the quiet anchor; Mary functions as the dynamic hinge between generations; Christ carries that forward momentum down toward the lamb. Through sfumato, Leonardo softens the edges, allowing the bodies to blend seamlessly through delicate gradations of light and shadow. What begins as an intricate, almost impossible arrangement of limbs ultimately flows with remarkable grace, unity, and life.

[Page 5]
Jesus does not sit quietly in Mary’s arms. He presses against or straddles the lamb, holds its neck, and turns with the energy of a child at play. Yet the lamb had long represented the Lamb of God—Christ offered in sacrifice. Leonardo therefore places the future inside a childhood game. Jesus seems both to embrace the lamb and to accept what it signifies.
Mary’s approaching hands can be read as an effort to pull him away, or as hesitation between maternal love and obedience. There is no cross, blade, or blood in the painting, but the Passion is already present. It has been condensed into the small distance between a mother’s fingers and a child she cannot quite reclaim.

[Page 6]
Anne watches her daughter and grandson with a calm, elusive smile. Mary is absorbed in the child’s movement, expressing tenderness but perhaps also anxiety. Jesus turns back toward his mother even as his body continues toward the lamb. The three faces seem to inhabit different times: Anne suggests a longer, eternal order; Mary occupies the immediate tension of maternal love; Jesus moves physically toward his destiny.
Still, no document proves that Leonardo intended Anne to be the one who already knows. What he gives us instead is a precise choreography of glances and expressions that remain open enough for affection, sorrow, understanding, and submission to coexist.

[Page 7]
Many viewers notice that the two adult women seem to possess only three clearly readable legs. Some then connect the number three to the Trinity and treat it as a hidden Leonardo code. A more direct explanation lies in the overlap of Mary’s blue mantle, Anne’s drapery, underlying lines, and incompletely resolved paint. Leonardo left no statement identifying a deliberate three-legged design, and technical evidence does not establish one.
The integration of three generations into an almost indivisible group may certainly evoke a visual sense of three in one. But that is a later theological association, not a secret that scholarship has conclusively decoded.

[Page 8]
Leonardo’s sfumato is not simply a blur placed around the figures. Fine tonal transitions weaken fixed contours. Cheeks dissolve into shadow, mouths lack a hard line that could lock an emotion in place, and arms and drapery seem to emerge gradually through the air. That is why Anne and Mary cannot be classified simply as smiling or grieving. Emotion appears as an ongoing formation rather than a completed statement.
The landscape uses atmospheric perspective: distant mountains become bluer, lighter, and less distinct. The rocks and water also reflect Leonardo’s lifelong interest in geology and natural movement. The figures do not sit like cutouts before a background. Body, air, and terrain seem to participate in a single, slow current.

[Page 9]
The faces, hands, and parts of the clothing are astonishingly refined. Yet the foreground, rocky stream, and middle ground between the figures and blue mountains remain substantially unfinished. This is not simply the result of later damage; the painting itself stopped at different stages. Infrared reflectography, X-rays, ultraviolet fluorescence, and material analysis show that Leonardo continued to revise drapery, hairstyles, gestures, and relationships after painting had begun.
He did not finish a perfect drawing and merely fill it with color. Thought continued inside the paint layers. The work is therefore both image and laboratory. Areas that were never fully covered allow us to watch an artist repeatedly refuse his previous answer.

[Page 10]
Vasari wrote that friars at Santissima Annunziata in Florence waited for Leonardo to develop a Saint Anne composition, and that a publicly displayed cartoon drew crowds for two days. The story shows that the design attracted enormous attention, but no surviving contract proves that the Louvre panel was the intended altarpiece. Another older theory proposed that Louis XII commissioned the painting to celebrate the birth of his daughter Claude, partly because Queen Anne of Brittany shared the saint’s name.
Current Louvre scholarship does not treat this as established evidence. The most honest conclusion is that Saint Anne had strong religious visibility in Florence around 1500, and the painting later became connected with the French court. The original patron, however, remains uncertain.

[Page 11]
The National Gallery’s Burlington House Cartoon shows Anne, Mary, Christ, and the young John the Baptist—without a lamb. The museum currently dates it to about 1506 to 1508 and describes it as a full-scale presentation drawing rather than an ordinary transfer cartoon. On April 3, 1501, Fra’ Pietro da Novellara described another Saint Anne cartoon that included a lamb. That sheet is lost. From the letter and later copies, scholars reconstruct a design in which Mary’s effort to restrain Christ, and Anne’s effort to restrain Mary, may have been more explicit than in the Louvre panel.
This is not a simple straight line, and not merely John replaced by a lamb. Leonardo repeatedly tested figures, orientations, and gestures. The surviving painting preserves one late state of a much longer process.

[Page 12]
In 2005, scholars identified a note written in October 1503 by the Florentine official Agostino Vespucci in a volume of Cicero at Heidelberg University Library. Vespucci compared Leonardo to the ancient painter Apelles and mentioned both the head of Lisa del Giocondo and the head of Anne, mother of the Virgin. This brief note gives the Mona Lisa and the Saint Anne project a rare contemporary coordinate.
It does not describe the entire composition or tell us which state of the painting existed. What it demonstrates is that by 1503, these two subjects—later among Leonardo’s most famous works—were already present together in his working life.

[Page 13]
Leonardo probably began the panel in Florence and continued to revise it while moving between cities. In 1516 or 1517 he settled in France. In October 1517, Antonio de Beatis recorded seeing a painting of the Virgin, Christ Child, and Saint Anne at Leonardo’s residence at Clos Lucé. The Louvre considers it likely that Francis I acquired the work around 1518, but no complete sale contract survives.
Large gaps remain after Leonardo’s death; a royal inventory of 1683 offers another clearer point in the history. After the French Revolution, the royal collection became national property. When the Louvre opened to the public in 1793, the painting’s identity gradually shifted from a monarch’s possession to a work of public cultural heritage.

[Page 14]
Examination in 2008 and the conservation campaign begun in 2009 directed new attention to the back of the panel. Beyond a horse’s head already visible to the eye, infrared imaging revealed a skull and the figure of a child. The lines demonstrate that the reverse was used for drawing, but they do not prove that all three images were made by Leonardo himself.
The child should not be identified automatically as Christ with the lamb. The uncertainty is part of the fascination. One of the most intensively studied artists in history could still leave unidentified lines on the back of a panel that had been viewed for centuries, waiting for scientific imaging to make them visible again.

[Page 15]
From 2009 to 2012, the Louvre and the C2RMF conducted a major conservation and research campaign. Cleaning made the complex ultramarine layers and subtle color relationships in Mary’s garment far more visible. Research also clarified unfinished areas, later overpaint, and the true physical condition of the paint. Conservators did not simply strip away every historic intervention; instead, they carefully weighed each layer. Still, critics including Ségolène Bergeon Langle and Jean-Pierre Cuzin worried that the cleaning went too far, perhaps removing a white layer from Christ’s body or weakening the smoky effect created by Leonardo’s delicate glazes.
It is therefore inaccurate to claim either that the painting was restored perfectly to its original state or that it was unquestionably ruined. Conservation revealed hidden relationships while sharpening an unavoidable question: after five centuries, what exactly counts as the original appearance?

[Page 16]
In 1910, Sigmund Freud connected the similarly aged Anne and Mary—the two mothers visually intertwined—with Leonardo’s separation from his birth mother and upbringing within his father’s household. This became the influential two mothers interpretation. Freud also saw a vulture in Mary’s drapery and linked it to Egyptian maternal mythology. But the Italian word in Leonardo’s remembered passage, nibbio, means a kite, not a vulture.
The translation mistake seriously weakened the vulture argument. This does not make every connection between Leonardo’s life and the image’s multiple motherhood meaningless. It reminds us that even a compelling interpretation may rest on a mistaken word. Biography can open a route into the painting, but it cannot replace historical, iconographic, and material evidence.

[Page 17]
The Virgin and Child with Saint Anne appears to show three generations of a family. Beneath that surface, it compresses family, nature, salvation, and artistic creation into one suspended instant. Anne does not clearly restrain her daughter; Mary reaches forward but does not pull Jesus away; the child looks back toward his mother while his body continues toward the lamb. Everyone is joined by love, yet everyone is being carried toward the next moment. Leonardo’s achievement is not to explain theology neatly, but to turn love itself into a question.
Does protection have a limit? Can letting go also be an act of love? When we cannot walk another person’s path for them, does the hand we still extend mean resistance—or blessing? Perhaps that is why the painting has refused to become quiet after five centuries.

[Page 18]
It does not remain safely inside a story about saints. It draws into the image everyone who has ever tried to protect, release, or lose someone they love.
