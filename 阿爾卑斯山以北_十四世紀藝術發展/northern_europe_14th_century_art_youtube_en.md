# A. Video Core Narrative

Fourteenth-century art north of the Alps was not a waiting room for an Italian Renaissance. It was where a new way of seeing took shape through tiny manuscripts, opening altarpieces, relic chapels, painted sculpture, urban workshops, and mobile court artists. The video asks, “Where did the eyes of the Northern Renaissance come from?” It follows light, surface, bodily weight, narrative, and emotion from Paris to Prague, Hamburg, and Dijon, then returns to celebrated fifteenth-century paintings to show that their breakthroughs recombined capacities developed over the previous century.

# B. Recommended Video Titles

1. The Northern Renaissance Did Not Begin Overnight
2. Before Van Eyck: How Fourteenth-Century Europe Learned to See
3. From Pocket Prayer Books to Monumental Prophets: The Making of Northern Art

## Segment 1 — Centuries of Art History Hidden in One Room

**Purpose**

Use a familiar Early Netherlandish masterpiece to pose the video’s counterintuitive central question.

**Key Message**

The domestic world of the Mérode Altarpiece did not appear from nowhere. It condenses habits developed through manuscripts, shrine-like altarpieces, sculpture, and private devotion.

**Content**

Enter the Mérode Altarpiece through its candlestick, book, bench, and glimpse of a city beyond the window. Then move backward by a century: Jean Pucelle’s miniature room, Claus Sluter’s heavy drapery, and Rogier van der Weyden’s figures arranged like polychrome sculpture. The useful question is not who “invented realism,” but how close looking, turning pages, opening wings, walking around objects, and devotional attention gradually gave invisible belief a tangible body.

## Segment 2 — Why Art Moved Closer to Life in an Age of Crisis

**Purpose**

Supply only the historical context needed to understand the works without reducing the century to catastrophe.

**Key Message**

Famine, war, plague, and church division intensified awareness of death, while courts, cities, and trade networks continued to move materials, makers, and images.

**Content**

Use a map of the Hanseatic world around 1400 instead of a modern map of fixed nations. The Great Famine, Hundred Years’ War, Black Death, and Western Schism made prayer, wills, intercession, and memory more urgent. Yet urban wealth, guilds, and confraternities broadened the field of patrons. Overlay shipping routes with altarpiece wings: ships carried timber, pigments, textiles, and luxury goods, but also designs, craftspeople, stories, and expectations about how art should look.

## Segment 3 — Different Materials Trained Different Eyes

**Purpose**

Give viewers a method for looking beyond a simple test of whether medieval images appear realistic.

**Key Message**

Pages, wings, gold leaf, wood, and painted stone were not inferior substitutes for fresco. Each trained a different form of close looking, movement, and attention to surfaces.

**Content**

Set three viewing distances against one another: a book turned in the hand, an altarpiece opened on a feast day, and a sculpture encountered by walking and looking upward. Gold ground is not an absence of setting but a responsive surface that changes in candlelight. Spatial inconsistency may serve story, symbolism, and both distant and close viewers at once. Introduce four terms that guide the rest of the video: light, space, scale, and emotion.

## Segment 4 — Paris: Jean Pucelle Fits a World into the Hand

**Purpose**

Use one object for a sustained close reading and show how miniature scale carries space, drama, and courtly identity.

**Key Message**

Pucelle’s achievement is more than grisaille: he combines Italianate volume, Parisian line, text, and unruly margins into a private theater activated by turning pages.

**Content**

Establish that the Hours of Jeanne d’Evreux is only about the size of a hand, contains 209 folios and twenty-five full-page paintings, and is tied to Pucelle by the queen’s testament. Look closely at figures modeled like ivory relief and at tiled floors, thrones, and curtains that suggest depth. Then travel into nearly 700 marginal figures, where holy narrative coexists with street life, animals, and hybrids. Detail is not merely a code to decipher; it controls how the eye pauses and moves.

## Segment 5 — Prague: Giving Saints Weight and Presence

**Purpose**

Present Bohemia as a major center that synthesized and exported artistic ideas rather than a passive periphery.

**Key Message**

From Vyšší Brod through Master Theodoric to the Třeboň Master, Prague painting intensified stage-like space, portrait-like presence, and psychological light.

**Content**

Set Charles IV’s imperial projects in the background while keeping three changing bodies in view. The Vyšší Brod Annunciation balances gold ground, simplified architecture, and several regional traditions. Theodoric’s Saint Luke has broad shoulders and a heavy face that press toward the frame; it once belonged to a larger environment of relics, gold, jewels, and candlelight. The Třeboň Resurrection uses a red mantle, diagonal tomb, soldiers, and cast shadows to make a miracle psychologically compelling.

## Segment 6 — Hamburg: A Merchant City’s Opening Bible

**Purpose**

Connect stylistic change to urban workshops, mercantile patronage, and the actual reading of an altarpiece.

**Key Message**

Master Bertram’s Grabow Altarpiece is not a single illustration but an urban narrative machine organized by opening, movement, and comparison between Old and New Testaments.

**Content**

Locate Hamburg within the Hanseatic world and then open the altarpiece. The viewer moves through Creation, Adam and Eve, Noah, Abraham, and the childhood of Christ, reading fall and redemption across multiple scenes. Gold still marks sacred space, while plants, animals, tools, and awkward bodies place theology in a material world. In Separation of the Waters, God’s repeated working body turns creation into a sequence of actions. Similarities across regions may travel through drawings, books, assistants, and patrons; they do not require an undocumented journey by the master.

## Segment 7 — Dijon: The Weight of Stone, Color, and Grief

**Purpose**

Use the international Champmol worksite to show how court patronage brought painting, carving, polychromy, and dynastic memory into dialogue.

**Key Message**

Sluter’s innovation was not simply making stone look lifelike. He gave figures weight, distance, color, and distinct postures of grief in the viewer’s own space.

**Content**

Philip the Bold founded Champmol as both a place of intercession and a project of dynastic memory. Begin with Broederlam’s wings moving between gold ground, opened architecture, and traversable landscape. Then turn to the Well of Moses, originally the base of a monumental Calvary: six surviving prophets carry scrolls that pointed toward a lost Crucifixion above. Close views of Jeremiah, Zechariah, and a weeping angel reveal faces, hands, drapery, surviving color, and real shadow working together to create personality and emotional gravity.

## Segment 8 — The Renaissance as Accumulated Light, Not a Midnight Divide

**Purpose**

Test the argument against three fifteenth-century masterpieces and return to the opening question.

**Key Message**

Campin’s workshop, Van Eyck, and Van der Weyden created genuine advances, but they still worked with habits of close looking, surface, altar structure, bodily presence, and emotional rhetoric developed in the fourteenth century.

**Content**

Return to the Mérode Altarpiece and identify the longer histories behind its objects, movable format, and domestic sacred space. In the Arnolfini Portrait, oil layers differentiate glass, metal, fur, skin, wood, and distance; this achievement should not become the false claim that Van Eyck single-handedly invented oil painting. End with The Descent from the Cross, where Mary and Christ echo one another in a shallow gold box resembling a polychrome sculpted shrine. The century closes with the manuscript’s metaphor: the fourteenth century is not merely buried roots, but the accumulated material through which later light takes on color.

# G. Fact-Checking Notes

- The Met dates the Hours of Jeanne d’Evreux to ca. 1324–28, identifies its medium as grisaille, tempera, and ink on vellum, and gives a single folio as about 9.2 × 6.2 cm. The queen’s testament connects the book to Pucelle; the video should not imply that every stroke is securely isolated as his personal work.
- National Gallery Prague dates the Třeboň Resurrection to after 1380, while some older Commons metadata says ca. 1380. Both language plans use the museum’s “after 1380.”
- The manuscript cautiously associates the Vyšší Brod cycle with Rožmberk patronage. Keep that qualified language rather than presenting the patron as uncontested.
- Hamburger Kunsthalle identifies the Grabow Altarpiece as the former high altar of Hamburg’s St. Peter’s Church and dates it 1379/83. Some image records describe only particular wings; verify the depicted state before writing final captions.
- The Well of Moses is the surviving base of a monumental Calvary, not a functional well in the ordinary sense. Its sculpture retains traces of polychromy. IMG-011 is CC BY-SA 4.0 and requires credit to Benjamin Smith / Wikimedia Commons and share-alike treatment where applicable.
- The Met attributes the Mérode Altarpiece to the Workshop of Robert Campin and dates it ca. 1427–32. The video should not silently convert this to an uncontested autograph work.
- Jan van Eyck did not single-handedly invent oil painting. The supported claim is that he and contemporary Netherlandish painters used drying oils, translucent layers, and optical observation with exceptional persuasive power.
- The Prado’s current record dates The Descent from the Cross to before 1443; the Commons image record gives 1435–38. The plans follow the museum’s more cautious “before 1443” and disclose the discrepancy.
- Plague, war, and devotional practice form a historical and interpretive context, not a single direct cause of a style. Use language such as “intensified demand” or “created conditions,” not a deterministic claim.
