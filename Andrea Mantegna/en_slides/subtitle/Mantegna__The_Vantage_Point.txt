Mantegna’s experiments with a viewpoint from below began before the Camera Picta. In Padua, he worked with other painters on the Ovetari Chapel frescoes, made around 1448 to 1457. Classical architecture and figures respond to a viewer standing below. Much of the cycle was destroyed in the bombing of 1944. Today, surviving originals, recovered fragments and later reconstructions need to be distinguished.

[Mantegna__The_Vantage_Point_slide_0022.png]
The San Zeno Altarpiece also has a history beyond its original setting. The main paintings are in the church in Verona, but the original predella scenes are in France. The Crucifixion is in the Louvre, while the Agony in the Garden and Resurrection are in Tours. Copies complete the ensemble in Verona. The Agony in the Garden in London, compared earlier with Bellini’s version, is a separate painting.

[Mantegna__The_Vantage_Point_slide_0023.png]
One detail in the original narration needs clarification. The Camera Picta’s ceiling is vaulted, rather than completely flat. What Mantegna creates in paint is the apparent opening to the sky, together with its railing and figures. The technique called di sotto in sù organizes these painted forms for a view from below, making the viewer’s position part of the illusion.
