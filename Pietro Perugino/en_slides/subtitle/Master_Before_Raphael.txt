To understand Perugino’s achievement, let’s look beyond his connection to Raphael and go back to his beginnings.
Born Pietro Vannucci around 1450 in Città della Pieve, in Umbria, he went on to build a thriving workshop and become one of the most sought-after painters of his generation. His gift for clear, balanced spaces became a defining feature of his art.
His earliest training remains uncertain. Scholars have suggested a connection with Piero della Francesca, but we can’t confidently describe him as Piero’s pupil. By 1472, however, records place him in Florence as a member of the painters’ confraternity of Saint Luke.
He is also traditionally associated with the workshop of Andrea del Verrocchio, where the young Leonardo da Vinci trained. Although the details of Perugino’s time there are less firmly documented, that connection helps us understand the artistic world he entered.
Florence offered an extraordinary setting for studying perspective, the human figure, and the convincing depiction of solid forms. Perugino brought those interests together with the gentle landscapes and quiet grace that became his signature. From that combination, he developed a visual language that patrons across Italy would come to recognize and admire.

[Master_Before_Raphael_slide_0005.png]
To see how Perugino’s style began to take shape, let’s turn to an early work: the Adoration of the Magi, painted around 1475.
Start with the richly dressed crowd on the left, then follow the kneeling king toward the Christ Child on the right. There’s plenty to explore here: distinctive faces, sumptuous fabrics, and tiny decorative details that reward a closer look.
Now look beyond the crowd. The landscape opens up, giving the busy foreground room to breathe. Distant hills grow softer and bluer, gently drawing your eye deeper into the scene. Even at this early stage, Perugino is bringing detailed observation together with an expansive sense of space.
The National Gallery of Umbria identifies this painting as an early example of his experiments with oil, a medium that helped him explore delicate lighting and fine surface details.
And there’s one more face worth noticing: the young man at the far left, looking out toward us. The museum identifies him as Perugino himself. Suddenly, the painting feels a little more personal—we’re meeting the young artist within his own work, before the serene style of his later career had fully emerged.

[Master_Before_Raphael_slide_0006.png]
Now let’s see how Perugino brought that command of space to one of the most prestigious settings in Rome: the Sistine Chapel.
Painted around 1481 to 1482, his Delivery of the Keys uses geometry and architecture to give a sacred event a powerful sense of order.
In the foreground, Christ hands the keys of the kingdom of heaven to the kneeling Saint Peter. Within the chapel’s papal setting, this gesture also affirms the authority of the popes as Peter’s successors.
The exchange is quiet and easy to follow, with the surrounding figures giving it a solemn, ceremonial presence.
But look beyond them. The vast paved square, the receding lines, and the temple at its center all help give this moment its extraordinary weight. Let’s follow those lines and see how the space shapes our experience of the story.

[Master_Before_Raphael_slide_0007.png]
Look past the foreground figures and follow the lines of the paving. They draw your eye across the open square toward the central temple, giving the scene a remarkable sense of depth—and room to breathe.
That octagonal temple anchors the composition, while the triumphal arches on either side recall Rome’s Arch of Constantine. Together, these buildings bring the grandeur of ancient Rome into an idealized Renaissance setting.
Notice how the spacious background balances the gathering in front. The figures have room around them, and the architecture gives the ceremony a stable, ordered framework.
Through that careful arrangement, Perugino makes the handing over of the keys feel solemn and enduring. Perspective becomes part of the storytelling, giving visible structure to the idea of sacred authority.

[Master_Before_Raphael_slide_0008.png]
And his contribution didn’t end with the Delivery of the Keys. Perugino worked alongside an extraordinary group of painters, including Botticelli and Ghirlandaio, to decorate the Sistine Chapel with scenes from the lives of Moses and Christ.
Here we see another of his contributions, the Baptism of Christ, painted with workshop assistance. Notice how the central ceremony unfolds within an expansive landscape, bringing that familiar sense of balance to a different sacred story.
The fresco also carries a Latin inscription identifying Perugino and his hometown, Città della Pieve. It’s a fascinating detail: here, in the pope’s own chapel, the artist’s name remains connected to his Umbrian origins.
For a painter building his reputation, this was an exceptional stage. These commissions placed Perugino among the leading artists of his day, trusted to help shape one of the most important decorative projects in Renaissance Rome.

[Master_Before_Raphael_slide_0009.png]
Now let’s turn to another work: The Virgin Appearing to Saint Bernard, painted around 1490 to 1494 and now in Munich’s Alte Pinakothek. Here, a miraculous encounter feels as intimate as a quiet conversation.
Mary stands on the left, without the Christ Child, while Bernard turns toward her from his desk on the right. Look at their hands and the gentle inclination of their bodies. Those small gestures help us follow the exchange without any need for dramatic movement.
Behind them, a sequence of arches draws our eyes toward a bright, distant landscape. The architecture gives the figures room to breathe and makes this extraordinary encounter feel grounded in a place we could almost walk into.
Take your time with the scene. A slight turn of the head or a raised hand becomes more expressive the longer you look. Perugino’s quietness focuses our attention, allowing the wonder of the encounter to emerge through the smallest details.

[Master_Before_Raphael_slide_0010.png]
Next, let’s look at Perugino’s Lamentation over the Dead Christ, signed and dated 1495 and now in the Palatine Gallery at Palazzo Pitti in Florence.
Follow the long line of Christ’s body across the lower part of the painting. Around him, the figures gather closely to support him, look on, and mourn. Their closeness creates an intimate circle of grief, while the countryside stretches into the distance beyond them.
Notice the lowered faces and the carefully placed hands. These small acts of care draw us into the scene and encourage us to look slowly. The emotion comes through in how the figures attend to Christ and remain beside him.
Here, Perugino brings his measured gestures and balanced composition to a deeply sorrowful subject. The stillness gives that sorrow room to register—and gives us time to stay with it.

[Master_Before_Raphael_slide_0011.png]
Perugino’s success extended well beyond the Vatican. Religious communities, civic institutions, and courtly patrons all found something to admire in his clear compositions and graceful figures.
Take a look at this room: the audience hall of the Collegio del Cambio, the money changers’ guild in Perugia. Commissioned in 1496 and carried out in the years leading up to 1500, its fresco decoration became one of his most ambitious projects.
Working with assistants, Perugino brought together personifications of the virtues, ancient heroes and sages, and Christian subjects. Above them, the ceiling adds another layer of classical imagery and elaborate ornament.
Imagine entering this room to discuss guild affairs or settle a financial dispute. All around you are images that invite you to think about justice, sound judgment, courage, and self-control.
The decoration gave the guild a prestigious setting while expressing the ideals it wished to uphold. Here, Perugino’s sense of harmony helped connect the practical business of money with a larger vision of civic responsibility.

[Master_Before_Raphael_slide_0012.png]
What makes this room so fascinating is the way Christian faith and classical learning come together. Biblical prophets and the Nativity share the decorative scheme with ancient figures such as Socrates, while the virtues provide a moral thread connecting them.
For the guild, these subjects offered complementary examples of wisdom, responsibility, and good conduct. Perugino gave them a shared visual language, making the different traditions feel at home in the same space.
And there’s a wonderfully personal detail: his own portrait appears on the wall, accompanied by a Latin inscription celebrating his artistic achievement.
We shouldn’t assume those words were simply Perugino praising himself. But the portrait and inscription certainly give the painter a place of honor within his own work—a lasting recognition of the reputation he had earned.

[Master_Before_Raphael_slide_0013.png]
Perugino could bring that same careful attention to the face of an individual. Take this portrait of Francesco delle Opere, painted in 1494.
Look at the firmly set mouth, the steady gaze, and the fine lines around the eyes. Together, these details give the sitter a striking physical presence. He feels like a particular person, someone you might actually encounter.
Now let your eye move toward the landscape behind him. Distant hills soften and fade, creating a gentle sense of depth around the sharply defined figure.
That balance is part of the portrait’s appeal. Perugino combines close observation of a face with the spacious, quiet world beyond it, giving us both a vivid human presence and room to look slowly.

[Master_Before_Raphael_slide_0014.png]
Perugino also brought his talents to the court of Isabella d’Este in Mantua, one of the Renaissance’s most demanding patrons.
In 1505, he completed The Combat of Love and Chastity for her studiolo, a private room devoted to art and learning. Here, his graceful figures enter a busy mythological struggle, quite different from the quiet devotional scenes we’ve been exploring.
But this commission also reveals the challenges of working for a patron with very precise expectations. Isabella supplied detailed instructions for the imagery, and the finished painting did not entirely satisfy her.
So the work gives us a more nuanced picture of Perugino’s versatility. It shows him adapting his familiar style to a different subject, while negotiating the demands of a tightly prescribed courtly commission.

[Master_Before_Raphael_slide_0015.png]
That success brings us to a question that has shaped Perugino’s later reputation: when does a recognizable style begin to feel repetitive?
Back in 1500, Agostino Chigi had praised him as the finest master in Italy, especially when he painted a work himself. But artistic tastes were changing, and familiar formulas could draw criticism as well as admiration.
A telling example is the altarpiece completed in 1507 for Florence’s Santissima Annunziata. Writing decades later, Giorgio Vasari reported that viewers criticized Perugino for reusing figures they had already seen in his other paintings.
In Vasari’s account, the very familiarity that had helped make Perugino successful had become a weakness. Patrons and viewers who wanted invention could see those repeated poses as a lack of fresh ideas.
But we should distinguish that later account from a verdict shared by everyone. It raises a useful question: how much of this repetition reflected artistic habit, and how much came from running a busy workshop whose customers wanted the style they already loved?

[Master_Before_Raphael_slide_0016.png]
So how should we understand those repeated figures? They raise questions about both artistic invention and the practical demands of a successful workshop.
According to Vasari’s later account, Perugino defended himself by pointing out that people had once praised the very figures they were now criticizing. It’s a revealing response: a familiar design could be admired in one setting and dismissed as repetitive in another.
His workshop used full-scale preparatory drawings, known as cartoons, to transfer designs onto painting surfaces. These drawings could be reused or adapted, helping assistants work efficiently and maintain a consistent style.
But a reliable process couldn’t guarantee that every painting would feel fresh—or satisfy every patron. Some customers wanted the serene figures they associated with Perugino; others increasingly expected something new.
That tension helps us look beyond a simple verdict of decline. His repeated designs belong to a larger story about craftsmanship, workshop production, and the changing expectations of the people who commissioned and viewed his art.

[Master_Before_Raphael_slide_0017.png]
The “sweetness” often associated with Perugino deserves a closer look, too. Used dismissively, the word can make his paintings sound sentimental or predictable. But look closely at how he creates those gentle expressions and softly glowing colors.
In his oil paintings, a light ground and carefully built layers of paint help give the figures their luminosity. Oil’s slower drying time allows delicate transitions between light and shadow, while transparent glazes can enrich colors without hiding the layers beneath.
These effects require careful control. Look at how a cheek gradually turns into shadow, or how a subtle highlight gives flesh a sense of softness and volume.
And technique is only part of the story. Restrained gestures, balanced compositions, and distant landscapes all contribute to that quiet atmosphere.
We can still ask whether a particular composition feels repetitive. But the apparent ease of Perugino’s “sweetness” should also invite us to notice the skill behind it.

[Master_Before_Raphael_slide_0018.png]
Despite those criticisms, Perugino continued to receive prestigious commissions. In 1508, under Pope Julius II, he painted the vault of a Vatican room later known as the Room of the Fire in the Borgo.
The room subsequently served as a meeting place for the pope’s highest tribunal under Leo X. Its walls were later decorated by Raphael and his workshop, while Perugino’s ceiling remained in place.
That gives us a remarkable opportunity to see two generations together. Above, Perugino’s sacred figures convey a measured solemnity; below, the scenes designed by Raphael unfold with greater movement and dramatic energy.
Within a single room, we can trace both continuity and change—and appreciate the older master’s contribution alongside the achievements of his celebrated pupil.

[Master_Before_Raphael_slide_0019.png]
So what happens when we look at Perugino on his own terms? The familiar story of a master overtaken by younger artists begins to feel incomplete. His paintings invite us to consider what he achieved through stillness, clarity, and restraint.
Take this depiction of Saint Sebastian. Notice the smooth modeling of the body, its almost sculptural presence, and the spacious setting beyond. The subject is martyrdom, yet the figure’s composure gives the scene a contemplative quality.
That balance between physical beauty and spiritual reflection is central to Perugino’s appeal. His carefully arranged spaces give us time to notice a gesture, follow a gaze, and linger over the relationship between a figure and the surrounding world.
In the nineteenth century, renewed interest in early Italian painting brought his work fresh attention, including among British collectors and artists drawn to its devotional character.
Recognizing those qualities doesn’t mean dismissing every criticism of his later work. It means giving his achievements the attention they deserve—and seeing him as an artist whose quiet, distinctive vision remains worth exploring.

[Master_Before_Raphael_slide_0020.png]
A Nativity fresco, left unfinished at his death in 1523 during a plague outbreak, offers a poignant glimpse of his final years.
Painted for a small country church, it carries the gentle emotion and balanced sense of space we’ve come to recognize throughout his work.
Take a moment to look at the figures and the room around them. Even in this modest setting, that familiar stillness gives the sacred story a quiet dignity—a moving way to remember an artist whose paintings so often invite us to pause and reflect.

As we wrap up, here’s a thought to take with you. The next time you encounter a Renaissance painting—in a book, online, or in person—let your eyes wander beyond the figures in the foreground.
Follow the paving into the distance. Look through the arches. Notice where the hills fade into the sky. How does that space shape the way you experience the story?
For Perugino, those carefully arranged settings are essential to a painting’s emotional power. They give his figures room to breathe and invite us to slow down, look closely, and reflect.
That’s a legacy worth remembering alongside his role as Raphael’s teacher: a distinctive way of bringing space, serenity, and sacred meaning together.
