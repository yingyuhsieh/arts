# Our Famous Artists | Rogier van der Weyden: The Painter Who Sculpted Sorrow

## Video Positioning

- **Suggested length:** 17–19 minutes
- **Central story:** Van der Weyden turned sorrow into bodily curves, gestures, folds, and compositional rhythm, making emotion project from the surface like a relief sculpture.
- **Opening work:** *The Descent from the Cross*
- **Audience:** General viewers encountering Northern Renaissance and Early Netherlandish painting for the first time.
- **Tone:** Immersive, clear, and emotionally attentive, with documented facts kept distinct from interpretation and later legend.
- **Channel:** Our Famous Artists

## Full Structure, Narration, and Visual Direction

### 1. Opening: Can Sorrow Be Sculpted? (00:00–01:05)

**Purpose:** Establish the emotional argument through *The Descent from the Cross*.  
**Key message:** Van der Weyden places sorrow in posture and structure, not only in tears or facial expressions.  
**Visuals:** Reveal Mary’s hand and sleeve, Christ’s falling arm, then pull back to the complete altarpiece.

**Narration:**

In Madrid’s Prado Museum, one painting makes sorrow feel as if it has been carved into the bodies themselves.

Christ has just been lowered from the cross. At his feet, the Virgin Mary collapses in grief, her body bending backward in a curve that echoes the falling body of her son. They do not embrace, yet they repeat the same sorrowful gesture like two echoes of one wound.

This is Rogier van der Weyden’s *The Descent from the Cross*. He left no reliable autobiography and very few secure signatures. Yet he made sorrow exist not only in tears and faces, but in shoulders, fingers, sleeves, and the direction in which one body leans toward another.

If Jan van Eyck makes us see how light rests on metal, fur, and mirrors, van der Weyden makes us feel how emotion passes through the body and enters our own.

### 2. Two Names, One Painter (01:05–02:05)

**Purpose:** Establish identity and the multilingual urban world of the fifteenth-century Netherlands.  
**Key message:** Rogier de la Pasture and Rogier van der Weyden are the same painter in different linguistic environments.  
**Visuals:** Map Tournai and Brussels; place French, Dutch, and Latin names side by side.

**Narration:**

Rogier van der Weyden was probably born in Tournai around 1399 or 1400. His early French name was Rogier de la Pasture. After he moved into the Dutch-speaking environment of Brussels, the name was translated as Rogier van der Weyden. These are not two artists, but one person moving through different languages and cities.

The fifteenth-century Netherlands did not have today’s clear national borders. French, Dutch, Latin, and local dialects overlapped in courts, churches, workshops, and commerce. Van der Weyden’s career developed inside those networks.

His father, Henri de le Pasture, was a master cutler, placing the family in the urban craft class. The record of Rogier’s childhood and earliest training is sparse. We should not romanticize him as a destitute genius, nor invent a fully documented family dynasty of painters.

### 3. The Campin Workshop: Sacred Stories Enter Ordinary Rooms (02:05–03:10)

**Purpose:** Explain his early training and Robert Campin’s influence.  
**Key message:** The Campin workshop placed religious narratives in recognizable interiors; van der Weyden later transformed that observation into a more intense emotional theater.  
**Visuals:** Benches, fireplaces, jugs, lilies, and windows in Campin’s work; transition to van der Weyden’s hands and gestures.

**Narration:**

In 1427, a Tournai guild document records a man named Rogelet de la Pasture entering the workshop of Robert Campin. On August 1, 1432, he became a master in the Tournai painters’ guild.

Scholars still debate how exactly this Rogelet relates to the later van der Weyden and how long his training lasted. Most accept that the two figures were probably the same, but the details of his early formation cannot be reconstructed completely.

The Campin workshop brought sacred stories into recognizable rooms: benches, fireplaces, water jugs, lilies, and wooden windows became the setting for holy events. Van der Weyden inherited close observation of cloth, wood, metal, and faces, as well as the ability to organize private devotion through an altarpiece.

But he moved toward a different emphasis. He compressed spatial depth and pushed bodies toward the viewer. The environment no longer carried the main narrative. Bodies, folds, and lines of sight did.

### 4. Brussels City Painter (03:10–04:20)

**Purpose:** Show how van der Weyden became a public artist and court-connected professional.  
**Key message:** He was not only a religious painter; he designed civic images of power and justice.  
**Visuals:** Brussels town hall; tapestry copies of *The Justice of Trajan and Herkinbald*; Burgundian court imagery.

**Narration:**

Around 1435, van der Weyden moved to Brussels. From March 2, 1436, he served as the city’s official painter. Brussels was the political and cultural center of the Burgundian Netherlands, where the duke’s court, nobles, officials, and foreign envoys generated commissions for paintings and luxury objects.

For the town hall’s Golden Chamber, he painted four monumental panels known as *The Justice of Trajan and Herkinbald*. Their subject was rulers dispensing justice. Albrecht Dürer reportedly made a special effort to see them. The panels were destroyed when Brussels was bombarded in 1695; tapestry copies and documentary traces are all that remain.

This lost commission matters. Van der Weyden did not paint only Virgins and martyrs for churches. He also designed public memory. His visual language of grief could serve justice, civic identity, and political legitimacy.

### 5. *The Descent from the Cross*: Sorrow Compressed into Perfect Structure (04:20–06:00)

**Purpose:** Provide the first extended close reading.  
**Key message:** Ten life-size figures occupy a gold, shallow stage in which bodily curves replace a dramatic landscape.  
**Visuals:** Full painting; relationship diagram; parallel curves of Mary and Christ; hands, tears, fabric, and the crossbow emblem.

**Narration:**

According to the Prado, *The Descent from the Cross* was painted around 1435–38 and completed no later than 1443. It is oil on oak, approximately 220 by 262 centimeters, and now hangs in the Prado Museum. The original commission came from the Leuven crossbowmen’s guild for a chapel outside the city walls. Two small crossbows in the tracery preserve the guild’s mark.

The painting resembles a golden wooden box or a shallow sculpted shrine. Ten life-size figures have almost nowhere to retreat. Shoulders, arms, sleeves, and feet approach the edges of the frame.

The central tragedy is not staged with storms, black clouds, or a bloody landscape. It is carried by two bodies. Christ slides down from the cross while Mary collapses. Their bodies form related curves, allowing Mary to repeat the posture of Christ’s death through her own body.

John supports Mary. Joseph and Nicodemus receive Christ. Mary Magdalene twists at the right. Everyone is trying to hold someone else, yet no gesture can undo death.

Van der Weyden paints the tears small, but makes them catch the light through transparent glazes. Reddened skin, clenched fingers, and compressed fabric turn emotion into matter. The more rigorously the grief is ordered, the more inescapable it feels.

### 6. *Saint Luke Drawing the Virgin*: How a Painter Claims a Profession (06:00–07:15)

**Purpose:** Introduce artistic identity and the self-projection question.  
**Key message:** Van der Weyden places the painter within Saint Luke’s theological lineage, but Luke should not be called a confirmed self-portrait.  
**Visuals:** Boston version; Luke’s posture, stylus, Virgin, river, and distant city.

**Narration:**

Around 1435–40, van der Weyden painted *Saint Luke Drawing the Virgin*. The Boston version is one of the most important surviving examples and is often considered closest to the original. Related versions survive in Munich, Saint Petersburg, and Bruges; the degree of autograph work and workshop involvement remains a technical question.

Luke kneels before the Virgin with a silverpoint or metal stylus. He resembles a court painter facing a sacred model, but he also becomes an allegory of van der Weyden’s own profession.

Some writers have suggested that Luke’s face contains a projection of the artist. There is not enough evidence to identify him securely as a self-portrait. A safer reading is that van der Weyden places himself inside the theological tradition of Saint Luke, declaring that painting is not merely manual labor. It can be witness, prayer, and service.

The interior, river, bridge, and distant city recall Jan van Eyck’s *Madonna of Chancellor Rolin*. But van der Weyden replaces the powerful donor with an artist kneeling at work. Optical marvel becomes professional humility.

### 7. Altarpieces as Theaters of Salvation (07:15–08:30)

**Purpose:** Introduce the *Miraflores Altarpiece* and *Seven Sacraments Altarpiece*.  
**Key message:** Van der Weyden compressed different times, rituals, and salvation events into spaces that could be read repeatedly.  
**Visuals:** Open the triptych; compare Holy Family, mourning, and post-Resurrection scenes; map the seven sacraments inside the church.

**Narration:**

The *Miraflores Altarpiece* was probably made in the first half of the 1440s, often placed around 1440–45. Its three panels show the Holy Family, Mary mourning Christ, and the risen Christ appearing to his mother.

This is not a record of one moment. It compresses birth, death, and resurrection into a theater that a believer can read again and again.

The *Seven Sacraments Altarpiece* distributes baptism, confirmation, confession, Eucharist, marriage, ordination, and extreme unction inside a Gothic church. A central crucifix becomes the theological source of every ritual. Past sacrifice and present liturgy occupy one space.

The work may have been commissioned by Jean Chevrot, bishop of Tournai, but no complete contract survives. His portrait and the arms of Tournai provide important clues, not a substitute for documentation. Van der Weyden shows that an altarpiece can hold time the way architecture holds bodies.

### 8. Beaune’s *Last Judgment*: Apocalypse at the End of a Hospital Ward (08:30–09:45)

**Purpose:** Connect religious imagery to the hospital setting.  
**Key message:** The work addressed patients, the donors’ charitable image, and Christian salvation at the same time.  
**Visuals:** Hospital plan; Archangel Michael and scales; saved and condemned sides; simulated patient viewpoint.

**Narration:**

Around 1443–51, perhaps as late as 1452, Nicolas Rolin and his wife commissioned the *Last Judgment Altarpiece* for the Hôtel-Dieu in Beaune. The work originally stood at the end of the patients’ ward. People lying in their beds could see Christ, Michael, the scales, the resurrected dead, Heaven, and Hell.

Michael’s scales divide the space between salvation and punishment. Van der Weyden does not rely mainly on monsters to produce fear. He uses frightened faces, the weight of bodies, and directions that cannot be reversed.

The donors remain visible within the religious scene. Their portraits and heraldry do not erase their public power. Charity, political reputation, and personal hope for salvation occupy the same image.

### 9. The Italian Journey: A Northern Painter Becomes a European Artist (09:45–10:50)

**Purpose:** Explain cross-cultural exchange without exaggerating its effect.  
**Key message:** Around the Jubilee of 1450, van der Weyden probably traveled to Rome; court and collecting networks carried his work into Italian artistic culture.  
**Visuals:** Map from the Netherlands to Italy; Rome during the Jubilee; compare Italian symmetry with Netherlandish altarpiece structure.

**Narration:**

Around the Jubilee of 1450, van der Weyden traveled to Italy, probably including Rome and perhaps as a pilgrim. Courts and collectors in Ferrara and Florence encountered Netherlandish painting through works and exchange.

The importance of this journey is not that it suddenly turned van der Weyden into an Italian Renaissance painter. It shows that fifteenth-century art did not develop along two sealed roads. Paintings, merchants, envoys, marriages, and pilgrims carried styles across the Alps.

Italian viewers admired Northern material description, portraiture, and detail. Van der Weyden may have encountered Italian symmetry and monumentality. Yet even in his late works, he retained shallow stages, sharp folds, gold grounds, and intense emotion. Travel created exchange without erasing his Northern language.

### 10. Portraits: Power Becomes a Silent Face (10:50–12:00)

**Purpose:** Introduce van der Weyden’s portrait practice.  
**Key message:** Three-quarter views, plain backgrounds, folded hands, clothing, and badges construct both social identity and psychological silence.  
**Visuals:** Philip the Good, *Portrait of a Lady*, and Philippe de Croy; close-ups of eyes, headdress, hands, and insignia.

**Narration:**

Van der Weyden’s portraits often use the three-quarter view. Sitters stand against plain backgrounds with hands folded below. This is not photographic copying. He could lengthen a nose, narrow a face, or raise a forehead, balancing resemblance with social image.

The portrait of Philip the Good survives largely through workshop versions and copies. Black clothing, the Order of the Golden Fleece, and a severe expression concentrate authority in the face, costume, and badge.

In the portrait generally known as *Portrait of a Lady*, painted around 1460, a high forehead, transparent veil, lowered eyes, and folded hands construct modesty and rank. The high forehead also reflects the fashion of removing some hair at the hairline.

These figures offer no narrative background to explain them. We must build their psychology from a mouth, a glance, and a finger. Silence becomes van der Weyden’s most efficient portrait language.

### 11. The *Braque Triptych*: Opening Death with One’s Hands (12:00–13:10)

**Purpose:** Introduce the private triptych and memento mori.  
**Key message:** The act of opening becomes a ritual passage from death into salvation.  
**Visuals:** Exterior skull and cross; open interior with Christ, Mary, and John; intimate scale for hand-held viewing.

**Narration:**

The *Braque Triptych*, painted around 1452, is now in the Louvre. The center and interior wings show Christ, the Virgin and Child, John the Baptist, Mary Magdalene, and John the Evangelist. When closed, the exterior presents a skull and a cross.

The exterior is not simply a gray decorative surface. It is a direct reminder of death. Louvre records connect the work with Jean Braque and Catherine de Brabant; it may have commemorated a deceased husband, but the precise commission and meanings of every symbol should remain qualified.

The triptych could be opened, closed, and carried in a private room. The viewer had to open the wings by hand. Looking became a ritual: from death outside to salvation inside, from the memory of the dead to the image of prayer.

### 12. Late Restraint: The *Columba Altarpiece* and the *Crucifixion Diptych* (13:10–14:15)

**Purpose:** Present the artist’s late transformation.  
**Key message:** Van der Weyden gradually removes secondary decoration, leaving bodies, the cross, and silence to carry emotion.  
**Visuals:** Central panel of the *Columba Altarpiece*; the two panels of the *Crucifixion Diptych*; compare early and late compositional density.

**Narration:**

The *Columba Altarpiece* was probably made around 1450–55, often dated about 1455. Its central *Adoration of the Magi* joins the Virgin and Child to a distant Flemish city, showing that late van der Weyden could still balance powerful feeling with an expansive landscape.

The *Crucifixion Diptych*, around 1460, strips the narrative down to Christ, Mary, and John. The background approaches abstraction. Emotion now depends on three bodies, the vertical of the cross, and a dark, nearly empty space.

The work survives as a diptych, but technical research suggests that its panels may once have been the outer wings of a larger altarpiece. This uncertainty does not reduce its power. It reminds us that museum objects can be survivors of centuries of dismantling and reconstruction.

### 13. Workshop, Replication, and the Question “Who Painted It?” (14:15–15:20)

**Purpose:** Explain attribution and workshop practice.  
**Key message:** A workshop painting is not automatically a forgery; fifteenth-century masterpieces depended on assistants, patterns, and a market for repeated images.  
**Visuals:** Underdrawing, repeated compositions, panel construction, and a four-level diagram: autograph, workshop, follower, later copy.

**Narration:**

Van der Weyden rarely signed his works, while his workshop produced repeated compositions and portrait versions. Art historians therefore cannot decide authorship by asking only whether a picture “looks like” him. They combine wood-ring analysis, underdrawing, pigments, brushwork, and documentary history.

Even a recognized masterpiece may include workshop labor. The master could design the composition and paint crucial faces and hands while assistants handled clothing, backgrounds, or repeated passages. This was not automatically deceptive. It was a normal production system.

“Workshop” does not mean “fake.” Van der Weyden’s influence exists not only in one wooden panel, but in a language of emotion that studios, patrons, and European markets could reproduce.

### 14. Van der Weyden and Van Eyck: One Lets the Eye Travel, the Other Makes the Body Endure (15:20–16:25)

**Purpose:** Give viewers a clear comparison with Jan van Eyck.  
**Key message:** Both masters value material observation and precision, but their pictorial centers of gravity differ.  
**Visuals:** *Arnolfini Portrait* and *Madonna of Chancellor Rolin* beside *The Descent from the Cross* and *Portrait of a Lady*; arrows indicate spatial travel and bodily rhythm.

**Narration:**

Calling van der Weyden the “heart” and van Eyck the “eye” is useful for a first approach, but it cannot replace close looking.

Both artists care about material, portraiture, religious symbolism, and exact structure. Van Eyck can express feeling, and van der Weyden possessed extraordinary technical skill.

The difference is the center of gravity. Van Eyck often opens space toward the distance, allowing the eye to travel through mirrors, windows, cities, and objects. Van der Weyden often flattens space and pushes bodies forward like relief sculpture. The eye does not travel among objects; it follows arms, folds, and curves.

*Saint Luke Drawing the Virgin* makes the connection visible. It borrows van Eyck’s interior, river, and city, but replaces a powerful chancellor with an artist kneeling at work. Optical marvel becomes professional humility.

### 15. Conclusion: The Language of Sorrow Keeps Moving (16:25–17:30)

**Purpose:** Summarize the artist’s legacy and leave a viewing method.  
**Key message:** Van der Weyden’s achievement is a portable emotional language that crossed generations and borders.  
**Visuals:** Montage of *The Descent from the Cross*, *Portrait of a Lady*, Beaune *Last Judgment*, *Braque Triptych*, and *Crucifixion Diptych*.

**Narration:**

Van der Weyden died in Brussels in 1464. Sources differ on the exact day and on details of his burial, but by the end of his life he was a public city figure, not an anonymous craftsman.

His influence reached Hans Memling, Dieric Bouts, Hugo van der Goes, and Petrus Christus, and traveled into Germany, Spain, Portugal, and Italy. Later artists repeatedly borrowed his fainting Virgin, twisted arms, three-quarter portraits, and folded hands.

When looking at van der Weyden, remember five things: follow parallel bodies; notice compressed space; watch tears, skin, and metal carry emotion into matter; see gold and skulls connect salvation to death; and read silent faces without forcing them to tell a complete story.

He did not paint sorrow as one expression. He turned sorrow into a form the body can remember.

## Essential Artwork Introductions

### 1. *The Descent from the Cross*

- **Artist:** Rogier van der Weyden.
- **Date:** c. 1435–38, completed no later than 1443.
- **Medium:** Oil on oak panel.
- **Dimensions:** Approximately 220 × 262 cm.
- **Current location:** Museo Nacional del Prado, Madrid.
- **Introduction:** Christ is lowered from the cross while the Virgin collapses in grief. Ten life-size figures occupy a gold, shallow stage that resembles a relief sculpture.
- **What to notice:** Parallel curves of Mary and Christ, supporting hands, tiny tears, directional folds, and the frame-like compression of the scene.

### 2. *Saint Luke Drawing the Virgin*

- **Date:** c. 1435–40.
- **Medium:** Oil and tempera on panel.
- **Dimensions:** The Boston version measures approximately 137.5 × 110.8 cm.
- **Current location:** Museum of Fine Arts, Boston; related versions survive in Munich, Saint Petersburg, and Bruges.
- **Introduction:** Saint Luke kneels before the Virgin and draws her, placing the artist within the theological lineage of the Evangelist.
- **What to notice:** Luke’s working posture, stylus, Mary’s humility, interior setting, and distant city.

### 3. *The Magdalen Reading*

- **Date:** c. 1438–40.
- **Medium:** Oil on panel; surviving fragment from a larger altarpiece.
- **Current location:** The National Gallery, London.
- **Introduction:** Mary Magdalene reads in a quiet interior, turning a saint into an image of private concentration and inward prayer.
- **What to notice:** Pages, ointment jar, textile, window light, and the psychological distance created by reading.

### 4. *Miraflores Altarpiece*

- **Date:** First half of the 1440s, approximately 1440–45.
- **Medium:** Oil on panel, triptych.
- **Current location:** Gemäldegalerie, Berlin.
- **Introduction:** The three panels combine the Holy Family, Mary mourning Christ, and the risen Christ appearing to his mother, compressing different moments into one theater of salvation.
- **What to notice:** Three temporal scenes, Mary’s emotion, doorway-like spaces, and the sequential way the triptych is read.

### 5. *The Seven Sacraments Altarpiece*

- **Date:** c. 1445–50.
- **Medium:** Oil on panel, multi-panel altarpiece; workshop collaboration is likely.
- **Current location:** Royal Museum of Fine Arts Antwerp (KMSKA).
- **Introduction:** The seven sacraments unfold inside a Gothic church, with the central crucifix linking Christ’s Passion to the Church’s continuing rituals.
- **What to notice:** Sacramental placement, architecture, bishop portrait, Tournai arms, and the central cross.

### 6. *Beaune Altarpiece / The Last Judgment Polyptych*

- **Date:** c. 1443–51, possibly as late as 1452.
- **Medium:** Oil on panel, multi-panel altarpiece.
- **Current location:** Hôtel-Dieu, Beaune, France.
- **Introduction:** Originally placed at the end of a hospital ward, the work allowed patients to see Christ, Michael’s scales, the resurrected dead, Heaven, and Hell from their beds.
- **What to notice:** The scales, saved and condemned directions, patient viewpoint, donor portraits, and the union of charity with salvation.

### 7. *Braque Triptych*

- **Date:** c. 1452.
- **Medium:** Oil on panel, hinged triptych.
- **Current location:** Musée du Louvre, Paris.
- **Introduction:** The interior creates a private prayer space, while the closed exterior displays a skull and cross as a direct memento mori.
- **What to notice:** Opening ritual, death outside and salvation inside, and the Braque family memorial context.

### 8. *Columba Altarpiece*

- **Date:** c. 1450–55, often dated c. 1455.
- **Medium:** Oil on panel, multi-panel altarpiece.
- **Current location:** Alte Pinakothek, Munich.
- **Introduction:** The central *Adoration of the Magi* joins the Virgin and Child to a distant Flemish city, balancing intense devotion with expansive landscape.
- **What to notice:** Procession of the Magi, Christ Child, distant city, luxury clothing, and Northern spatial detail.

### 9. *Crucifixion Diptych*

- **Date:** c. 1460.
- **Medium:** Oil on panel; possibly the outer wings of a larger altarpiece.
- **Current location:** Philadelphia Museum of Art.
- **Introduction:** Christ, Mary, and John occupy an almost empty space, allowing bodies, the vertical cross, and darkness to carry the emotional weight.
- **What to notice:** Relationship of the three bodies, the cross axis, near-abstract background, and late restraint.

### 10. *Portrait of a Lady*

- **Date:** c. 1460.
- **Medium:** Oil on panel.
- **Current location:** National Gallery of Art, Washington, D.C., holds an important version.
- **Introduction:** A woman’s three-quarter view, high forehead, transparent veil, lowered eyes, and folded hands construct social identity through silence.
- **What to notice:** Headdress, hairline fashion, hands, mouth, and the psychological space created without a narrative background.

### 11. *Portrait of Philippe de Croy*

- **Date:** c. 1460.
- **Medium:** Oil on panel; originally connected with a devotional wing.
- **Current location:** Related panels are divided among collections.
- **Introduction:** A nobleman appears in prayer, linking portraiture, social rank, and private devotion within a diptych format.
- **What to notice:** Three-quarter view, folded hands, costume, and the reconstructed relationship between portrait and devotional image.

### 12. *The Justice of Trajan and Herkinbald*

- **Date:** c. 1439–50; the ensemble was completed before 1450.
- **Medium:** Monumental panels; original paintings destroyed in the 1695 bombardment of Brussels, surviving through tapestry copies and records.
- **Original location:** Golden Chamber, Brussels Town Hall.
- **Introduction:** Stories of rulers dispensing justice shaped a civic ideal and demonstrate that van der Weyden was also a designer of public political memory.
- **What to notice:** Justice, ruler imagery, civic memory, and the difference between an original and a later woven copy.

## YouTube Description

How did Rogier van der Weyden make sorrow visible?

In this episode of **Our Famous Artists**, we begin with the Prado’s *The Descent from the Cross* and explore how van der Weyden used bodily curves, gestures, fabric, tears, and compressed space to create a language of emotion that traveled across Europe.

We visit the Campin workshop, Brussels city painting, *Saint Luke Drawing the Virgin*, the *Miraflores Altarpiece*, Beaune’s *Last Judgment*, the *Braque Triptych*, late portraits, and the workshop system. We also compare van der Weyden with Jan van Eyck in terms of space, material, and feeling.

The episode distinguishes evidence from interpretation: Luke is not a confirmed self-portrait; workshop participation does not mean forgery; the *Crucifixion Diptych* may once have belonged to a larger altarpiece; and van der Weyden’s legacy must be understood through ongoing attribution and technical research.

## Historical and Production Notes

- Treat “the painter who sculpted sorrow” as the episode’s interpretive framing, not as a phrase used by van der Weyden or a fixed art-historical term.
- Do not identify Luke in *Saint Luke Drawing the Virgin* as a confirmed self-portrait.
- The Campin connection is widely accepted, but the exact length and nature of van der Weyden’s early training remain uncertain.
- Date Beaune’s *Last Judgment* around 1443–51, possibly as late as 1452.
- The *Crucifixion Diptych* may have been part of a larger altarpiece; do not present its original format as certain.
- Distinguish autograph works, workshop productions, followers, and later copies.
- For the lost *Justice of Trajan and Herkinbald*, use tapestry copies and documentary records only; never present a copy as the original painting.
- Confirm image licenses and photographic rights for every museum image before production.
