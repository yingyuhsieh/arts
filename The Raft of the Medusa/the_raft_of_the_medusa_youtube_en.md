# The Raft of the Medusa | YouTube Video Content Plan (English)

## A. Video Core Narrative

In 1816, roughly 150 people were left on an improvised raft after a French frigate ran aground. Géricault chose the instant when a distant ship is visible but rescue remains uncertain. His enormous painting turns official failure into a journey for the eye, from bodies already dead to a figure signaling across an almost impossible distance. The video asks how anonymous survivors came to occupy the scale of history painting.

## D. Detailed Segment Plan

### Segment 1 — A ship in sight, but no rescue yet

**Purpose:** Establish the question through an almost invisible detail.  
**Key Message:** The painting shows hope before it receives an answer.  
**Content:** Open on a tiny mark at the horizon. Is that really a ship? Pull back to reveal an immense canvas, 4.91 meters high and 7.16 meters wide. A man waves cloth toward that mark, while people in the foreground have already died. Géricault painted the work in 1818–19, but withheld the ending of its real-life story. For the viewer, the distance between raft and ship is the distance between a desperate signal and a reply that may never come. That visual question will organize the film.  
**Artwork / Person / Place Mentioned:** Théodore Géricault; *The Raft of the Medusa*; Musée du Louvre.  
**Visual Assets:** IMG-001 full painting; IMG-001 horizon crop; IMG-001 signaling figure crop.  
**Source Page URLs / Direct Image URLs / Rights:** See IMG-001 in E; all three shots derive from one image, labeled Public Domain Mark on Commons.  
**Suggested Visual Sequence:** Horizon Detail Crop → Slow Zoom Out to full painting → Pan to signal cloth → title.

### Segment 2 — The wreck and the abandoned raft

**Purpose:** Supply the historical stakes without drowning out the painting.  
**Key Message:** This raft was the consequence of a colonial expedition, a wreck, and decisions to abandon people.  
**Content:** The French frigate *Méduse* was heading toward the colonial settlement of Senegal when it grounded on the Arguin Bank on July 2, 1816. On July 5, roughly 150 people boarded an improvised raft. Boats meant to tow it departed, leaving those aboard at sea. When the *Argus* found them on July 17, fifteen were alive; five died soon after rescue. Use a sparse sequence of dated captions over the painting. The picture does not reproduce each person's identity or the exact weather on that morning. Its crowded boards make the consequences of abandonment visible before we explain the politics behind them.  
**Artwork / Person / Place Mentioned:** *Méduse*, *Argus*, Arguin Bank, Senegal; Henri Savigny and Alexandre Corréard.  
**Visual Assets:** IMG-001 full raft; IMG-001 crowded boards crop; IMG-001 ship crop; editor-made July 2/5/17 timeline.  
**Source Page URLs / Direct Image URLs / Rights:** IMG-001 in E; timeline drawn from the [Louvre record](https://collections.louvre.fr/en/ark:/53355/cl010059199) and [survivors’ account](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/11772/pg11772-images.html), not an external image.  
**Suggested Visual Sequence:** Full painting → Pan across boards → three date overlays → horizon Detail Crop.

### Segment 3 — Reconstructing an event he never witnessed

**Purpose:** Show the labor and choices behind the image.  
**Key Message:** Testimony and figure studies became a designed painting, not a literal eyewitness record.  
**Content:** Géricault was not on the raft. He met survivors, considered a model of the raft, studied bodies, and tried alternative compositions. A Metropolitan Museum sheet containing six studies for Corréard and Savigny lets us watch him work out posture and direction. An early Louvre oil sketch offers a second stage of the process. Compare both with the final painting, especially the placement and scale of the distant vessel. Do not identify each drawn figure as a particular historical passenger: the artist used firsthand evidence while making deliberate visual choices. Sensational anecdotes about his studio should remain outside the narration unless independently supported.  
**Artwork / Person / Place Mentioned:** Géricault; Corréard, Savigny; The Met, Louvre.  
**Visual Assets:** IMG-002 sheet of studies; IMG-003 early oil sketch; IMG-001 final canvas; IMG-002 pose crop.  
**Source Page URLs / Direct Image URLs / Rights:** IMG-001–003 in E.  
**Suggested Visual Sequence:** Slow Zoom on study → Cross Dissolve to sketch → Side-by-Side with final canvas → aligned figure crops.

### Segment 4 — The moment before an answer

**Purpose:** Explain why the selected moment is so tense.  
**Key Message:** Spotting the *Argus* did not instantly mean survival.  
**Content:** The survivors described seeing a vessel, signaling to it, and then losing sight of it for a time. Only later did rescue arrive. Géricault could have painted the violent struggle or the moment of deliverance; instead he kept the outcome suspended. The ship in an early oil sketch is easier to find than the tiny horizon mark in the monumental final version. There is another telling difference: the survivors described a clear, sunny morning on July 17, while the painting gives us turbulent, darkening skies. Put these images beside the text of the account to demonstrate how historical evidence becomes emotional structure in art.  
**Artwork / Person / Place Mentioned:** Final painting and early oil sketch; *Argus*.  
**Visual Assets:** IMG-003 full sketch; IMG-003 ship crop; IMG-001 ship crop; IMG-001 signaling arms crop.  
**Source Page URLs / Direct Image URLs / Rights:** IMG-001 and IMG-003 in E; [1818 English translation](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/11772/pg11772-images.html) for the sequence and weather.  
**Suggested Visual Sequence:** Sketch ship → Side-by-Side horizon comparison → cloth Detail Crop → ship fades from view.

### Segment 5 — Climbing from the dead toward the signal

**Purpose:** Teach the composition by moving through it.  
**Key Message:** Two pyramids tie the bodies’ upward effort to the mast and the pressure of the sea.  
**Content:** Begin with the sprawled foreground bodies and the grey-haired man holding a younger figure. Move upward through people sitting, reaching, and supporting one another until the cloth rises at the top. Then trace the second pyramid formed by mast, ropes, and sail. Its lines meet a human structure striving against the wind and waves. Avoid assigning the two foreground figures a verified father-and-son identity; that familiar reading concerns the painted relationship. A slow camera journey can let the audience feel how far the dead remain from the apparent hope above them, without covering the picture in explanatory arrows.  
**Artwork / Person / Place Mentioned:** Géricault; *The Raft of the Medusa*.  
**Visual Assets:** IMG-001 foreground crop; IMG-001 grey-haired figure crop; IMG-001 apex crop; IMG-001 mast and sail crop.  
**Source Page URLs / Direct Image URLs / Rights:** IMG-001 in E; crops share its source and rights.  
**Suggested Visual Sequence:** Lower-left Detail Crop → Pan Up along arms → restrained mast line overlay → Slow Zoom Out.

### Segment 6 — Who stands at the top?

**Purpose:** Place the visual apex within the painting’s colonial context.  
**Key Message:** The model, a recorded survivor, and the painted figure are distinct people and categories.  
**Content:** A Black figure at the top waves the signal cloth for the whole group. The Musée d’Orsay identifies Joseph, a model from Haiti, as a source for this figure and notes that Black figures became more prominent in the finished work. The survivor list separately names Jean Charles as a Black soldier. That record does not prove that the figure is Jean Charles’s portrait. Show the model-related drawing, if its image rights and direct file can be confirmed, against a crop of the finished painting. Discuss the image’s possible abolitionist force as a museum interpretation, while keeping the colonial mission and the persistence of slavery in view.  
**Artwork / Person / Place Mentioned:** Joseph, Jean Charles, Géricault; Musée d’Orsay, Morgan Library.  
**Visual Assets:** IMG-001 apex crop; IMG-004 *Head of a Black Man* source page (image pending); IMG-001 group crop.  
**Source Page URLs / Direct Image URLs / Rights:** IMG-001 and IMG-004 in E; [Orsay exhibition essay](https://www.musee-orsay.fr/en/program/whats-on/exhibitions/presentation/black-models-gericault-matisse). IMG-004 needs a verified direct URL and rights before editing.  
**Suggested Visual Sequence:** Apex Detail Crop → Pan Down the group → IMG-004 only if cleared, otherwise remain on IMG-001 → return to signal.

### Segment 7 — A recent catastrophe at the scale of history painting

**Purpose:** Connect artistic ambition with political criticism.  
**Key Message:** Anonymous victims occupy a canvas on the scale usually reserved for celebrated heroes.  
**Content:** At the 1819 Salon, the painting appeared as *Scene of Shipwreck*. Its more than seven-meter width, sculptural bodies, and concentrated light evoke the ambitions of history painting, but the people on display were left behind by an official expedition. The original Salon hanging was high at first and lowered only near the end of October; a present-day Louvre encounter should not be projected onto every moment of that exhibition. The royal collection did not acquire the work during Géricault’s lifetime. It entered the collection at his posthumous sale in 1824 for 6,005 francs. Use a simple scale caption and the original image to make both institutional and bodily stakes legible.  
**Artwork / Person / Place Mentioned:** Géricault; 1819 Salon, Louvre, Auguste de Forbin.  
**Visual Assets:** IMG-001 full painting; IMG-001 foreground figures crop; IMG-001 apex crop; editor-made 4.91 × 7.16 m scale caption.  
**Source Page URLs / Direct Image URLs / Rights:** IMG-001 in E; exhibition and acquisition from the [Louvre record](https://collections.louvre.fr/en/ark:/53355/cl010059199).  
**Suggested Visual Sequence:** Full image and scale caption → figure Cross Dissolve → Salon/1824 text timeline → full image.

### Segment 8 — Look for the ship again

**Purpose:** Resolve the opening question with a new way of looking.  
**Key Message:** The possibility of rescue does not erase the people who have already died.  
**Content:** Now repeat the journey with a more informed eye: the foreground boards and bodies, the man holding the younger figure, the human climb, the cloth, and finally the little ship. But do not let the camera end on that optimistic point. Travel back to the foreground. The ship is so small because the survivors cannot yet claim the ending we know from history. Géricault made an immediate political scandal as monumental as an epic; he also asks today's viewers to remain with the uncertain time between an unanswered signal and help. Close on the whole canvas without declaring an easy victory.  
**Artwork / Person / Place Mentioned:** Géricault; *The Raft of the Medusa*; Louvre.  
**Visual Assets:** IMG-001 foreground crop; IMG-001 apex crop; IMG-001 horizon crop; IMG-001 full painting.  
**Source Page URLs / Direct Image URLs / Rights:** IMG-001 in E.  
**Suggested Visual Sequence:** Foreground → apex → Slow Zoom on ship → reverse Pan to foreground → full image fade.

## G. Fact-Checking Notes

- The [Louvre record](https://collections.louvre.fr/en/ark:/53355/cl010059199) identifies the final oil on canvas as 1818–19, 491 × 716 cm, INV 4884 / C 51. Its 1819 Salon title was *Scène de naufrage*.
- Roughly 150 boarded the raft; fifteen were alive when discovered and five died soon afterward. July 5 to 17 is twelve elapsed days or thirteen calendar dates if both endpoints are counted. “Nearly two weeks” avoids false precision.
- The [early English translation of the survivors’ account](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/11772/pg11772-images.html) describes the ship disappearing temporarily and a clear morning on July 17. The stormy pictorial setting is an artistic reconstruction; survivor testimony also has its own perspective.
- Orsay links Joseph to the model and reads the addition of Black figures in abolitionist terms; Jean Charles appears separately on the survivor list. Do not collapse model, named survivor, and pictorial figure into a single identity. [Orsay](https://www.musee-orsay.fr/en/program/whats-on/exhibitions/presentation/black-models-gericault-matisse)
- The Salon picture was initially hung high and lowered near the end of October. The Louvre documents the 1824 purchase for 6,005 francs and display from 1826. [Louvre provenance](https://collections.louvre.fr/en/ark:/53355/cl010059199)
- Image rights are recorded as stated by the relevant museum or file page. A Commons Public Domain Mark is not a guarantee in every jurisdiction. IMG-004 must not enter the edit without verification.
