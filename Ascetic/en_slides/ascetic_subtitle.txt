An old man hangs in the air among demons. A friar opens his hands toward the light. A woman sits quietly beside a candle. One English word brought these very different images together for me: ascetic. Today, we will explore twelve works of Christian art and ask how painters and a printmaker gave an inner life a visible form.
[Page 2]
Recently, I learned the English word ascetic. At first, I simply wanted to understand its meaning. Then I looked into its etymology, the history of the word. During that search, ChatGPT suggested artworks showing hermits and other figures associated with spiritual discipline. A vocabulary question suddenly opened onto monsters, wilderness, quiet rooms, and candlelight. I wanted to know who these people were, why they changed their lives, and why artists kept returning to them. That curiosity became this film. Instead of arranging everything by date, I will follow different motives for withdrawal and self-denial, using each artwork to make the next question easier to see.
[Page 3]
As a noun, ascetic describes someone who practices rigorous self-denial. As an adjective, it can describe an austere, disciplined way of life. Hermit places more emphasis on living apart from other people. The meanings often overlap, but the focus differs. The Greek roots of ascetic concern exercise and training, including athletic practice. That language later entered spiritual and religious life. It gives us a useful question for these pictures: how might repeated practice reshape desire and attention? The word has uses beyond Christianity. Our selection here has a narrower scope: twelve images from Western Christian art, showing several different relationships between discipline, solitude, and devotion.
[Page 4]
Within the Christian traditions behind these works, self-denial could help turn a life toward God. Giving up possessions, regulating desire, and maintaining prayer or study offered different forms of practice. Anthony, one of the early models of monastic life, became known for leaving wealth behind and withdrawing into the wilderness. His followers also formed communities devoted to prayer and work. Later artists brought such examples into churches, religious houses, and private viewing. In the Catholic reform context, an intensely present saint could guide contemplation, penitence, and imitation. Rough cloth, a book, or a skull gave viewers something concrete to consider while thinking about commitments that could not themselves be seen.
[Page 5]
The twelve works unfold across five themes. Anthony withdraws into the wilderness and resists temptation; Francis chooses poverty and prayer; Jerome joins scholarship with penitence; Anthony’s search for Paul reveals that solitude can lead to hospitality; and Serapion and Magdalen bring us to martyrdom and traditions of penitence. Each theme gives spiritual discipline a different shape, from daily choices to inherited sacred stories.
[Page 6]
Our first guide is Anthony. He gives up wealth and withdraws into the wilderness, yet his sacred biography still describes assaults by demons. Michelangelo concentrates the trial into a ring of monsters in the air. Bosch spreads temptation across an entire world. Knowing the story helps us find a steady center inside their very different kinds of disorder.
[Page 7]
This is The Torment of Saint Anthony, which the Kimbell Art Museum dates to fourteen eighty-seven. Michelangelo was about twelve. Monsters lift Anthony into the air and pull at his body and clothing, while his face remains comparatively composed. The episode comes from a sacred biography. Its force here depends on the contrast between outward violence and inward steadiness. The young painter adapted a composition by the German printmaker Martin Schongauer, adding color, animal textures, and a quiet landscape below. Early biographers say he studied fish at a market to make the creatures convincing. With no secure commission record, the clearest creative context is artistic training: learning from an existing print while changing what that image could become in paint.
[Page 8]
The panel also tells a story about collecting and attribution. When it changed hands in two thousand eight, it appeared under the name of Ghirlandaio’s workshop. Further study brought new attention to the painting. The Kimbell acquired it in two thousand nine and identifies it as Michelangelo’s earliest known painting. Later technical imaging added evidence for discussion of its underlying drawings and early workshop context. This changes the questions we bring to the object. We are looking at a religious image, an exercise by a very young artist, and a work whose identity has been reconsidered. Its early ownership history still has gaps. A biography of the artist cannot simply fill those missing centuries of provenance.
[Page 9]
Bosch’s Temptations of Saint Anthony is a triptych, now in Lisbon’s Museu Nacional de Arte Antiga. The museum dates it to fifteen oh five and fifteen oh six. Across its three panels, episodes of Anthony’s trials occupy a connected landscape. At the left he suffers attack and receives help. At the right, temptation takes other forms. The center fills with hybrid creatures and distorted ceremonies. Our eyes keep wandering toward the strangest detail, and we can lose track of Anthony himself. That experience becomes part of the picture’s effect. The original patron remains uncertain, but the composition gives us firmer ground. Bosch keeps a small spiritual point of orientation within an environment that constantly competes for the viewer’s attention.
[Page 10]
Bring your attention into the central panel. Anthony kneels and turns toward us. Farther inside the ruined structure, Christ and an altar covered with white cloth offer another point of focus. This small area gives the eye somewhere to return after wandering through the surrounding commotion. The creatures reward close looking, but they do not come with a complete, agreed dictionary of meanings. Scholarly interpretations differ. When the triptych closes, Passion scenes appear on the outer wings, connecting Anthony’s trials with the imitation of Christ. That religious framework helps explain the purpose of the imagery without decoding every monster. The work later belonged to a Portuguese palace collection. Its original commission and the route by which it reached Portugal remain incompletely documented. Try moving your gaze from Anthony to the altar, then back into the surrounding water and buildings. The small scale of the focus asks something active of us. Finding it again becomes a repeated choice within the act of looking.
[Page 11]
Francis turns away from a comfortable life toward poverty and prayer. Bellini places him within a luminous landscape. Zurbarán brings him close, almost surrounded by darkness. Their very different viewing distances suggest different answers to a shared question: when a life becomes simpler, what receives the person’s attention?
[Page 12]
Bellini painted Saint Francis in the Desert around fourteen seventy-five to fourteen eighty. It belongs to the Frick Collection in New York. Francis steps out from a simple shelter, stands barefoot on rock, and opens his hands toward light from the upper left. Desert here can suggest a place of retreat. Plants, animals, and distant settlements remain visible. Chosen poverty has changed his relationship with the world while leaving that world vividly present. Bellini makes light, gesture, and landscape communicate the sacred experience. The scene may relate to the tradition of Francis receiving the stigmata, although the museum treats that identification with care. The original commission remains uncertain. A picture’s suitability for devotional viewing does not identify a particular patron.
[Page 13]
Look behind Francis. His retreat contains a book, a shelter, growing plants, and objects for daily use. Study and practical care continue here. Bellini gives poverty the substance of a lived routine, while light guides us through the details. The panel’s upper edge was trimmed, and that once encouraged speculation that an angel had occupied the missing sky. Technical research at the Frick suggests the lost section may have been small. Trimming alone cannot establish a vanished angel. This is a useful example of looking and material evidence working together. Details encourage interpretation, while research can revise it. The painting was recorded in a Venetian private collection in fifteen twenty-five and entered Frick’s collection in nineteen fifteen, adding a history of collecting to its devotional subject. Notice how little of the whole image Francis occupies. His open hands and lifted head nevertheless give its many details a center. Following his gaze back toward the light makes the surrounding rock and vegetation feel involved in the experience.
[Page 14]
Zurbarán’s Saint Francis in Meditation, painted around sixteen thirty-five to sixteen thirty-nine, almost removes the environment entirely. This is the hooded, kneeling version in London’s National Gallery. Side light reveals rough cloth, patches, and heavy folds, while much of the face disappears into shadow. Clasped hands cradle a skull. The raised head and slightly open mouth still make prayer perceptible. The skull prompts meditation on death and relates to Christ’s crucifixion. The museum places this concentrated saint within the devotional culture of Catholic reform, when images could encourage identification and sustained contemplation. The original patron remains uncertain. The visible artistic choice is clearer: cloth and gesture carry emotion even when a readable facial expression is largely withheld.
[Page 15]
The painting’s modern reputation includes an episode of resistance. It belonged to the French king Louis-Philippe and appeared in his celebrated Spanish gallery at the Louvre. After political upheaval, the collection went to auction in London. The National Gallery bought this work in eighteen fifty-three. The purchase drew criticism, including an attack by the British politician William Coningham. Today the museum values the painting’s austerity and intense spirituality. The same darkness and worn cloth can produce very different responses across time. This collecting story helps us recognize that judgment also depends on a viewer’s expectations. Looking at the work includes asking why one audience found its severity unpleasant and another finds concentration and expressive power in it.
[Page 16]
Jerome was a biblical scholar and an important Latin translator who also spent time in the wilderness. Four works now give him different visual lives. Leonardo emphasizes a strained body. Dürer shows concentration at a desk. El Greco and La Tour give penitence different kinds of weight. In Jerome, scholarship and self-discipline repeatedly meet.
[Page 17]
Leonardo’s unfinished Saint Jerome in the Wilderness belongs to the Vatican Museums, which date it to about fourteen eighty-one and fourteen eighty-two. Jerome turns his head and extends an arm. Neck, shoulder, and rib cage respond together, making prayer feel physically demanding. The lion below comes from his later legendary identity and also balances the composition with a broad curve. Jerome’s retreat did not end his intellectual life, yet Leonardo chooses bodily strain rather than a furnished study. No secure commission is known, and the reason for stopping remains uncertain. Its unfinished condition lets us see different stages together: drawing, modeled shadow, and areas of paint. That surviving state makes the construction of the body unusually available to us.
[Page 18]
The panel’s later damage belongs to a different history from Leonardo’s unfinished work. The Vatican confirms that it was cut into five pieces. Joins and losses therefore reflect events after its making, as well as the artist’s interrupted process. A vivid rediscovery story concerns Cardinal Fesch, a secondhand shop, and a shoemaker’s workbench. The museum presents this as a traditional account, so its colorful details deserve qualification. The clearer acquisition point is Pope Pius the Ninth’s purchase in eighteen fifty-six. The picture now carries two kinds of time: the visible stages of an artist constructing a work, and the subsequent life of an object divided, preserved, and reunited. Keeping those histories separate helps us understand what its current surface can actually tell us.
[Page 19]
Dürer’s Saint Jerome in His Study, dated fifteen fourteen, places us at the entrance to a room. Jerome works at the far end. A lion and a dog rest in the foreground. A skull, an hourglass, and a crucifix bring mortality, time, and faith into an orderly interior. Jerome’s translation work required sustained attention. Dürer gives that attention a space in which it can continue. Engraved lines establish wood, glass, and fur, while areas with less ink allow the paper itself to become light. The patience of making echoes the subject. Multiple impressions survive in different collections, including the National Gallery of Art. Each sheet has its own provenance. One museum’s acquisition record therefore describes that particular impression rather than every example of the image.
[Page 20]
El Greco’s Saint Jerome in Penitence, painted around fifteen ninety-five to sixteen hundred, belongs to the National Galleries of Scotland. Jerome nearly fills the image. The rock behind him presses close, giving our eyes little room to wander. His right hand holds a stone against his chest. His left presents a crucifix, toward which he directs his gaze. Books, writing materials, and a skull keep scholarship beside penitence. The museum relates such imagery to Catholic reform culture, although the original patron is unknown. The red cardinal’s hat is a later identifying convention. Jerome lived before the later office of cardinal existed. Through compressed space and brilliant red cloth, El Greco intensifies a familiar subject and brings its devotional confrontation directly before the viewer.
[Page 21]
La Tour’s Saint Jerome in Penitence, dated about sixteen thirty, belongs to Sweden’s Nationalmuseum. A fuller body enters the picture. One knee meets the ground while the other leg provides support. Jerome holds a crucifix in his left hand and lowers a knotted cord in his right. Aging skin and exposed bones let us feel the weight of the posture. An open book below and a red hat nearby bring the scholar together with the penitent. These attributes condense a tradition rather than document a single day. The museum emphasizes the naturalism of the flesh, while the original commission remains unknown. The painting entered the collection as a gift in nineteen seventeen. Its devotional presence depends on a body that still needs knees and feet to support it.
[Page 22]
A hermit’s story can also concern another person. Anthony sets out to find Paul of Thebes, a different figure from the apostle Paul. Jerome wrote Paul’s important sacred biography, reconnecting several of our subjects through a text. In the next painting, a loaf of bread becomes the focus of a meeting between two solitary lives.
[Page 23]
Velázquez painted Saint Anthony Abbot and Saint Paul the Hermit around sixteen thirty-four. It now belongs to the Prado. Two elderly men sit in the foreground as a raven arrives with bread. In Jerome’s sacred biography, the bird usually brings Paul half a loaf. Anthony’s visit brings enough for both to share. This is a religious narrative rather than an independently verified survival record. Velázquez gives the encounter an expansive landscape, making hospitality part of retreat. The painting relates to the Buen Retiro palace’s hermitage program, although documents leave uncertainty about its first altar location. A court painter’s image of withdrawal therefore belongs to both devotion and palace culture. The contrast helps us understand the circumstances in which the work could have meaning.
[Page 24]
The landscape also contains a special kind of time. Anthony appears repeatedly as he searches, approaches a doorway, and finally joins Paul in the foreground. Other episodes concern what happens after Paul’s death. Continuous narrative places successive events within one coherent view. We read a biography by moving our eyes through space, rather than treating the picture as a photograph of one instant. Strange figures in the distance, including a centaur, serve as guides in the sacred story. Their unusual bodies do not automatically make them attacking demons. Compared with Bosch’s dense temptations, Velázquez uses roads, rock, and atmospheric distance to connect the episodes. The foreground meeting gains emotional scale because the surrounding landscape allows us to imagine the journey that made it possible. Begin with the expressions and gestures of the large foreground figures, then return to the smaller ones farther away. Changes in scale set the rhythm. The encounter feels immediate, while distant figures preserve the time and effort of the search.
[Page 25]
Ribera’s Saint Paul the Hermit, dated about sixteen thirty-eight, belongs to the Walters Art Museum in Baltimore. Paul now nearly fills the painting alone. Strong light reveals the rib cage, shoulder bones, and wrinkled skin. Thick, textured brushwork makes age seem almost touchable. A raven brings bread at the upper left, while a skull rests below. Clasped hands and an uplifted face give these objects a shared direction. Ribera worked in Naples, and the museum relates the image to Catholic reform models of penitence. The first patron remains unidentified. Holiness here still involves a body that needs food. Henry Walters acquired the painting in nineteen oh two. His bequest brought it into the museum in nineteen thirty-one, giving this solitary figure a new public audience.
[Page 26]
Our last two works bring us to the edges of this theme. Serapion is remembered as a martyr. Violence suffered by a bound person differs from chosen poverty. Magdalen’s familiar penitent image developed through later tradition. Knowing those distinctions helps us understand the separate meanings carried by a white habit and a candle.
[Page 27]
Zurbarán’s Saint Serapion, painted in sixteen twenty-eight, belongs to the Wadsworth Atheneum. Serapion figures in the tradition of the Mercedarians, an order devoted to ransoming captive Christians. Their memory connects his sacrifice with that mission, although accounts of his death vary. The painting was made for a Mercedarian monastery in Seville and associated with a place where deceased friars were laid out. Its purpose is therefore more specific than that of many works we have seen. Bound wrists, a lowered head, and falling white cloth convey a body deprived of support. Zurbarán withholds most graphic injury while making the consequence of violence visible. The work extends our theme into communal memory of martyrdom, whose meaning requires its own distinction from voluntary ascetic practice.
[Page 28]
A painting’s life also includes how later people preserve it. The Wadsworth began major conservation in two thousand fifteen and announced completion the following year. Removing yellowed coating and discolored older restorations recovered differences within the white cloth. The saint’s own right hand, at the upper left of the image, presented a harder problem. Much original paint was missing, leaving only traces of fingers. Conservators consulted surviving marks, models of the pose, and medical expertise, then reconstructed the losses with reversible paint. The hand now visible includes a modern interpretation. Every stroke cannot be described as original to sixteen twenty-eight. Acquired by the museum in nineteen fifty-one, the work continues to change through research and care. Its apparently complete surface carries that history within it. Look from the hand to the sleeve and chest. Warm highlights give way to gray and brown folds, creating the thickness of cloth. Recovering that legibility forms part of conservation, alongside the responsibility to explain where surviving paint meets modern reconstruction.
[Page 29]
La Tour’s Penitent Magdalen, painted around sixteen forty, belongs to the Metropolitan Museum of Art in New York. In the Gospels, Mary Magdalene is a follower of Jesus associated with his Passion and the witness to his Resurrection. Later Western tradition combined her with other female figures and developed the familiar penitent identity. The painting therefore presents a later devotional image rather than a directly documented Gospel evening. La Tour turns her toward a candle, places a skull near her lap, and lets her face withdraw from us. Simplified forms and strong shadow give inward change very little outward movement. No original patron is securely known. Devotional contemplation provides its cultural setting. The Wrightsmans gave the painting to the museum in nineteen seventy-eight.
[Page 30]
The New York version is recognizable by its two flames. There is one actual candle. The second flame is its reflection in the mirror. A mirror often suggests appearance and vanity, yet this one does not return Magdalen’s face to her. The skull recalls mortality. The candle may suggest spiritual enlightenment, an interpretation the museum expresses cautiously. Jewelry and other objects remain within the painting. A changed direction of attention does not require every former possession to disappear. La Tour builds a slow visual route through the mirror frame, light, and hands. Like Zurbarán’s Francis, this figure invites sustained looking through limited movement and a reduced setting. Their subjects remain distinct, even as the comparison helps us recognize how much content a quiet image can hold. Her face also stays turned away from us. That distance prevents an instant reading of every feeling. We approach through light and gesture instead. Withholding a full expression preserves the duration of thought and encourages us to remain with the picture.
[Page 31]
I began with a word. After twelve works, ascetic now brings several different images to mind. Anthony’s steadiness, Francis’s poverty, Jerome’s study, and Paul’s hospitality give spiritual practice different directions. Serapion and Magdalen remind us to distinguish personal history from later religious imagery. The artists use light, cloth, engraved lines, and human bodies to make an invisible commitment perceptible. The next time you look at a painting, try finding a small action and asking what it sustains. That is the viewing journey I hoped to share when a vocabulary search unexpectedly became a film about art.
