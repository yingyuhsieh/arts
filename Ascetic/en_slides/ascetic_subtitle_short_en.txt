Recently, I learned the English word “ascetic.” Looking into its meaning led me to a world of hermits, saints, and remarkable works of art. I found elderly men surrounded by strange demons, monks in dramatic light, and a woman sitting quietly beside a candle. Who were these people? Why did some leave familiar lives behind? And what drew artists to their stories? Let's explore what these images reveal about solitude, discipline, and contemplation.

[Ascetic_Masterpieces_slide_0002.png]
“Ascetic” comes from the Greek askētikos, associated with training or exercise. The word invites us to see ascetic practice as a kind of discipline: habits of restraint through which people seek a spiritual purpose.

[Ascetic_Masterpieces_slide_0003.png]
A hermit and an ascetic are not quite the same. A hermit lives in solitude; an ascetic practices self-discipline and restraint. The two can overlap, but an ascetic might also live in a community. The figures in these artworks follow different paths, even when their images share similar objects and settings.

[Ascetic_Masterpieces_slide_0004.png]
What did solitude and discipline mean to these figures? And how could artists make an inward struggle visible? Their answers range from fantastic monsters to quiet rooms. Let's begin in the wilderness.

[Ascetic_Masterpieces_slide_0005.png]
Section one: Chaos in the Wilderness.

[Ascetic_Masterpieces_slide_0006.png]
Hieronymus Bosch's The Temptation of St. Anthony draws our eyes in every direction. Yet in the center of the middle panel, Anthony kneels by a ruined altar, remarkably calm amid the disorder.

[Ascetic_Masterpieces_slide_0007.png]
Flying creatures, strange buildings, and threatening figures surround him. Bosch gives temptation a vivid, unsettling form. Anthony's stillness becomes more striking because of everything happening around him: the wilderness offers solitude, but not necessarily peace.

[Ascetic_Masterpieces_slide_0008.png]
Section two: The Body as Battlefield.

[Ascetic_Masterpieces_slide_0009.png]
Now consider two images of Saint Jerome: Leonardo da Vinci's painting on the left and Albrecht Dürer's engraving on the right. Both portray the same saint, but each artist finds a very different way to show devotion.

[Ascetic_Masterpieces_slide_0010.png]
Leonardo's unfinished Saint Jerome lets us see the artist's attention to anatomy. Look at the tense neck, prominent collarbone, and arm holding a stone. The saint's physical strain gives form to an intense moment of penitence and prayer.

[Ascetic_Masterpieces_slide_0011.png]
Zurbarán's Saint Serapion presents another kind of suffering. Bound by his wrists, the saint hangs against a dark background. Heavy white folds dominate the canvas. Serapion was a Mercedarian martyr, so the violence depicted here was imposed on him; it differs from the voluntary discipline associated with ascetic practice.

[Ascetic_Masterpieces_slide_0012.png]
Section three: The Order of the Study.

[Ascetic_Masterpieces_slide_0013.png]
Dürer's Saint Jerome takes us into a quiet study. Light enters through the windows, and a lion rests peacefully beside a dog. Here, Jerome appears as a scholar absorbed in his work. His concentration emerges through the orderly room and the patient activity of reading and writing.

[Ascetic_Masterpieces_slide_0014.png]
Objects help tell these stories. A skull can remind viewers of mortality; books point to learning and sacred texts. Stones and cords may suggest penance or religious discipline. Their meanings depend on the work, but each gives us a way into the figure's life and devotion.

[Ascetic_Masterpieces_slide_0015.png]
Section four: Stillness and the Mirror.

[Ascetic_Masterpieces_slide_0016.png]
In Georges de La Tour's The Penitent Magdalene, the pace slows. A woman sits in darkness, her face turned away, a skull on her lap. There is little outward action. Instead, light and shadow invite us to share a moment of quiet contemplation.

[Ascetic_Masterpieces_slide_0017.png]
Look at the candle and its reflection in the mirror. Mirrors can suggest vanity, yet this one does not return Magdalene's face to us. The flame draws our attention instead, offering a subtle image of reflection and a change in what she chooses to contemplate.

[Ascetic_Masterpieces_slide_0018.png]
Section five: Fellowship in the Desert.

[Ascetic_Masterpieces_slide_0019.png]
In Diego Velázquez's landscape, Saint Anthony meets Paul of Thebes, also known as Paul the Hermit. The vast wilderness brings the two figures together. Their encounter reminds us that stories of solitude can also make room for friendship and hospitality.

[Ascetic_Masterpieces_slide_0020.png]
Look for the raven carrying bread. According to the sacred legend, it brought Paul half a loaf each day. When Anthony visited, it brought a whole loaf for them to share. In this quiet detail, the desert becomes a place of generosity and fellowship.

[Ascetic_Masterpieces_slide_0021.png]
These works give different forms to religious devotion: struggle, study, contemplation, and fellowship. We may live far from the desert, but their questions can still feel familiar. What captures our attention? And where do we find room for quiet? Thank you for joining me on this journey through the art of asceticism.

[Ascetic_Masterpieces_slide_0022.png]
Bellini offers another vision of contemplation in Saint Francis in the Desert. Light connects the saint with the rocks, plants, and distant town around him. The marks on his hands are original to the painting. Its trimmed upper edge, however, does not establish that an angel once appeared in a missing section.

[Ascetic_Masterpieces_slide_0023.png]
These images also ask us to make distinctions. Asceticism involves chosen restraint; a hermit's life emphasizes solitude. Saint Serapion's bound body recalls martyrdom and imposed violence, a different experience from voluntary desert discipline.

[Ascetic_Masterpieces_slide_0024.png]
The stories and objects have histories of their own. The raven belongs to sacred legend, and Paul the Hermit is distinct from the apostle Paul. The penitent Magdalene reflects later Western devotional traditions. In Saint Serapion, the saint's right hand includes a modern, reversible reconstruction completed by 2016.
