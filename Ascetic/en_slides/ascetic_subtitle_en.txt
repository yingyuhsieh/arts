Hello, and welcome to Our Famous Artists! An old man hangs in the air among demons. A friar opens his hands toward the light. A woman sits quietly beside a candle. What connects these very different images?
For me, it began with one English word: ascetic. An ascetic is someone who practices self-discipline and restraint, often for spiritual or religious reasons.
Today, we’ll explore twelve works of Christian art, meet the people they portray, and look closely at how painters and a printmaker used bodies, gestures, light, and everyday objects to give an invisible inner life a visible form.

[Page 2]
Recently, I learned the English word ascetic. At first, I just wanted to know what it meant. Then I became curious about its origins. Along the way, ChatGPT suggested artworks depicting hermits and other figures associated with spiritual discipline.
What began as a vocabulary question opened up a world of monsters, wilderness, quiet rooms, and candlelight. Who were these people? What led them to change their lives? And why did artists return to their stories again and again?
That curiosity became this film. Rather than move through the works in chronological order, we’ll follow the different choices and beliefs behind these lives, looking at how each artist made those experiences visible.

[Page 3]
Before we look at the paintings, let’s pause on the word itself. As a noun, an ascetic is someone who practices strict self-discipline and self-denial, often for religious reasons. As an adjective, it describes a simple, austere way of life. A hermit, meanwhile, is someone who lives apart from others. A person can be both, but the words emphasize different things.
The Greek roots of ascetic relate to exercise and training, including the training of athletes. That idea later extended into spiritual and religious life. It gives us a question to carry through these works: how can daily practice change what someone desires, and where they direct their attention?
Asceticism exists beyond Christianity. For this film, though, we’ll focus on twelve works from Western Christian art, exploring different ways that discipline, solitude, and devotion come together.

[Page 4]
In the Christian traditions behind these works, self-denial was a way of turning life toward God. Giving up possessions, learning to restrain desire, and making time for prayer, study, or work could all become part of that daily practice.
Saint Anthony became an important early example. His story tells of leaving wealth behind and withdrawing into the wilderness. But withdrawal did not always mean living entirely alone. Other believers gathered around such figures, forming communities centered on prayer and work.
Later, artists brought these stories into churches, monasteries, and private homes. Particularly in the context of Catholic reform, a vivid image of a saint could help viewers reflect, repent, and consider how to follow that example.
So the rough robe, the book, and the skull deserve a closer look. These objects gave people something visible to focus on as they contemplated the commitments that shaped a life of faith.

[Page 5]
We’ll explore these twelve works through five themes. First, we’ll follow Anthony into the wilderness, where his faith is tested by temptation. Then we’ll turn to Francis and his choice of poverty and prayer.
With Jerome, we’ll see how scholarship and penitence come together. Anthony’s visit to Paul will show us how a life of solitude can also make room for hospitality and sharing.
Finally, Serapion and Mary Magdalen will lead us to martyrdom and traditions of repentance, where the distinctions between these stories become especially important. Together, these five themes will help us see the different forms spiritual commitment can take in art.

[Page 6]
Our first guide is Saint Anthony. He leaves wealth behind and withdraws into the wilderness. But in his sacred biography, solitude brings no escape from struggle: demons continue to torment him.
Artists give these trials very different forms. Michelangelo surrounds Anthony with a ring of monsters, pulling at him in midair. Bosch spreads temptation across a whole landscape, filling it with strange creatures and unsettling encounters.
As we look, keep an eye on Anthony himself. Notice his face, his posture, and his gestures. How does each artist help us find a steady center amid so much disorder?

[Page 7]
This is The Torment of Saint Anthony, dated by the Kimbell Art Museum to fourteen eighty-seven, when Michelangelo was about twelve years old.
Look at the monsters surrounding Anthony. They lift him into the air, tugging at his body and clothing. Yet his face remains remarkably composed. The contrast makes his endurance visible: the creatures thrash around him, while he holds steady.
The young Michelangelo adapted the composition from Martin Schongauer’s engraving, The Temptation of Saint Anthony. He added color, detailed animal textures, and a quiet landscape below. Early biographers also tell us that he visited a fish market to study forms he could use for the monsters.
We don’t have a secure record of a commission. What we can see is a young artist learning through imitation and experimentation, taking another artist’s image and exploring what he could make of it in paint.

[Page 8]
This panel also has a story about how a painting’s identity can change through research. When it changed hands in two thousand eight, it was attributed to Ghirlandaio’s workshop. Cleaning and further study brought renewed attention, and the Kimbell acquired it in two thousand nine, identifying it as Michelangelo’s earliest known painting.
Technical imaging has also allowed researchers to examine drawings beneath the paint, offering clues about how the work developed and its relationship to early workshop practice. Alongside the saint and monsters, there is another layer of evidence beneath the surface.
Its early ownership history still has gaps, though. Accounts of Michelangelo’s childhood cannot tell us where the panel spent all those intervening years. Today, we’re looking at both a religious image and an object whose history scholars are still piecing together.

[Page 9]
Bosch’s Temptations of Saint Anthony is a triptych, a painting in three panels. It is now in Lisbon’s National Museum of Ancient Art. The museum dates it to fifteen oh five to fifteen oh six.
Across a connected landscape, Anthony faces different trials. On the left, he is attacked and receives help. On the right, other temptations appear. The central panel teems with hybrid creatures and unsettling scenes that resemble religious ceremonies.
It’s easy to follow one strange detail after another and lose sight of Anthony himself. Try finding him again. That movement between distraction and renewed attention can become part of our experience of the painting.
The original patron remains uncertain, but the composition gives us something concrete to explore. Amid a world competing for our attention, Bosch gives Anthony’s faith a small but persistent place to hold our gaze.

[Page 10]
Now look more closely at the central panel. Anthony kneels and turns toward us. Deeper inside the ruined building, you can find Christ and an altar covered with a white cloth. They occupy only a small part of the painting, but give our eyes somewhere to return after wandering through the commotion.
The creatures invite close inspection, though their meanings are not always clear. Scholars still disagree about some of these details. Close the triptych, however, and scenes of Christ’s Passion appear on the outer wings, linking Anthony’s trials with the imitation of Christ. That connection helps us understand the imagery without having to decode every monster.
The painting later entered a Portuguese royal collection, but its original commission and journey to Portugal remain incompletely documented.
For now, let your gaze move from Anthony toward the altar, then out across the water and buildings. Notice how easily something else catches your attention. Returning to that small spiritual focus takes an active choice, each time we look.

[Page 11]
Next, we turn to Saint Francis, who leaves a comfortable life behind and chooses poverty and prayer. Two artists give that choice very different visual forms.
Bellini places him in an open landscape filled with light. Zurbarán brings us close to a kneeling figure, almost surrounded by darkness. One invites our eyes to explore the world around him. The other draws our attention inward.
As we look at both, keep one question in mind: when someone simplifies their life, what do they make room to notice?

[Page 12]
Bellini painted Saint Francis in the Desert around fourteen seventy-five to fourteen eighty. Now in the Frick Collection in New York, it shows Francis stepping out from a simple shelter, barefoot on the rocks, opening his hands toward the light from the upper left.
Here, “desert” can mean a place of retreat. Look around him: plants, animals, and distant settlements fill the landscape. Francis has chosen poverty, yet the world remains vividly present. His open posture seems to connect him with everything around him.
Bellini lets light, gesture, and landscape convey the sacred encounter. The scene may relate to Francis receiving the stigmata, the wounds of Christ. But the museum leaves room for other interpretations.
We also don’t know for certain who commissioned the painting. Its quiet atmosphere invites prayer and reflection, though that alone cannot tell us who originally asked Bellini to create it.

[Page 13]
Look behind Francis. His retreat contains a book, a simple shelter, growing plants, and objects for everyday use. There is still studying, tending, and work to do. Bellini gives poverty the texture of daily life, while the light draws our attention from one detail to another.
The painting’s upper edge was trimmed, which once led to speculation that an angel had been cut away. But technical research at the Frick suggests that very little may be missing. The trimming itself cannot prove an angel was ever there. It’s a useful reminder: looking closely can inspire an interpretation, while examining the painting’s materials can help us reconsider it.
The work was recorded in a Venetian private collection in fifteen twenty-five and entered Henry Clay Frick’s collection in nineteen fifteen. Its journey through different collections adds another history to the scene we see.
Finally, step back and notice how little space Francis occupies. His open hands and raised head still give the whole landscape a focus. As we follow his gaze toward the light, the rocks and plants around him seem to become part of the encounter.

[Page 14]
Zurbarán’s Saint Francis in Meditation, painted around sixteen thirty-five to sixteen thirty-nine, brings us into almost complete darkness. In this version, now in London’s National Gallery, Francis kneels with his hood drawn over his head.
Look at the light falling from the side. It reveals rough fabric, patches, and heavy folds, while leaving much of his face in shadow. We can barely see his expression, yet his raised head, slightly open mouth, and hands clasped around a skull convey his absorption in prayer.
The skull invites reflection on mortality and Christ’s crucifixion. The museum connects this image with the devotional culture of Catholic reform, when paintings could help viewers sustain contemplation and consider a saint’s example. The original patron remains uncertain, but Zurbarán’s visual choices are clear: cloth, light, and gesture carry much of the emotion. After Bellini’s expansive landscape, our attention gathers around this single figure.
That intensity has not always been admired. The painting once belonged to the French king Louis-Philippe and was displayed in his Spanish Gallery at the Louvre. Following political upheaval, the collection was auctioned in London, where the National Gallery bought this work in eighteen fifty-three. The purchase drew criticism, including an attack from the British politician William Coningham.
Today, the museum values its austerity and spiritual intensity. The same darkness and worn clothing that troubled one viewer can hold another’s attention. This part of the painting’s history asks us to consider our own expectations: what shapes the way we respond to the figure before us?

[Page 15]
Next, we turn to Saint Jerome, a biblical scholar and an important translator of the Bible into Latin, who also spent time in the wilderness. His life brings study and self-discipline together, giving artists several ways to approach his story.
We’ll explore four of them. Leonardo focuses on the tension in his body. Dürer draws us toward quiet concentration at a desk. El Greco and La Tour each give penitence a different emotional and physical weight.
As we move from wilderness to study, notice how each artist makes an inward effort visible through posture, gesture, and the space around him.

[Page 16]
Leonardo’s unfinished Saint Jerome in the Wilderness is now in the Vatican Museums, which date it to around fourteen eighty-one to fourteen eighty-two.
Look at Jerome’s turning head and outstretched arm. His neck, shoulder, and rib cage seem to move together, giving his penitence a physical intensity. Below him, the lion’s curved body helps balance the composition. The animal comes from later legends about Jerome and became one of his familiar identifying symbols.
Jerome was both a scholar and a wilderness ascetic. Here, Leonardo directs our attention to the tension in his body. We don’t know for certain who commissioned the painting or why he stopped working on it. But its unfinished state reveals drawing, modeled shadows, and areas of paint side by side, allowing us to see how he gradually built the figure.
Some marks on the panel belong to a different story. The Vatican confirms that it was later cut into five pieces. The joins and losses record what happened to the object after Leonardo left it unfinished.
A colorful account of its rediscovery involves Cardinal Fesch, a secondhand shop, and a shoemaker’s workbench. The museum presents this as a traditional story, so we should treat those details with care. A firmer point in its history is Pope Pius the Ninth’s purchase in eighteen fifty-six.
The painting carries two histories on its surface: Leonardo’s developing work, and the later cutting, preservation, and reassembly of the panel. Keeping them separate helps us recognize both the artist’s process and the traces of what the painting subsequently endured.

[Page 17]
Dürer’s Saint Jerome in His Study, dated fifteen fourteen, brings us to the entrance of a quiet room. Jerome works at a desk toward the back, while a lion and a dog rest in the foreground. Their stillness invites us to slow down.
Look for the skull, the hourglass, and the crucifix. They bring mortality, passing time, and faith into this orderly interior. Jerome’s scholarship required sustained attention, and Dürer gives us a room that seems to make such concentration possible.
Now look more closely at the lines. This is an engraving: dense networks of marks describe wood, glass, and fur, while untouched or sparsely marked paper creates the light. The patience involved in making the image echoes the patient work it depicts.
Several impressions survive in different collections, including the National Gallery of Art. They share the same composition, but each sheet has traveled its own path. A museum’s acquisition history tells us about that particular print, rather than every surviving example.

[Page 18]
El Greco’s Saint Jerome in Penitence, painted around fifteen ninety-five to sixteen hundred, is now in the National Galleries of Scotland. Jerome nearly fills the painting, with a rock face close behind him. There is little room for our eyes to wander.
Look at his hands. His right holds a stone against his chest, while his left holds a crucifix. His gaze rests on the crucified Christ. Nearby, books, writing materials, and a skull bring his scholarship and penitence into the same space.
The red cardinal’s hat needs a little explanation. It became a traditional way of identifying Jerome in art, although he lived before the later office of cardinal existed. It tells us how subsequent generations pictured him.
The museum connects this imagery with the devotional culture of Catholic reform, though the original patron remains unknown. Coming from Dürer’s orderly study, the change feels striking. El Greco’s compressed space, vivid red cloth, and concentrated gaze bring Jerome’s penitence close before us.

[Page 19]
La Tour’s Saint Jerome in Penitence, painted around sixteen thirty, is now in Sweden’s Nationalmuseum. Here, we see more of Jerome’s body. One knee rests on the ground while the other leg supports him. He holds a crucifix in his left hand, with a knotted cord hanging from his lowered right hand.
Notice the loose skin and prominent bones. The posture has weight: we can almost feel the effort of holding it. The museum highlights La Tour’s naturalistic treatment of the flesh, which makes this elderly figure feel physically present.
An open book below and a red hat nearby bring together Jerome’s identities as scholar and penitent. These objects draw on an established visual tradition; they do not record everything he did on one particular day.
The original commission remains unknown, and the painting entered the museum as a gift in nineteen seventeen. Compared with El Greco’s close view, La Tour lets us see more of how Jerome supports himself. His spiritual effort takes shape in an aging body, resting its weight on a knee and a foot.

[Page 20]
Our next theme is encounter and sharing. Even a life of solitude can make room for another person. In the sacred biography, Anthony sets out to find Paul of Thebes. This is a different Paul from the apostle in the New Testament.
The author of Paul’s story was Jerome, whom we’ve just encountered in four very different images. His writing connects these figures as we move from one artwork to the next.
In the next painting, look for a loaf of bread. It draws our attention to a moment of hospitality between two people who had each chosen a solitary life.

[Page 21]
Velázquez painted Saint Anthony Abbot and Saint Paul the Hermit around sixteen thirty-four. Now in the Prado, it shows two elderly men seated in the foreground, looking toward a raven arriving with bread.
According to Jerome’s sacred biography, the bird usually brought Paul half a loaf. When Anthony visited, it brought enough for both men. Within this religious story, the bread connects divine provision with the simple act of sharing a meal.
Now look beyond the figures to the rocks, trees, and open sky. The landscape makes them seem small, yet their companionship remains the focus. Even here, far from other people, a solitary life has room for hospitality.
The painting was made in connection with the hermitages at the Buen Retiro palace, although its original altar location remains uncertain. A court painter’s image of withdrawal thus became part of a royal setting, bringing the ideal of retreat into the palace’s devotional life.

[Page 22]
This landscape also holds several moments in time. Anthony appears more than once: searching along distant paths, approaching a doorway, and joining Paul in the foreground. Other scenes take the story beyond Paul’s death.
This is called continuous narrative. As our eyes move through the landscape, we follow events that happened at different times. Even the strange figures in the distance have a role to play. In the sacred story, creatures such as the centaur help guide Anthony. Their unfamiliar appearance does not automatically make them hostile demons.
Think back to Bosch’s crowded world of temptation. Here, Velázquez uses paths, rocks, and hazy distances to connect the episodes. The landscape gives us a sense of the journey behind the meeting.
Start with the faces and gestures of the two men in front, then look for the smaller figures farther away. The changing scale creates a rhythm: the large figures bring us close to the encounter, while the distant ones preserve the time and effort it took to get there.

[Page 23]
Ribera’s Saint Paul the Hermit, painted around sixteen thirty-eight, is now in the Walters Art Museum in Baltimore. This is the same Paul we’ve just seen meeting Anthony, but here his body nearly fills the painting.
Strong light reveals his ribs, shoulder bones, and wrinkled skin. Ribera’s textured brushwork makes age feel almost tangible. At the upper left, a raven brings bread; below, a skull reminds us of mortality. Paul’s clasped hands and raised face direct our attention upward, bringing these details into a moment of prayer.
Ribera worked in Naples, and the museum connects this image with models of penitence associated with Catholic reform. The original patron remains unknown. What feels especially immediate is Paul’s physical vulnerability: this holy man has an aging body and still needs bread to sustain him.
Henry Walters acquired the painting in nineteen oh two, and it entered the museum through his bequest in nineteen thirty-one. A figure devoted to solitude became available to a public audience, inviting each viewer to pause before his concentrated prayer.

[Page 24]
Our final two works bring us to the edges of our theme. Saint Serapion is remembered as a martyr. The violence inflicted on him needs to be distinguished from the voluntary choice of poverty or self-discipline.
Mary Magdalen’s familiar image as a penitent also needs context. It developed through later religious traditions and should be distinguished from the Gospel accounts of her life.
Keeping those differences in mind, we’ll move from a white religious habit to a quiet candle flame, exploring how each artist gives sacrifice or inward change a visible form.

[Page 25]
Zurbarán’s Saint Serapion, painted in sixteen twenty-eight, is now in the Wadsworth Atheneum. Serapion belonged to the Mercedarians, a religious order devoted to ransoming captive Christians. The order connects his sacrifice with that mission, although accounts of his death differ.
This painting was made for a Mercedarian monastery in Seville, for a space associated with laying out deceased friars. Here, we know more about the setting in which people would have encountered the image and reflected on its meaning.
Look at his bound wrists and lowered head. The white habit falls in heavy folds, making us feel the weight of his hanging body. Zurbarán leaves most wounds unseen, but the effects of violence remain unmistakable.
Serapion brings our journey into a community’s remembrance of martyrdom. His religious commitment connects him with our other subjects, while the violence inflicted on him remains distinct from the disciplines they chose to practice.

[Page 26]
A painting’s story also includes the people who preserve it. The Wadsworth began major conservation in two thousand fifteen and announced its completion the following year. Removing a yellowed coating and discolored earlier retouching revealed subtle differences within the white habit.
Look across the sleeve and chest. Warm highlights give way to gray and brown shadows, making the cloth feel thick and heavy. Conservation helped these relationships become clearer again.
The saint’s right hand, at the upper left of the painting, posed a more difficult problem. Much of the original paint was missing, with only traces of fingers remaining. Conservators studied those traces, used models of the pose, and sought medical advice before filling the losses with reversible paint that can be removed in the future.
The hand we see today therefore includes a modern reconstruction. We cannot treat every visible brushstroke as Zurbarán’s own.
The museum acquired the painting in nineteen fifty-one, and research and care continue to shape how we see it. Its seemingly complete surface holds a history of damage and repair. Understanding where original paint survives and where restorers have intervened helps us look more carefully at the whole work.

[Page 27]
Our final work is La Tour’s The Penitent Magdalen, painted around sixteen forty and now in the Metropolitan Museum of Art in New York. After wilderness, monsters, and suffering bodies, we arrive at a quiet figure beside a candle.
In the Gospels, Mary Magdalene follows Jesus and witnesses his death and Resurrection. Later Western tradition combined her story with those of other women, developing the familiar image of the repentant saint. This painting draws on that later tradition, rather than illustrating a particular evening described in the Gospels.
Look at how she turns toward the candle, with a skull near her lap and her face turned away from us. La Tour simplifies the forms and surrounds them with shadow. Very little moves, yet her stillness gives us a sense of sustained reflection.
The original patron remains unknown, though devotional contemplation helps us understand the painting’s cultural setting. The Wrightsmans donated it to the museum in nineteen seventy-eight. Today, it invites us to consider how a few objects, a quiet posture, and a small flame can suggest an inner life.

[Page 28]
One detail helps us recognize this New York version: the two flames. Look carefully, and you’ll see that there is only one candle. The second flame is its reflection in the mirror.
Mirrors often suggest beauty and vanity, but this one does not reflect Magdalen’s face back to her. The skull recalls mortality, while the candle may suggest spiritual enlightenment. These are possibilities to consider, rather than a fixed code for every object. Jewelry remains in the scene, even as her attention turns elsewhere.
Let your eyes move slowly along the mirror frame, toward the flame, and back to her hands. Her face stays turned away, keeping some of her feelings beyond our reach. We approach her contemplation through light and gesture.
Think back to Zurbarán’s kneeling Francis. Both paintings concentrate our attention through stillness and a spare setting, while preserving their subjects’ different religious stories. Here, La Tour leaves us time to look without revealing everything. That quiet reserve may be what keeps us with the painting a little longer.

[Page 29]
I began with a word. After twelve works, ascetic now brings a whole range of images to mind: Anthony holding steady amid turmoil, Francis choosing poverty, Jerome absorbed in study, and Paul welcoming a visitor.
Serapion and Magdalen remind us to keep the differences between these stories in view. Chosen discipline, suffering inflicted by others, and later traditions of repentance each need their own context.
Through light, cloth, engraved lines, and human bodies, these artists give invisible commitments a visible form. The next time you look at a painting, pause over one small action: hands held in prayer, a head bent over a book, or a face turned toward the light. Ask yourself: where is this person’s attention, and what are they trying to sustain?
