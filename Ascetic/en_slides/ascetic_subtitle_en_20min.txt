Welcome to Our Famous Artists! An old man hangs among demons. A friar opens his hands toward the light. A woman sits beside a candle. What connects them?
For me, it began with one word: ascetic. It describes someone who practices self-discipline and restraint, often for spiritual reasons.
Through twelve works of Christian art, we’ll explore how artists made an invisible inner life visible. Watch the hands, the light, and the everyday objects. They’ll guide us through very different stories of faith.

[Page 2]
I first looked up ascetic to learn its meaning. Then curiosity took over. ChatGPT suggested artworks connected with spiritual discipline, and suddenly a vocabulary question led to monsters, wilderness, quiet rooms, and candlelight.
Who were these people? Why did they change their lives? And what kept artists returning to them?
Those questions became this film. We’ll follow the choices behind these lives, moving between dramatic struggles and quieter moments. Along the way, we’ll see how much a small gesture can tell us.

[Page 3]
First, a useful distinction. An ascetic practices self-discipline and self-denial. A hermit lives apart from others. Someone can be both, but the words describe different things.
The Greek roots of ascetic relate to exercise and training, including athletic training. That idea later extended into spiritual life.
So here’s our question: how can daily practice change what someone desires and notices?
Asceticism appears in many religions. Our focus today is Western Christian art, where discipline, solitude, and devotion take several different forms.

[Page 4]
In these Christian traditions, self-denial helped turn a person’s life toward God. Giving up possessions, restraining desire, praying, studying, and working could all become daily practices.
Saint Anthony offered an influential example: he left wealth behind and withdrew into the wilderness. Yet retreat could also lead to community, as others gathered around such figures.
Artists brought these stories into churches, monasteries, and homes. Especially during Catholic reform, images of saints encouraged reflection and repentance. A rough robe, book, or skull gave viewers something visible through which to consider an inward commitment.

[Page 5]
We’ll follow five themes. Anthony brings us into the struggle with temptation. Francis shows us poverty and prayer. Jerome brings study and penitence together.
Then Anthony’s visit to Paul reveals hospitality within a solitary life.
Finally, Serapion and Mary Magdalen take us toward martyrdom and traditions of repentance. Their stories connect with our theme, but need careful distinctions.
Across all five themes, we’ll ask how the artists turn spiritual commitment into something we can actually see.

[Page 6]
Anthony leaves wealth behind, but his sacred biography describes a struggle that follows him into solitude. Demons continue to torment him.
Michelangelo surrounds him with monsters pulling at his body in midair. Bosch spreads temptation across an entire landscape.
As we compare them, keep returning to Anthony’s face and posture. The creatures may grab our attention first, but the saint gives each image its center. How do these artists show someone holding steady when everything around him seems to fall apart?

[Page 7]
The Kimbell Art Museum dates The Torment of Saint Anthony to fourteen eighty-seven, when Michelangelo was about twelve.
Monsters lift Anthony into the air and tug at his clothing. Yet his composed face contrasts with their frantic movement.
Michelangelo adapted Martin Schongauer’s engraving, adding color, animal textures, and a quiet landscape below. Early biographers say he studied fish at a market for inspiration.
The commission remains uncertain. What we can see is a young artist learning through imitation, then making the borrowed image his own. Look from the creatures’ restless bodies back to Anthony’s still expression.

[Page 8]
This painting also shows how research can change an artwork’s identity. In two thousand eight, it changed hands as a work attributed to Ghirlandaio’s workshop.
Cleaning and further study brought renewed attention. The Kimbell acquired it the following year as Michelangelo’s earliest known painting.
Imaging has revealed drawings beneath the surface, helping researchers examine how the work developed. Its early ownership still has gaps, however. We’re looking at both a religious scene and a physical object whose history scholars continue to piece together. The visible image is only part of the evidence. Childhood biographies alone cannot explain where this panel spent all the intervening years.

[Page 9]
Bosch’s Temptations of Saint Anthony expands the struggle into three connected panels. Lisbon’s National Museum of Ancient Art dates the triptych to fifteen oh five to fifteen oh six.
Anthony faces attacks, strange encounters, and unsettling ceremonies across a crowded landscape. On the left, he also receives help.
It’s easy to chase one bizarre creature after another. Try finding Anthony again. That shift from distraction back to the saint becomes part of our viewing experience. Bosch gives us a world full of competing attractions, with faith holding a small but persistent place within it. Its original patron remains uncertain, leaving the painting itself as our starting point.

[Page 10]
In the central panel, Anthony kneels and turns toward us. Inside the ruined building, look for Christ and an altar covered with white cloth. These small details give our eyes somewhere to return.
The monsters are harder to interpret, and scholars disagree about some of them. But the outer wings show Christ’s Passion, connecting Anthony’s trials with the imitation of Christ.
We don’t need to decode every creature to recognize that connection. Let your gaze wander, then return to Anthony and the altar. Notice how often another detail pulls you away. Looking becomes an exercise in attention.

[Page 11]
Francis leaves a comfortable life for poverty and prayer. Bellini and Zurbarán make that choice look very different.
Bellini surrounds him with an open, luminous landscape. Zurbarán brings us close to a kneeling figure in darkness.
One painting encourages our eyes to explore. The other gathers our attention around a single body. As we move between them, consider this: when someone gives things up, what might they become more able to notice? Each artist offers a different visual answer.

[Page 12]
Bellini’s Saint Francis in the Desert, painted around fourteen seventy-five to fourteen eighty, is now in the Frick Collection.
Francis steps from a simple shelter, barefoot, opening his hands toward light from the upper left. Here, “desert” can mean a place of retreat: plants, animals, and distant settlements fill the scene.
His chosen poverty exists within a richly present world. Light and gesture make the encounter feel sacred.
The scene may relate to his receiving the stigmata, Christ’s wounds, though the museum allows other interpretations. Follow his open hands into the landscape and notice how the surroundings share in the moment.

[Page 13]
Behind Francis, a book, plants, and everyday objects suggest a life of study and practical work. Bellini makes poverty feel lived in.
The painting’s upper edge was trimmed, prompting speculation that an angel had disappeared. Technical research suggests very little may be missing, so the trimming cannot establish that an angel was there.
That distinction matters: an appealing interpretation still needs evidence.
Now step back. Recorded in a Venetian collection in fifteen twenty-five, the painting entered Frick’s collection in nineteen fifteen. Francis occupies surprisingly little space, yet his raised head and open hands organize the whole scene. Light connects the figure with the rocks and plants, drawing us from small details toward the wider landscape.

[Page 14]
Zurbarán’s Saint Francis in Meditation, painted around sixteen thirty-five to sixteen thirty-nine, is now in London’s National Gallery.
Side lighting reveals patches and heavy folds in his rough habit. His face stays shadowed, but his raised head and hands around a skull convey deep concentration.
The skull invites reflection on mortality and Christ’s crucifixion. Within Catholic reform’s devotional culture, an image like this could help sustain prayer.
After Bellini’s expansive landscape, the change is striking. Cloth, light, and gesture carry the emotion. Imagine standing before this painting: with so little surrounding detail, where does your eye settle? Francis’s stillness becomes hard to leave. Its austerity once drew criticism, reminding us that viewers have not always responded to this image in the same way.

[Page 15]
Jerome was a biblical scholar and an important translator of the Bible into Latin. He also spent time in the wilderness, bringing study and self-discipline into the same life.
Four artists approach him differently. Leonardo emphasizes bodily tension. Dürer builds a quiet space for concentration. El Greco and La Tour give penitence different emotional and physical weight.
Watch how posture and surroundings change our response. A turning head, a desk, or a bent knee can suggest the effort involved in sustaining an inner commitment.

[Page 16]
Leonardo’s unfinished Saint Jerome in the Wilderness is now in the Vatican Museums, dated around fourteen eighty-one to fourteen eighty-two.
Jerome’s turning head and extended arm make penitence physically intense. Below, a lion from later legends helps identify him and balance the composition.
The unfinished surface reveals drawing, shadows, and paint side by side. We can follow Leonardo building the figure, though the patron and reason for stopping remain uncertain.
Later, the panel was cut into five pieces. Its joins record damage after Leonardo’s work. Separate these two histories as you look: the artist’s developing image, and the object’s later survival and reassembly. Pope Pius the Ninth purchased it in eighteen fifty-six, a firmer historical point than the colorful traditional stories of its rediscovery.

[Page 17]
Dürer’s Saint Jerome in His Study, dated fifteen fourteen, brings us into a quiet room. Jerome works at the back while a lion and dog rest in front.
Find the skull, hourglass, and crucifix. Mortality, passing time, and faith accompany his scholarship.
Now look at the engraved lines. Dense marks create wood, glass, and fur; lighter areas of paper let daylight enter. The patience of the technique echoes the concentration it portrays.
Several impressions survive, each with its own history. Here, our attention rests on what they share: a space carefully arranged around sustained, purposeful work.

[Page 18]
El Greco’s Saint Jerome in Penitence, painted around fifteen ninety-five to sixteen hundred, is now in Scotland’s national collection.
Jerome nearly fills the picture. His right hand holds a stone against his chest; his left holds a crucifix. His gaze stays on Christ.
Books and writing materials connect penitence with scholarship. The red cardinal’s hat is a later identifying tradition: Jerome lived before that office existed in its later form.
Compared with Dürer’s spacious study, this compressed setting feels urgent. Red cloth, close rock, and concentrated gesture bring his spiritual struggle directly before us, with little room for our eyes to escape. The museum places this devotional imagery within Catholic reform, although the original patron remains unknown.

[Page 19]
La Tour’s Saint Jerome in Penitence, painted around sixteen thirty, is now in Sweden’s Nationalmuseum.
Jerome holds a crucifix in his left hand and a knotted cord in his lowered right. One knee rests on the ground; the other leg supports him.
Notice the loose skin and prominent bones. The posture seems to take effort. An open book and red hat connect scholar and penitent through familiar visual traditions.
El Greco brought us close to Jerome’s concentrated gaze. La Tour makes us feel the weight of his aging body. Spiritual effort becomes visible in the simple challenge of holding a position.

[Page 20]
Can solitude make room for hospitality? Our next painting brings Anthony to Paul of Thebes, a different person from the New Testament apostle.
Jerome wrote Paul’s sacred biography, connecting the scholar we’ve just explored with the two hermits we’ll meet next.
Look for a loaf of bread. After so many images of struggle and restraint, this ordinary food shifts our attention toward welcome and sharing. A solitary life can still include an encounter with another person, and that meeting can become the heart of the story.

[Page 21]
Velázquez painted Saint Anthony Abbot and Saint Paul the Hermit around sixteen thirty-four. It is now in the Prado.
Two elderly men look toward a raven carrying bread. In Jerome’s sacred biography, the bird usually brings Paul half a loaf. For Anthony’s visit, it brings enough for both.
Divine provision takes the form of a shared meal. Rocks, trees, and sky surround them, but companionship remains the focus.
The painting was connected with the hermitages at the Buen Retiro palace. A court painter thus brought an image of retreat into royal devotional life, joining wilderness hospitality with a palace setting.

[Page 22]
Look farther into the landscape. Anthony appears several times, searching along paths, approaching a doorway, and meeting Paul. Other episodes carry the story beyond Paul’s death.
This is continuous narrative: different moments share one landscape.
Even the strange creatures need context. In the sacred story, a centaur helps guide Anthony; unfamiliar appearance does not automatically mean a hostile demon.
Follow the paths from the small distant figures to the two men in front. Changes in scale let us sense the journey’s time and effort. The meeting feels more meaningful once we notice everything that led up to it.

[Page 23]
Ribera’s Saint Paul the Hermit, painted around sixteen thirty-eight, is now in Baltimore’s Walters Art Museum.
This is the same Paul, now filling most of the picture. Strong light reveals ribs, shoulder bones, and wrinkled skin. Textured brushwork makes age almost tangible.
A raven brings bread at the upper left; a skull recalls mortality. Paul’s clasped hands and raised face gather these details into prayer.
His physical vulnerability is central to the experience. Even this solitary holy man needs food. After Velázquez’s broad landscape, Ribera brings us close enough to notice the fragile body sustained by that daily gift.

[Page 24]
Our final works approach the edges of asceticism. Serapion is remembered as a martyr. Violence inflicted on him must be distinguished from chosen poverty or self-discipline.
Mary Magdalen’s familiar image as a penitent also needs context: it developed through later religious traditions, beyond the Gospel accounts.
Keep those differences in mind as we move from a white habit to a candle flame. Both paintings suggest deep religious commitment, but their subjects arrive there through different stories. Looking carefully includes understanding which story we are seeing.

[Page 25]
Zurbarán painted Saint Serapion in sixteen twenty-eight. Now in the Wadsworth Atheneum, it depicts a member of the Mercedarians, an order devoted to ransoming captive Christians.
Accounts of his death differ, but the order connects his sacrifice with that mission.
The painting was made for a Seville monastery, for a space associated with laying out deceased friars.
Look at his bound wrists, lowered head, and heavy white habit. Most wounds remain unseen, yet the violence is unmistakable. The cloth makes us feel his body’s weight. This is a community remembering martyrdom, a distinct story within our wider exploration of devotion.

[Page 26]
Conservation also shapes what we see. Work completed in twenty sixteen removed yellowed coating and discolored retouching from Saint Serapion, clarifying the habit’s subtle whites and shadows.
The saint’s right hand, at the painting’s upper left, presented a harder problem. Much original paint was missing. Conservators studied surviving traces, used models, and sought medical advice before reconstructing losses with reversible paint.
That hand therefore includes modern restoration. Every visible stroke cannot be assumed to be Zurbarán’s.
Look across the sleeve and chest again. The apparent unity of the image holds both original painting and later care, preserving a work that has suffered damage. Those warm highlights and brown-gray shadows help the heavy fabric regain its depth.

[Page 27]
La Tour’s The Penitent Magdalen, painted around sixteen forty, is now in New York’s Metropolitan Museum of Art.
After monsters and suffering bodies, we arrive beside a candle.
In the Gospels, Mary Magdalene follows Jesus and witnesses his death and Resurrection. Later Western tradition merged her story with those of other women, creating the familiar penitent image.
La Tour draws on that tradition. Her face turns away, a skull rests near her lap, and shadow surrounds simplified forms. Little moves. Through posture and light, the painting lets us approach a moment of reflection without revealing all her thoughts.

[Page 28]
This New York version has a memorable detail: two flames, but only one candle. The other flame appears in a mirror.
That mirror does not return Magdalen’s face to her. A skull recalls mortality; candlelight may suggest spiritual enlightenment. These associations invite interpretation, rather than forming a fixed code.
Jewelry remains nearby even as her attention shifts elsewhere.
Follow the mirror frame toward the flame, then back to her hands. Like Zurbarán’s Francis, she holds our attention through stillness and a spare setting. Her turned face keeps something private, giving us time to look without resolving every feeling.

[Page 29]
I began with a word. Now ascetic brings many images to mind: Anthony enduring turmoil, Francis choosing poverty, Jerome studying, and Paul welcoming a guest.
Serapion and Magdalen remind us that chosen discipline, inflicted suffering, and later traditions of repentance need different contexts.
Through light, cloth, engraved lines, and bodies, these artists make inward commitments visible.
Next time you look at a painting, pause over one small action: hands in prayer, a head over a book, a face toward light. Where is that person’s attention, and what are they trying to sustain?
