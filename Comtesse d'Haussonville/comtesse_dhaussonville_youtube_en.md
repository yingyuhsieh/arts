# Comtesse d’Haussonville — YouTube contents (English)

## A. Video Core Narrative

Ingres makes Louise appear to have just returned from the opera and turned toward us by chance. Yet the ease was developed across studies, interruptions, and revisions from 1842 to 1845. By following gesture, reflection, and objects, the video shows how an instant was constructed while giving Louise a life beyond the frame.

### Segment 1 — Just Back from the Opera?

**Purpose:** Open with an apparently spontaneous moment.  
**Key Message:** The scene feels accidental, but its ease was carefully made.  
**Content:** Louise touches her cheek and meets our eyes. A blue-grey satin dress fills the foreground; a yellow wrap rests on a chair, opera glasses lie on the mantel, and a bright blue bag hangs behind her. The Frick’s account treats these objects as cues to someone who seems to have just returned from the opera, not as a record of one particular night. Let viewers guess whether the artist caught a passing moment. Then reveal the long preparation between 1842 and 1845: arms, pose, costume, and reflection were all reconsidered. The film asks how Ingres made so much planning look effortless.

### Segment 2 — The Painter and the Sitter

**Purpose:** Give each person a distinct identity.  
**Key Message:** Louise had intellectual interests beyond the social role this portrait presents.  
**Content:** By 1845 Ingres had returned to Paris after directing the French Academy in Rome. He valued history painting highly and complained at times about the demands of portraiture, but that does not prove reluctance toward this commission. Louise de Broglie came from the Coppet family and was Madame de Staël’s granddaughter. She loved literature, music, and opera, and later published biographies of Robert Emmet and Byron. No pen or book advertises her writing in this image. That absence does not prove Ingres deliberately suppressed it. In 1845 she was still a viscountess; the familiar title “Comtesse” reflects her later rank.

### Segment 3 — Three Years of Choices

**Purpose:** Show construction through studies and revision.  
**Key Message:** 1842–45 describes a process, not continuous painting every day.  
**Content:** The Frick says the portrait began in 1842 and was completed in 1845. It estimates roughly eighty preparatory drawings, with fifteen surviving, including life-size work on the arm, head, and reflection. An early oil sketch from around 1842 reverses the sitter’s direction and has no mirror as in the final painting. Place the sketch next to the finished canvas, then highlight pose and background rather than inventing an exact sequence of decisions. Louise’s pregnancy interrupted the sittings; her son Paul-Gabriel was born in September 1843. The apparent instant emerged from starts, pauses, and revisions.

### Segment 4 — An Arm and a Red Ribbon in the Mirror

**Purpose:** Read line and reflection as compositional choices.  
**Key Message:** Ingres adjusts natural anatomy for pictorial rhythm.  
**Content:** Follow the arm crossing Louise’s body to the hand at her cheek, then pause at the shoulder. The Frick notes that her right arm sits slightly lower than natural anatomy would suggest. Critic Christopher Knight has also discussed the surprising continuity of the lines and the hand reflected in the mirror. These are observations viewers can examine, not a completed optical proof that the reflection is impossible. Let the red ribbon in the mirror lead back toward her face. Then bring back the early sketch without a mirror. The surviving versions show a significant change but do not establish the precise day or stage when the reflection entered the design.

### Segment 5 — Objects Tell a Story

**Purpose:** Read color and accessories as narrative cues.  
**Key Message:** Each casually placed object invites a story about what happened just before this moment.  
**Content:** The blue-grey satin of Louise’s dress relates to the cool tone of her eyes; a yellow wrap and the red ribbon in the mirror add warmer accents. A Frick guide says Ingres tried the wrap on her shoulders before leaving it on the chair. Opera glasses, a small blue bag, a folded visitor’s card, porcelain, and turquoise jewelry supply clues to a social life outside the canvas. The Frick calls the blue object an evening bag; Knight described it as a pouch for a fan. Neither description tells us what was actually inside. Let viewers assemble the “return from the opera” reading from details, then reveal how the full composition makes those clues feel unforced.

### Segment 6 — The Woman Beyond the Portrait

**Purpose:** Distinguish a painted role from a whole life.  
**Key Message:** One portrait cannot stand for everything Louise later did.  
**Content:** Start with Louise looking at us, then notice the absence of a pen or book. She was in her twenties when the canvas was completed. Robert Emmet appeared in 1858 and her work on Byron in 1872, so place both publication dates after the 1845 portrait on the timeline. They must not be read backward as already visible authorial identity in the image. According to the Frick-derived account, her books appeared anonymously in connection with her husband’s concern about public scrutiny; this does not establish that she herself preferred obscurity. Avoid an invented inner monologue. Instead, ask how a social portrait and later writing can both inform our understanding of the same person.

### Segment 7 — Two Women in the de Broglie Family

**Purpose:** Compare another Ingres portrait and trace later collection history.  
**Key Message:** The same artist and family yield different pictorial encounters.  
**Content:** Beside Louise, show the Met’s Princesse de Broglie: her sister-in-law Pauline, painted in 1851–53 for her husband Albert. Compare blue fabric and jewelry, then the distance each figure creates. Louise leans against a fireplace and looks out at us; Pauline stands in a more formal presentation. The portraits appeared together in 1867 and were reunited at the Frick in 1986. Louise’s painting entered the Frick Collection in 1927, after Henry Clay Frick’s lifetime. Keep the women clearly identified and avoid deriving private temperament simply from painted poses.

### Segment 8 — The Ease Is the Achievement

**Purpose:** Return to the opening with a reusable looking method.  
**Key Message:** The apparent instant is the end product of many decisions.  
**Content:** Start once more at Louise’s fingers, then move to the ribbon in the mirror, the wrap on the chair, and the glasses on the mantel. The early sketch lacked the mirror; surviving drawings explored the head and arm. Fade those studies away until the finished canvas again seems to show a woman who has just turned toward us. Do not claim that she returned from a documented performance on that evening. Ask instead: which detail convinces you that this is a moment of life? Would the impression survive without the wrap or reflection? That question is a useful way to look at Ingres, and at any portrait whose naturalness has been carefully constructed.

## G. Fact-Checking Notes

- The sitter was still a viscountess in 1845; “Countess” is the later conventional title. The Frick-derived record gives oil on canvas, 131.8 × 92.1 cm, and a 1927 purchase.
- The return from the opera is a reading of pictorial clues, not a documented evening. Sources differ between “evening bag” and “fan pouch” for the blue accessory.
- 1842–45 spans planning and revision, including an interruption for pregnancy. Approximately eighty preparatory studies and fifteen survivors are Frick estimates; a 1985 account used a different count of sixteen known studies.
- The reversed early sketch lacks the finished mirror, but does not pinpoint the date of the mirror decision. The auction reproduction is not automatically free to reuse because the artwork is old.
- The Frick’s anatomical observation and Knight’s critique of the reflected hand are distinct claims; visual criticism is not independently validated optical proof.
- Louise’s biographies appeared in 1858 and 1872, after the portrait. Anonymous publication does not establish her personal wish for anonymity.
- Pauline de Broglie is Louise’s sister-in-law; her Met portrait dates 1851–53. The two pictures were reunited in 1986.
- The Frick’s historic home reopened in 2025, but display locations and loans change; avoid a fixed gallery promise.
