# Domenico Ghirlandaio: The Painter Who Put Florence into Sacred History｜YouTube Contents (EN)

## A. Video Core Narrative

Ghirlandaio placed Florentine bankers, children, and scholars inside stories of Mary, Christ, and the saints. His ordered architecture and recognizable faces allowed sacred time and a patron’s family memory to occupy the same space. Moving from chapel walls to intimate portraits, the film asks whom a city chooses to remember and how a large workshop made those images possible.

## D. Detailed Segment Plan

### Segment 1 — Who belongs inside a Bible story?

**Purpose:** Open on the surprising presence of Florentines in sacred scenes.  
**Key Message:** Ghirlandaio made contemporary people witnesses to ancient stories.  
**Content:** Begin close to the richly dressed visitors in the Birth of Mary. Pull back to show a biblical household that looks like a prosperous Florentine interior. In the Visitation, figures wearing contemporary clothes stand near Mary and Elizabeth. These details are a clue to Domenico Ghirlandaio’s remarkable appeal to patrons: he could make a family’s memory and a sacred story share one wall. He was not simply recording a city as if with a camera. Every face, building, and gesture had to serve the scene and the people paying for it. Ask the audience to keep one question in mind: why did a banker or a scholar want to be there?  
**Artwork / Person / Place Mentioned:** Birth of Mary; Visitation, Tornabuoni Chapel; Portrait of Giovanna degli Albizzi Tornabuoni; Florence, Rome, and the named churches and museums.

### Segment 2 — From workshop to room-sized images

**Purpose:** Introduce his training and control of viewing spaces.  
**Key Message:** Details and architecture gave a workshop artist a scalable language.  
**Content:** Born in Florence around 1448–49, Domenico di Tommaso Bigordi came from a family whose work connected him to the city’s crafts. His nickname means garland maker, though the details of his father’s trade should not be flattened into a proven goldsmith shop. At Ognissanti, his dated 1480 Saint Jerome shows objects on a scholar’s desk. The same year, his Last Supper turned a refectory wall into a painted extension of the monks’ dining room; Judas sits on the near side of the table facing Christ. Then Rome’s Sistine Chapel demanded a larger scale. This was a career built through observation, perspective, and teams, not a single isolated masterpiece.  
**Artwork / Person / Place Mentioned:** Saint Jerome in His Study; Last Supper, Ognissanti; Calling of the First Apostles; Florence, Rome, and the named churches and museums.

### Segment 3 — A Florentine crowd in the Sistine Chapel

**Purpose:** Show how present-day witnesses enter an ancient event.  
**Key Message:** One landscape can link different moments and different societies.  
**Content:** In the Calling of the First Apostles, painted around 1481–82 on the Sistine Chapel’s north wall, Christ blesses the kneeling Peter and Andrew in the foreground. The calling of James and John appears farther back. Follow the picture from riverbank to background, then toward the contemporary-looking onlookers at its edges. Their clothing inserts a social present into the sacred past. This wall was painted with a group of Florentine and Umbrian artists before Michelangelo’s ceiling. An entrance-wall Resurrection once associated with Ghirlandaio was lost after a 1522 collapse; today’s replacement cannot be treated as his original.  
**Artwork / Person / Place Mentioned:** Calling of the First Apostles; Confirmation of the Franciscan Rule; Florence, Rome, and the named churches and museums.

### Segment 4 — The Sassetti Chapel: a banker and a saint

**Purpose:** Make patronage and visual borrowing concrete.  
**Key Message:** Franciscan poverty and a wealthy family’s memory occupy one chapel.  
**Content:** Francesco Sassetti, a senior Medici banker, chose the life of Saint Francis for his family chapel in Santa Trinita. In the Confirmation of the Rule, an event from Francis’s medieval life unfolds before a recognizable Florentine civic setting and patrons’ likenesses. These identifications vary in certainty, so label names cautiously. The 1485 Adoration of the Shepherds brings rough faces, classical fragments, and a Roman sarcophagus repurposed as the animals’ trough; the baby lies on the ground in front of it. Hugo van der Goes’s Portinari Triptych arrived in Florence in 1483 and offers a useful Northern comparison. Here devotion, social standing, and artistic exchange all share the frame.

### Segment 5 — A chapel turned into a Florentine city

**Purpose:** Give the Tornabuoni cycle sustained visual analysis.  
**Key Message:** Architecture and contemporary figures make sacred time locally present.  
**Content:** Giovanni Tornabuoni commissioned the main chapel at Santa Maria Novella in 1485; its principal fresco work was finished by 1490. Facing the altar, the left wall follows Mary, the right John the Baptist. In the Birth of Mary, a carefully ordered bedroom surrounds childbirth and visitors. In the Birth of John, a brisk servant carrying a tray belongs to a different scene: do not move her into Mary’s room. The Visitation places an intimate encounter inside classical architecture and an urban vista, with a figure commonly linked to Giovanna Tornabuoni. Slow pans can reveal how furnishings, doorways, and witnesses organize separate tempos of labor, family memory, and worship.

### Segment 6 — Many commissions, many hands

**Purpose:** Explain the scale and division of a Renaissance workshop.  
**Key Message:** Unified design does not mean one painter laid every brushstroke.  
**Content:** While the Tornabuoni project was under way, Ghirlandaio’s shop also produced the Adoration of the Magi for the Ospedale degli Innocenti. Commissioned in 1485 and bearing a 1488 inscription, the panel links vulnerable children within biblical history to the hospital’s care for abandoned infants. The children are not documented portraits of individual residents. Brothers and assistants worked within the painter’s designs; Bartolomeo di Giovanni’s involvement in the predella is documented. Fresco also required a fresh area of plaster for each day of painting. Conservation identified twelve such workday sections in the separate Saint Jerome fresco, not in the entire Tornabuoni Chapel. Keep attribution and technique specific to each object.

### Segment 7 — Two portraits, two kinds of closeness

**Purpose:** Contrast family commemoration and intimate attention.  
**Key Message:** His small panels could hold a person’s identity without a crowded scene.  
**Content:** Giovanna degli Albizzi Tornabuoni died in 1488, but the Thyssen portrait is dated 1489–90: the date in its inscription commemorates her death, not necessarily its execution. Her profile, dress, prayer book, and Latin text turn a likeness into a memorial; technical study shows revisions under its calm surface. In the Louvre’s Old Man and Young Boy, two faces exchange a gaze while the child touches the older man’s chest. Their family relationship is not established, despite the traditional “grandson” title. The older face’s nasal features invite careful looking, not a retrospective medical diagnosis. Compare this quiet pair with the chapel’s public witnesses.

### Segment 8 — A thirteen-year-old apprentice and the legacy of a city

**Purpose:** Close with the workshop’s legacy and a way to look.  
**Key Message:** The master’s achievement lies in people, place, and organized collaboration.  
**Content:** A 1488 apprenticeship agreement placed the thirteen-year-old Michelangelo with Domenico and his brother Davide. The surviving transcription specifies payment from the workshop to the pupil, but it does not identify a particular fresco passage he painted. Years later, Michelangelo worked on the Sistine ceiling above the wall containing his master’s Apostles. Ghirlandaio died in January 1494, before the French invasion and Medici expulsion later that year. End by returning to the Tornabuoni visitors, then to Giovanna and the old man: sacred crowds and private portraits are two ways of asking whose face a city chooses to remember.

## G. Fact-Checking Notes

- Birth is given as about 1448–49 and death in January 1494. Vasari’s goldsmith and precocious portrait anecdotes are later biography, not direct archival proof.
- Judas sits on the near side facing Christ in the Ognissanti Last Supper. Calling of the First Apostles is on the Sistine north wall; Ghirlandaio’s original Resurrection on the entrance wall was destroyed.
- The baby in the Sassetti Adoration lies on the ground before the sarcophagus, which serves as an animal trough. The Portinari Triptych arrived in Florence in 1483.
- Facing the altar, Mary’s cycle is on the Tornabuoni left wall and the Baptist’s on the right. The brisk tray-bearing servant belongs to Birth of Saint John, not Birth of Mary.
- The Innocenti Magi commission began in 1485 and the painting bears a 1488 inscription. The 1488 date in Giovanna’s portrait commemorates her death; the museum dates the painting 1489–90.
- The old man and boy are not proven relatives. The apprenticeship agreement has the workshop paying Michelangelo; it does not prove which fresco passages he painted.
